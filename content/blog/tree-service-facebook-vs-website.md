Your crew posts completed removals on Facebook, and neighbors tag your business when someone needs tree work. Is that enough, or would a website solve a real problem? Look at how easily a stranger can find your services, availability, and estimate process before deciding.

## Give Facebook credit for the work it does

Tree work often begins with a local recommendation. A neighbor remembers your crew, sees a post, and passes your name to someone else. A maintained Facebook page can give that referral a place to see recent activity and contact the business.

It also suits short updates after weather changes or a busy stretch of work. You can explain whether you are taking storm cleanup inquiries or booking planned pruning estimates. Be precise about capacity and remove outdated availability claims when circumstances change.

For a small crew with a full referral schedule, Facebook may meet the immediate need. If you answer messages reliably and customers already understand your narrow range of services, a larger website project may not be urgent. Keep the business details accurate and make the phone number easy to find.

The useful test is whether the page supports suitable work without creating avoidable confusion. Count the repeated questions you answer. Requests about stump grinding, wood removal, or travel distance may reveal information that belongs somewhere more permanent.

## Notice what a job photo cannot explain

A picture of a removed tree does not tell a homeowner whether stump grinding was included. It also does not explain whether the logs were hauled away, left for firewood, or handled under a separate agreement. New customers may assume the visible result describes every job you quote.

Dramatic removals can also give a distorted picture of the business. Someone seeking careful pruning may see only storm damage photos and conclude you mainly clear fallen trees. A property manager might need routine maintenance information that never appears in the recent feed.

Qualifications require context too. A photo of a crew beside a truck does not establish who holds a credential, what it covers, or whether insurance information is current. Put those details in clear, verifiable language rather than relying on a badge in an old post.

Look at your page as someone who has never heard of you. Can they distinguish planned work from urgent contact? Can they find your actual service area? If not, adding more photos may not address the underlying problem.

## Let a website explain the lasting details

A small website can separate removal, pruning, stump grinding, and any other service you genuinely provide. Each explanation can cover assessment, access, cleanup, and the next step. That structure helps a property owner compare the right details before requesting an estimate.

Use your homepage to identify the business, coverage, and contact routes. If you offer storm work, describe availability accurately and keep a direct phone option visible. Do not imply that submitting a routine form triggers immediate dispatch.

Create a few project examples with permission. Explain the original request and the agreed scope, including cleanup when relevant. Our [tree service website guide](/blog/website-tree-service/) shows how to organize those details without turning the site into an equipment catalog.

A domain also provides a consistent address for truck lettering, estimates, and printed cards. Keep it registered in your own name and retain account access. Your ability to change website providers should not depend on recovering a domain from someone who left the business.

## Choose based on capacity and customer needs

Facebook alone is a reasonable temporary choice when your business is small, referrals fill the schedule, and the information customers need stays simple. It becomes less comfortable when you offer several services, cover a wider area, or spend evenings answering the same scope questions.

A website still requires upkeep. Someone must correct service areas, update phone details, replace expired credential information, and remove unavailable services. A stale website can create the same confusion as a stale Facebook page.

If you hire help, ask how changes are requested, who handles them, and what happens if you leave. Avoid buying a large site simply because a seller promises a flood of tree removal leads.

Start with the smallest site that answers the recurring questions well. A clear service overview, real evidence, and a working contact route may solve the immediate problem. Add pages when you have a distinct service or useful information that needs its own space.

## Connect both without doubling your workload

Give each place a purpose. Use the website for current service details and use Facebook for selected job updates or timely notices. You can reuse approved photos while adjusting the explanation to suit the context.

For a planned removal, a short Facebook post can point to the page explaining estimates and cleanup choices. For stump grinding, link to the scope information so readers know whether hauling chips is part of the service. Use descriptive links rather than asking everyone to message for basic facts.

Keep urgent messages consistent across both places. If storm demand changes your response capacity, update the information customers will see before contacting you. Never leave a guaranteed arrival claim running just because it performed well as a post.

Check both channels during a normal working week. Track where inquiries arrive and assign responsibility for answering them. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can Facebook replace a website for a new tree crew?

It can serve as a starting point when the business is referral-led and the service range is simple. Keep contact details, coverage, and service boundaries clear. Add a website when scattered information starts creating missed inquiries or repeated explanations.

### Should storm photos be the main content everywhere?

Only if they reflect the work you want and can responsibly take. Show planned pruning and other services as well when they matter to the business. Avoid using frightening images to pressure people into a removal decision.

### Do I have to post every day if I use both?

No, choose an update routine you can maintain. Accurate service information and relevant examples matter more than publishing for its own sake. Keep time-sensitive notices current even when you are not adding new project posts.
