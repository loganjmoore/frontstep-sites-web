How should you set up a Google Business Profile for a landscaping or lawn care business? Make it easy to tell whether you mow lawns, build landscapes, or do both. Accurate route coverage and service descriptions help a weekly mowing customer find a different answer from someone planning a patio or planting project.

## Start with ownership and the right business category

Find out whether your business already has a profile before creating one. Use an account controlled by the owner and follow the verification steps Google offers. If an employee or former marketing helper manages the listing, resolve access before making a second version of the business.

Choose from the available categories based on your main operation. A recurring lawn maintenance company should not present itself as a landscape design firm merely because it occasionally installs shrubs. Add another category only when it describes a real part of the business.

Use your established business name, not a string of search phrases. Keep category choices focused on what the company is, rather than listing every task your crew performs. These principles are part of [Google's business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en).

Write a short description of your actual work mix. You might explain that you provide recurring lawn care and seasonal cleanups, or focus on planting design and installation. Avoid broad promises such as "all outdoor services" when tree removal or irrigation repairs are outside your scope.

## Describe a service area your routes can support

A profitable mowing route and a one-time landscape installation may have different travel limits. Decide what your crew can regularly serve before publishing a large coverage list. A distant weekly mowing request can create a very different scheduling problem from a larger project.

If customers do not visit your business address, use the appropriate service-area setup and hide the address from public display. Only represent a customer-facing location when it actually operates that way. [Google explains service-area and hybrid businesses here](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en).

Use the profile's supported service-area options accurately. Explain any important differences on your website, such as recurring maintenance in a smaller group of towns and project consultations across a wider area. Avoid implying you maintain a staffed office in every town you serve.

The [landscaping local SEO checklist](/blog/landscaping-local-seo-checklist/) helps keep that coverage consistent across your website and listings. Revisit it when your routes change or your crew stops taking work in a particular area.

## Separate maintenance services from landscape projects

Customers should be able to recognize the kind of work they need. "Landscaping" alone does not tell them whether you offer mowing, mulching, drainage work, patios, or seasonal cleanup. List genuine services in plain language and clarify what is included where necessary.

Useful service descriptions might cover:

- Recurring mowing, edging, and cleanup, with the actual visit scope.
- Seasonal leaf removal and whether hauling is part of the quote.
- Mulch installation and any bed preparation you include.
- Planting projects and how you arrange a site consultation.
- Hardscape or irrigation services, only when your team provides them.

Keep exclusions visible on the linked service pages. If lawn treatment, large tree work, or snow removal is not available, do not imply it is included in a maintenance package. If you offer regulated work, check the applicable state requirements before advertising credentials or capabilities.

Send profile visitors to a website that explains the next step. A customer looking for recurring mowing should be able to request a route check without completing a landscape design questionnaire. The [landscaping website guide](/blog/website-landscaping/) covers that distinction.

## Use photos that show scope and progress honestly

Choose photos of work you performed and have permission to publish. A maintained lawn, a finished planting bed, and a patio installation each communicate a different service. Give your photo selection the same balance as the jobs you want to attract.

Before-and-after images need context. Identify a newly planted bed as newly planted rather than suggesting it represents mature growth. If a follow-up image shows a different season, say so. Do not make a dormant lawn and a summer lawn look like a direct result of one visit.

Include recognizable team or equipment photos where useful, without exposing license plates, private documents, or customer details unnecessarily. Avoid using a large landscape project from elsewhere to fill a gap in your own portfolio.

Keep original project notes with the images so you can describe the job accurately later. If you need a simple shooting routine, use the [business phone photography guide](/blog/how-to-take-photos-for-your-business-website-with-a-phone/).

## Keep seasonal hours, posts, and answers current

Check your public hours when the season changes. Make them reflect your actual customer-facing operation rather than the longest day a crew might spend outside. Explain estimate scheduling and response expectations on your website so customers know when a request will be reviewed.

Google Business Profile posts can share timely updates and offers. Use them for current information, such as opening an estimate schedule for spring projects, and review old offers when they expire. See [Google's guidance on profile posts](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7342169?hl=en).

Prepare answers to recurring questions: Do you remove the leaves you collect? Is mowing available without a long-term agreement? Can you quote a yard without the owner present? Publish your real answers on the website, and answer public questions wherever that option is available on your profile.

Do not rely on a particular question feature always being available. Your website FAQ can remain the reference point. Keep its answers aligned with what your office tells customers.

## Make reviews and upkeep part of the weekly routine

Ask for an honest review after a clear service milestone, such as a completed cleanup or the walkthrough of a planting project. For recurring mowing, choose a point when the customer has experienced the service and understood the arrangement. Keep the request optional.

Reply with care. Thank customers without publishing gate codes, route schedules, or details about when a home is empty. Move complaints about missed areas, damaged plants, or billing into a direct conversation that can resolve the specifics.

The [landscaper review guide](/blog/landscaping-get-more-reviews/) provides scripts and timing examples. Assign one person to check profile details, new feedback, and broken links regularly, especially when seasonal work changes your schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should mowing and landscape design have separate profiles?

Do not create duplicate profiles simply to advertise different services from the same business. Describe the service mix accurately and consult Google's eligibility guidance if you operate genuinely separate businesses or locations.

### Can I list a town where I want more work?

List it when you actually serve it under your normal operating arrangements. Do not advertise routine mowing coverage where you would decline the request because of travel time.

### What if I have no impressive project photos yet?

Use honest images of your team, equipment, and completed work within your current scope. A cleanly maintained small yard is better evidence than a borrowed image of a project you did not perform.
