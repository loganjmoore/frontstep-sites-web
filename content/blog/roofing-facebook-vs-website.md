Can you run your roofing business with only a Facebook page? You can use one to show work and receive inquiries, especially when referrals already bring customers to you. But homeowners comparing repair options or planning a replacement may need organized information that is difficult to find by scrolling through recent posts.

## Understand what a Facebook page does well

A social page gives you a place to share timely business news. You can show a completed shingle replacement, introduce a crew member, or explain how to contact the office during a busy period. It is useful when customers already follow you or hear about you through local conversations.

Project posts can start a conversation. A homeowner who recognizes a roof material or a familiar neighborhood may ask whether you handle similar work. Clear captions make those posts more helpful than a photo accompanied only by "Another happy customer."

Keep the context factual. Say what the project involved and get permission before showing the property. Do not publish a street address, imply that every nearby roof has the same problem, or invent praise from the owner.

A Facebook page can be a reasonable starting point for a new roofer with a small body of work to show. That does not mean it answers every question a prospective customer has. Check whether important service details remain easy to find after several weeks of posting.

## Consider the homeowner arriving with a specific problem

Someone searching "roof leak repair near me" may not care about your latest full replacement project. They want to know whether you investigate leaks, work on their roof material, and cover their town. Those answers should not depend on finding the right old post.

A replacement customer has another set of questions. They may want to understand the inspection, material discussion, written scope, cleanup, and scheduling process. A dedicated page can explain those steps in order.

There are also limits to communicating through a feed. Posts accumulate, important explanations move farther down, and the way visitors see a platform can change. Check the public experience rather than assuming everyone sees what you see while signed in as the owner.

## Give stable information a stable page

Start with the details customers repeatedly need. A modest website can organize your services, service area, project examples, business information, and contact process. It does not need to be large to make the next step clear.

Separate repair and replacement information if you offer both. Say which roof materials you handle and whether the work is residential, commercial, or a specific mix. Explain any limits before someone sends a request.

A useful inspection or estimate page can answer:

- What kind of visit the homeowner can request.
- Whether that visit has a fee under your policy.
- What you assess and what information you provide afterward.
- How the homeowner receives and discusses a proposal.
- How to contact the office about availability.

Keep insurance and warranty claims precise. Describe your own process without promising what another party will approve or cover. The website should help someone understand the service, not encourage a decision through unsupported assurances.

## Use each channel for the next useful step

A social post and a service page can work together. A post about a completed metal roof project can point to a page explaining your metal roofing work. A short repair update can lead to your inspection request information.

Make the destination match the post. Sending every visitor to a generic homepage makes them find the relevant answer again. If you are discussing replacement estimates, link to the page that explains replacement estimates.

Keep your business name, phone, and service boundaries consistent across both places. If you temporarily change how the office handles calls after a storm, update the information customers rely on. Avoid leaving conflicting availability claims in old prominent content.

Review your [roofing Google Business Profile](/blog/roofing-google-business-profile/) as part of the same check. People may move between a listing, your social page, and your website before contacting you. Each should describe the same business.

## Plan who will answer and maintain each channel

Adding a website does not fix an unattended inbox. Decide who handles phone calls, website forms, and social messages. Give each channel a clear response expectation and a backup when the usual person is away.

Do not describe a form or message as a confirmed inspection booking unless that is actually how your system works. A homeowner should know when they are requesting contact and when an appointment has been agreed.

Check the practical workflow yourself. Send a website inquiry, inspect the confirmation, and confirm delivery. Look at your social page as a visitor and find the current phone number. Ask whether an urgent leak inquiry would reach someone who can respond appropriately.

Maintenance can stay simple. Add selected project photos, check the service descriptions when your scope changes, and remove expired messages. You do not need to publish constantly on both channels to keep basic information accurate.

## Choose according to the work you want to win

If your work comes almost entirely through established relationships, a well-maintained social page may be a useful starting point. You can share recent jobs and keep existing contacts informed while you prepare fuller website content.

If you want unfamiliar homeowners to compare your services, a website becomes more useful. They need an organized way to understand your roof types, repairs, inspection process, and project evidence. That is especially helpful for larger replacement decisions where people may return to the information more than once.

Compare the upkeep, not just the launch effort. Think about who writes updates, who answers inquiries, and who controls your domain. The [roofing website cost guide](/blog/roofing-website-cost/) explains the different ways to budget for a site.

Neither channel guarantees inquiries. Judge them by whether people can find accurate answers and successfully contact you about suitable work. Fix missing information before treating a new platform as the solution to every marketing problem.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I stop posting on Facebook once I have a website?

No, keep using it if it helps you communicate with your audience. Share real projects and timely updates, then link to useful website pages. Give each channel a clear role you can maintain.

### Can the website reuse my project photos?

Yes, when you have permission and the images are appropriate to publish. Add captions that explain the work rather than copying a vague social update. Keep private customer details out of both places.

### Is one website page enough to begin?

It can be if your services are focused and the essential information is easy to find. Add separate repair or replacement pages when the questions need more room. A small clear site is a reasonable starting scope.

### Should customers message me about roof leaks?

Offer the contact route you can monitor and explain its limitations. If calls are the quickest way to discuss availability, say so. Do not imply that an unattended message provides immediate help.

## The short version

Facebook can show current activity, while a website can organize the details homeowners need before an inspection. Use both where they fit your business, and keep contact information consistent. Make every inquiry route dependable.
