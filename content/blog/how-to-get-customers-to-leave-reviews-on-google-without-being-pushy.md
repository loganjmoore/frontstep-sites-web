You want more Google reviews, but you do not want customers to feel cornered at the end of a job. Make it easy to leave an honest review and just as easy to decline. A brief request with a working link is enough to give someone the choice.

## Ask at a useful point in the customer journey

Choose a normal step after the customer has experienced the service. That might be the completion email for a fence installation, a receipt after a haircut, or the closing paperwork for an appliance repair.

Match the moment to the work. A homeowner cannot judge how an irrigation repair performs while your crew is still testing the system. An auto repair customer may want to drive the car before describing the result. Give people room to form their own view.

Use the same basic approach for customers at that stage, regardless of whether they seemed delighted or raised concerns. Do not make a positive answer to "Were you happy?" the ticket to receiving a public review link.

You can ask for service feedback as part of normal follow-up. Keep the option to leave a public review available without sorting customers into happy and unhappy groups first.

## Keep the request short and optional

Explain what you are asking for and provide one clear route. You do not need a story about how hard it is to run a small business, and the customer does not owe you a review.

Here are example messages you can adapt with your business name and real review link:

**In a completion email:** "Thanks for choosing our team. If you'd like to share your experience, you can leave an honest Google review using this link. It's completely optional."

**On a receipt:** "Want to share your experience? Leave an honest Google review using the link or QR code below."

**In conversation:** "If you ever want to leave a review, there's a link in your receipt. No pressure either way."

Then move on. Do not wait beside the customer while they type or ask to see the finished review. Avoid requests for five stars, particular phrases, a staff member's name, or praise for a specific service. The customer should choose what to say.

## Use a direct link and test it yourself

Google provides a way to create a review link or QR code for your Business Profile. Follow Google's current [review link and QR code instructions](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16816815?hl=en) from the account that manages the correct business.

Before sending the link, open it on a phone and confirm the business name and location. A company with several locations should take particular care not to send customers to the wrong branch.

Put the link where it fits the existing job paperwork:

- A completion email.
- A receipt or invoice note.
- A small card included with aftercare instructions.
- A clearly labeled link on your website.

If you print a QR code, scan the printed version before ordering more copies. Use enough contrast and space around it to make scanning easy. Include a written explanation so customers know what the code opens.

For a digital message, include a clickable link as well. A customer reading the message on their own phone should not need another device to scan it. If your business listing needs attention first, start with [getting your business on Google Maps](/blog/how-to-get-your-business-on-google-maps/).

## Avoid incentives, screening, and staff pressure

[Google's review policies](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) prohibit incentives, selectively requesting positive reviews, pressuring people to review on the premises, and requesting specific review content. They also prohibit asking staff to solicit a set number of reviews.

That means you should not offer a discount, gift, prize entry, or free service for posting. Do not send satisfied customers to Google while diverting dissatisfied customers into a private-only form. Avoid coaching customers on phrases to include or rewarding employees for producing reviews.

Teach the request as a courtesy, without turning the customer's response into a performance target. Staff should know that "no thanks" ends the conversation.

Check any outside review service before using it. Walk through the customer's actual screens and messages. If the process asks for a satisfaction score before deciding who sees the Google link, change the process. A supplier calling that feature "feedback management" does not change what the customer experiences.

## Use reminders sparingly and respect the contact channel

A sensible starting point is one invitation through the channel already used for that job. If the customer ignores it, that is not a problem you need to solve with repeated messages.

Do not move from email to repeated texts and phone calls just to get a rating. If you are considering automated texts or email campaigns, check the applicable rules and the service provider's requirements before turning them on. A phone number collected for an appointment is not a reason to assume every use is appropriate.

Avoid sending review requests from several people. The technician, office manager, and automated invoice service may each believe they are making the only request. Assign ownership and check the messages a customer actually receives.

A small internal note that an invitation was sent can prevent duplicates. Keep that record limited to what your process needs, and respect anyone who asks you to stop contacting them.

## Respond to feedback and keep the service improving

Make room to read the reviews you request. Look for repeated confusion about arrival windows, estimate details, cleanup, or what a service includes. Those are useful signals for your booking conversations and website wording.

Thank customers naturally when you have something useful to say. For complaints, use a calm response and a clear private contact route. The guide to [replying to a bad review](/blog/how-to-reply-to-a-bad-review/) includes examples for missed appointments, billing questions, and unresolved repairs.

Do not make a remedy depend on a customer changing their rating. Address the service issue on its own merits. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do customers need a Gmail address to leave a review?

They need to be signed into a Google Account, but that account can use a non-Gmail email address. Google explains this in its [review guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en).

### Should I ask only the customers who compliment us?

Use a consistent invitation process instead of choosing people based on how positive you expect their rating to be. Customers who raise concerns should have the same opportunity to review their experience.

### Can I write a suggested review for customers to copy?

Leave the wording to the customer. Give them a direct link and an optional invitation without supplying a script, keywords, or a requested rating.

### What if someone says they will review us but forgets?

Let it go unless they ask you to resend the link. A verbal intention is not a commitment you need to enforce.

## The short version

Ask once at a sensible point, provide a tested link, and make the choice optional. Welcome honest feedback without rewards, screening, or pressure. Let the customer decide the wording and rating.
