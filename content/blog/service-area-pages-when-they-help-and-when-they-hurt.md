You serve several towns, so should your website have a page for every one? Sometimes a separate page makes booking easier. Other times it creates a pile of nearly identical pages that give customers nothing beyond a changed place name.

## Start with the question a customer needs answered

A service area page should help someone decide whether you can do their job where they live. That means being clear about coverage, the work available, and any practical limits. A town name in a heading cannot answer those questions by itself.

Start with a main service area page that lists the places you regularly visit. Explain how people near the edge of your coverage can check their address. If some services travel farther than others, say so: a landscaper might install patios across the county but offer weekly mowing only along established routes.

Before adding more pages, make sure your main service descriptions are useful. Our guide to [writing a service page](/blog/how-to-write-a-service-page-that-ranks-and-converts/) explains what customers need before they request an estimate. Location details cannot rescue a page that never explains the work.

## Give a town its own page when the details change

A separate town page earns its place when it contains information that affects a customer's decision. Look at actual jobs, route plans, and questions from callers. You do not need to invent differences between neighboring places.

Useful differences might include:

- A cleaning business offering recurring appointments in one town but only larger move-out jobs farther away.
- A tree crew explaining access requirements for the narrow backyards where it has worked.
- A mobile detailer describing whether customers must provide water and power at that location.
- A real branch with its own customer entrance, opening hours, and team.

Add a short example of work you actually completed there, with customer permission where needed. Explain the original problem, the service performed, and what the customer needed to arrange. A real example is more helpful than a paragraph about the town's parks and history.

Keep conditional information conditional. If travel charges depend on the address or job size, explain how you confirm them before booking instead of promising that every visit is free.

## Recognize pages that create more trouble than value

Open two of your town pages side by side and remove the place names. If everything else is interchangeable, ask why a visitor needs both. Repeated slogans, a stock photo, and the same contact form do not create local knowledge.

Google's spam policies identify doorway abuse as pages made to target similar searches while sending visitors toward a more useful destination. Its examples include city pages that funnel people to one page and substantially similar pages without a clear browsing structure. That makes large sets of near-identical town pages a poor bet. [Google's doorway abuse guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#doorway-abuse) explains the distinction.

The customer problem matters even before search performance does. Someone searching for a nearby office may drive to an address that is only a mailing location. Someone needing an evening appointment may call because a copied page promises availability you cannot offer.

Describe where you work honestly. Saying you serve a town does not mean you have an office there. Keep that distinction clear in headings, addresses, and maps.

## Build a useful page from your own job records

Choose one town where you already do worthwhile work. Gather the facts before you write: services offered, coverage boundaries, scheduling arrangements, travel charges, and a suitable completed job. If you cannot find much that is different, improve the main coverage page instead.

A useful page can follow this order:

1. State which services you provide in the town.
2. Explain booking, access, or travel details that customers need.
3. Describe a relevant completed job without exposing private information.
4. Answer local questions you actually receive.
5. Offer a clear way to check availability or request an estimate.

Use a genuine project photo if you have one. Do not publish house numbers, license plates, or recognizable customers without considering permission. The guide to [taking business photos with a phone](/blog/how-to-take-photos-for-your-business-website-with-a-phone/) can help you collect useful pictures during ordinary work.

Avoid guessing about building rules, permit requirements, or neighborhood conditions. Link to the responsible local authority when customers need current rules, and check the details before publishing.

## Connect town pages to the rest of the website

Customers should be able to reach location pages through a clear service area list. From each town page, link to the relevant service details and your contact options. Do not make someone return to the homepage to find the next step.

Keep your actual business name, phone number, and published address consistent. A separate page is not a reason to invent a branch identity or a different business name. Our guide to [consistent business information](/blog/nap-consistency-why-your-name-address-and-phone-must-match-everywhere/) covers the basic checks.

Read each page on your phone. Can a person identify the service, confirm coverage, and contact you without hunting? Check that a quote form asks for enough location information to route the request, without making someone enter details you do not need yet.

## Review existing pages before adding more

Make a list of your current location pages and mark each one: keep, improve, or combine. Keep pages with useful facts and a clear purpose. Improve pages where you have real material to add. Combine pages that repeat one another without helping customers make different decisions.

Before removing a page, check whether it receives visits, inquiries, or links from other websites. Save useful content and ask your website provider to send the old address to the most relevant replacement when appropriate. Do not delete everything in a rush because one page looks thin.

Judge the remaining pages by useful inquiries and booked work, as well as visits. Keep a note of changes so you can compare similar periods later.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a page for every town I serve?

No. One clear coverage page can handle several towns when the services and arrangements are the same. Add separate pages when you can answer useful questions that differ by location.

### Can I make a town page without having an office there?

Yes, if you genuinely serve customers there and describe that accurately. Say you serve the town, and keep your real business location clear rather than implying a branch exists.

### How long should a service area page be?

Make it long enough to answer the customer's practical questions. Do not pad it with town history, repeated service lists, or generic paragraphs just to reach a target length.

### Will new town pages guarantee better rankings?

No. A useful page can give customers clearer information, but publishing it does not guarantee visibility or inquiries. Start with accurate coverage and measure whether the page helps people book suitable work.

## The short version

Create separate service area pages when location changes something customers need to know. Use real work, honest coverage, and practical booking details, then combine pages that add nothing beyond a town name.
