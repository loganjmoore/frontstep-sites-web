You need a website for your landscaping business, but the quotes do not seem to describe the same thing. What should it cost, and what do you actually need? Start by separating a clear lawn-care website from a custom system for managing design projects, payments, or customer accounts.

## Define the work the website must do

A mowing business needs to explain its routes, service frequency, basic scope, and how to request a quote. A landscape design and installation company needs room for project photographs, the consultation process, and distinctions between design, installation, and ongoing maintenance.

If you offer both, make those paths clear. Someone looking for weekly mowing should not have to complete a detailed design-project questionnaire. Someone considering a patio and planting project needs more context than a generic "lawn care near me" page.

A practical starting scope might include:

- A homepage explaining the business and coverage.
- Clear pages for your main services.
- Selected real projects or lawn-care examples.
- An introduction to the business and crew.
- A phone number and a short quote-request form.

The [landscaping website guide](/blog/website-landscaping/) can help you decide what belongs in that first version. Make the scope concrete before comparing prices, or you may be comparing a simple website with an entirely different product.

## Compare DIY with paying someone to handle the site

A DIY builder can fit an owner who wants direct control and has time to write, arrange pages, prepare images, and maintain the site. The subscription is only one part of the decision. Someone still has to explain your mowing area and organize the project gallery.

As of September 2026, Wix Light is $17 a month billed yearly, or $204 a year. Squarespace Basic is $19 a month. Those prices alone do not tell you whether a particular plan meets your requirements, so confirm the current offer and your full scope before choosing.

## Understand what a freelancer or agency quote covers

As a broad, typical range rather than a quote, freelancer and agency projects often run from a few thousand dollars to many thousands, depending on scope. Custom design, substantial writing, project photography, and specialized functionality can all change the work involved. Ask for an itemized proposal for your business.

A freelancer may fit if you want a particular design approach and can coordinate the content and decisions directly. An agency may fit a larger operation that needs a broader project team or work spanning multiple marketing channels. Neither label tells you what is included in a specific proposal.

Clarify whether the quoted build includes service-page writing, image preparation, mobile checks, and the contact form. Ask who supplies project descriptions and whether revisions are limited. Find out what support remains after launch.

Do not assume a higher quote automatically produces more landscaping customers. A carefully scoped site can still fail if the service area is vague or nobody answers inquiries. Compare the deliverables and the provider's understanding of your business, without accepting promises about rankings or lead counts.

## Identify the features that really add complexity

A gallery of completed planting projects is different from an interactive design tool. A quote-request form is different from software that measures a lawn and calculates a binding price. Be precise about what you mean when you request "online quotes."

Features that may require additional work include:

- Connecting inquiry forms to an existing customer management system.
- Offering customer logins or project document access.
- Taking payments or managing recurring service accounts.
- Building separate experiences for several real branches.
- Creating a detailed portfolio from unorganized project files.

Ask whether you need each feature on the public website at launch. Your existing business software may already handle part of the workflow. Avoid commissioning a custom replacement simply because someone included it in a proposal.

For lawn care, a simple inquiry can collect the location and type of service before you assess the property. For larger installations, a consultation request can establish fit before collecting extensive plans. A clear first step often serves customers better than a complicated tool built on incomplete information.

## Include upkeep and ownership in the comparison

Your website will change as routes fill, services shift, and new project photographs become available. Ask how updates are requested, who makes them, and how quickly they are normally handled. Find out what happens when you need a new page rather than a sentence changed.

Request a clear explanation of ongoing costs. Depending on the provider, the agreement may treat hosting, the domain, maintenance, content changes, or outside services differently. Do not assume an initial build price includes everything indefinitely.

Confirm who controls the domain and which parts of the website can move if you leave. Domain ownership and ownership of the website's design or content are separate questions. The [domain ownership guide](/blog/who-owns-your-domain-name-and-why-it-matters/) explains the basic distinctions.

Keep credentials in accounts the business can access. If an employee or contractor helps, make sure their departure does not leave you unable to reach your own website provider. Ask a lawyer about contract terms you do not understand rather than relying on a sales explanation alone.

## Compare proposals against one written brief

Send each provider the same description of your business, services, towns, and desired customer action. Include examples of your actual project photos and say whether you need help writing descriptions. Mention any existing domain or software that the work must accommodate.

Ask each provider to explain how a mowing customer and a design-project customer would use the site. That question reveals whether the proposal understands your different inquiry types. It also makes unnecessary complexity easier to spot.

Before accepting delivery, test the experience on your phone. Find your town, read a service page, tap the number, and submit a quote request. Confirm that the inquiry arrives and the acknowledgement sets realistic expectations.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a lawn-care business need an expensive custom website?

Not necessarily. Clear services, route coverage, real photos, and a working inquiry process may cover the immediate need. Custom functionality is worth considering when it solves a specific problem your simpler setup cannot handle.

### Should I pay extra for online estimates?

First, define whether you mean a request form or an automated price. Landscaping prices can depend on site conditions and scope, so a form may be the appropriate first step. Ask how any automated estimate handles those limits before paying for it.

### Is a one-time build free to keep after launch?

Do not assume so. Ask about hosting, domain renewals, maintenance, and changes in writing. Compare the ongoing arrangement as well as the initial payment.
