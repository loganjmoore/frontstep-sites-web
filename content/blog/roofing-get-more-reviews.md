You finish roofing jobs, but asking for reviews keeps slipping between cleanup, paperwork, and the next estimate. How do you make it a normal part of the job without putting homeowners on the spot? Choose a sensible closeout moment and give one person responsibility for a simple, neutral invitation.

## Define when the roofing job is actually complete

The last bundle of shingles leaving the driveway does not always mean the customer considers the job finished. They may be waiting for debris removal, a walkthrough, a punch-list item, or the documents you agreed to provide. Make those items visible in your closeout process.

For a replacement project, a suitable request point may be after the final walkthrough and delivery of agreed paperwork. For a repair, it may be after you explain the completed work and any follow-up plan. Use the real completion stage, not a particular emotional reaction from the customer.

Do not treat a weather delay as a reason to rush the invitation. If the work is still underway, concentrate on communicating the next step. Customers should not feel that a review request is arriving ahead of the service they purchased.

Keep this process consistent for straightforward jobs and difficult ones. The trigger is completion of the agreed work, not whether the homeowner has already said something complimentary.

## Ask briefly and leave the wording to the homeowner

Use a short invitation that makes it clear participation is optional. Here is sample wording for a replacement project: "Thanks for choosing us for your roofing project. If you would like to share your experience, you can leave an honest Google review using this link."

For a repair, you could write: "Thank you for hiring us for your roof repair. If you would like to leave feedback about your experience, here is our Google review link." Adapt the message to the actual service without writing a review on the customer's behalf.

Do not request a particular rating, mention keywords you want included, or tell customers to name the crew leader. [Google's review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) prohibits incentives, selective positive solicitation, and attempts to dictate review content.

Keep the request separate from the invoice amount, warranty terms, and any service issue. An honest review should never become a condition for receiving documents, resolving a problem, or getting the work you already promised.

## Put the link where the closeout already happens

You do not need a complicated campaign to start. Use the channel through which you already communicate appropriately with the customer, such as the project closeout email. A printed handover sheet can include a review invitation too, provided it does not crowd out essential care or warranty information.

Google supports review requests through a review link or QR code. Use the link for your own business and test it before sending it. [Google's review guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en) describes these options.

Check the experience on a phone. A homeowner should arrive at the correct roofing business, not a similarly named company across the state. If the link changes or stops working, fix the version in every template you use.

Let the homeowner complete the process privately when convenient. Do not stand in the driveway waiting for proof that they submitted a review. A simple invitation is enough; the quality of the work should carry the rest of the conversation.

## Avoid incentives, screening, and pressure

Keep discounts, gifts, and referral arrangements separate from reviews. Do not offer a gutter cleaning, service credit, or prize entry in exchange for a review, even if you say any rating is acceptable. Do not buy reviews or ask people without a real customer experience to supply them.

Avoid a two-step survey that sends happy customers to Google and unhappy customers only to a private form. You can collect private feedback, but it should not be used to decide who receives an opportunity to review publicly.

Choose a modest follow-up practice. If you send a reminder, keep it courteous and stop after that. Never frame it as a debt the homeowner owes the crew, and do not make repeated calls asking why the review has not appeared.

The broader guide to [asking for Google reviews without pressure](/blog/how-to-get-customers-to-leave-reviews-on-google-without-being-pushy/) can help you keep the process simple. Make it easy to participate and equally easy to decline.

## Handle criticism as a service issue first

A complaint about nails in the driveway, damaged landscaping, or an unclear change order deserves investigation. Check the project record and talk with the people involved before replying publicly. Your response should not become a debate about the homeowner's character.

For example, an illustrative reply to a cleanup concern is: "We are sorry to hear about your concern with the cleanup. Please contact our office so we can review the project and arrange the next step." Only promise a specific action if you can follow through.

Do not publish the customer's address, claim details, invoice balance, or private conversations. Discuss the particulars through the normal business channel. Keep any remedy independent of whether the person edits the review.

The guide to [replying to a bad review](/blog/how-to-reply-to-a-bad-review/) gives more help with tone. If a review appears to break platform policy, use the platform's reporting process rather than promising yourself or the crew that criticism will be removed.

## Give the routine an owner and learn from patterns

Decide who checks that closeout is complete and who sends the invitation. That may be the project manager, office coordinator, or owner. Having several people independently ask the same homeowner can feel disorganized, especially after a lengthy project.

Record whether the invitation was sent so you avoid duplicates. Do not set employee quotas for reviews or reward staff for obtaining particular ratings. The operational task is offering a respectful opportunity, not controlling what customers say.

Review feedback for repeated issues. If customers often mention uncertainty about start dates, improve pre-job communication. If paperwork repeatedly arrives late, fix the handoff. The review process is useful when it helps you see where the customer experience needs attention.

Link relevant service information from your website so future customers understand your process before hiring you. The [roofing website guide](/blog/website-roofing/) and [Google calls guide](/blog/roofing-more-calls-from-google/) connect those explanations to the rest of your online presence.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I ask after a small roof repair?

Yes, when the customer has had a real experience and the agreed work is complete. The invitation does not need to be reserved for replacement projects. Keep the wording neutral and participation optional.

### What if a customer says they do not use Google?

Thank them and move on. Do not create an account or submit a review for them. Private feedback can still help you improve your service, without being presented as a public Google review.

### Should I ask someone to change a negative review after a callback?

Focus on resolving the problem you are responsible for. Do not make a remedy depend on a changed rating or offer something in exchange for an edit. Let the customer decide whether their public account needs updating.

## The short version

Ask after a real roofing closeout, use a direct link, and leave the rating and wording to the customer. Treat complaints as opportunities to fix service problems, without tying remedies to reviews.
