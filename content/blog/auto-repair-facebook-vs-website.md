Can your repair shop get by with a Facebook page, or do you need a website too? Facebook can be enough to maintain contact with regulars when your bays are already full. If you want unfamiliar drivers to choose your shop, a small website gives them a more direct way to check whether you handle their problem.

## Facebook page and auto repair website compared

| Customer need | Facebook page | Shop website |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Check current shop activity | Strong fit | Possible, but not the main job |
| Confirm services and vehicle coverage | Details can be buried in posts | Permanent service pages are easier to scan |
| Find hours and arrival instructions | Available if the page stays current | Can be kept beside booking and contact details |
| Request an appointment | Messages work when someone monitors them | Call or request instructions can stay prominent |
| Best role | Updates and referrals | Lasting service information and the next step |

Use both when you can maintain both: Facebook for timely shop updates and the website for facts a new driver needs before calling.

## Start with what a driver needs right now

Picture someone searching for "brake repair near me" before work. They want to know whether you service their vehicle, when they can call, and whether they need an appointment. A feed full of holiday greetings and oil change reminders may not answer those questions quickly.

Another driver might need air conditioning diagnosis for an older pickup. Your recent post about replacing a clutch shows real work, but it does not explain whether you handle that truck or have diagnostic appointments available.

Write down the questions your service advisor answers repeatedly. Those answers belong somewhere permanent and easy to find. The [auto repair website guide](/blog/website-auto-repair/) can help you decide which details should come first.

## Where Facebook earns its place

Facebook is useful for showing the people behind the counter. A photo of your technician explaining a worn suspension part can make the shop feel familiar before a customer visits. Ask permission before sharing a customer's vehicle and keep plates, paperwork, and personal details out of the shot.

It also fits updates that have a short shelf life:

- A change to holiday hours.
- A newly available inspection appointment.
- A reminder that the shop entrance moved during road construction.
- An introduction to a new service advisor.

A customer can share your page when a neighbor asks for a mechanic. That recommendation is useful, especially when the customer can describe an actual repair experience.

Facebook alone may suit an established owner who wants only occasional referral work and has no trouble answering inquiries. It still needs accurate hours, a working phone number, and someone responsible for messages.

## Where a website makes the decision easier

Your website can organize information by the job instead of by the date you posted it. Give brake work, diagnostics, maintenance, and other core services clear descriptions. List the makes or vehicle types you actually service, including any limits on diesel, hybrid, electric, or heavy vehicle work.

Explain the difference between requesting an appointment and receiving a confirmed slot. If you need to inspect the vehicle before estimating a repair, say so. Do not present a warning light as proof that one specific part needs replacement.

Practical arrival information matters too. Explain where customers should park, whether you have an after-hours key drop, and how you arrange repair approval. Mention a shuttle or loaner only if you actually offer it and can explain the conditions.

A website will not make a shop rank first just because it exists. Its immediate job is simpler: give a prospective customer enough accurate information to make a useful call.

## Compare the work involved, not just the bill

A Facebook page still costs attention. Somebody must answer messages, correct old information, and keep time-sensitive posts from creating confusion. A website needs upkeep too, especially when your labor policies, service range, or hours change.

Choose a manageable setup. A clear homepage, service information, and contact details can be more useful than a large site with unfinished pages. You do not need a parts catalog, customer portal, or custom appointment system just to explain how your shop works. Keep pricing honest. If diagnostics carry a charge, explain how customers learn that charge and approve the work. Avoid promising an exact repair price before you know the vehicle and fault. Assign one person to maintain a short list of current facts. That list should cover the phone number, opening hours, booking method, accepted vehicles, and services you have stopped offering.

## Let the two channels support each other

Use Facebook to introduce a topic, then point to the relevant page for the details. A photo of brake work can lead to your explanation of inspections and estimates. A seasonal air conditioning reminder can lead to a page explaining how customers request diagnosis.

Give customers the same basic instructions everywhere. If online requests are reviewed during business hours, say that on the website and wherever you accept messages. Do not encourage urgent inquiries through a channel nobody watches.

On the website, make the call button easy to find. A Facebook link can be useful for recent shop activity, but it should not interrupt someone trying to arrange service. Keep the main path focused on the repair request.

## Test the choice with real shop questions

Ask someone unfamiliar with your business to use your current page on their phone. Have them find out whether you work on their vehicle, how to book a diagnostic visit, and where to leave the car. Watch where they hesitate.

Then review actual inquiries for a few weeks. Note how callers found you and which questions they still needed answered. You may discover that people can find your number but cannot tell whether you do transmission work or only routine maintenance.

Build or revise the website around those gaps. Judge the result by clearer inquiries and suitable appointments, not by whether a post gets reactions. Keep useful Facebook activity if it brings referrals you want.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I keep Facebook as my main contact point?

Yes, if you monitor it and your customers are comfortable using it. Publish a phone option for people who prefer to call, and explain when messages receive replies. Do not let an unread message stand in for an appointment confirmation.

### Do I need online scheduling on the website?

No, a working call button and a simple appointment request can be enough. Repairs often need a conversation about symptoms, vehicle details, and available diagnostic time. Make clear when your staff has confirmed the booking.

### Should I copy all my Facebook posts onto the site?

No, move lasting answers onto service pages and leave brief shop updates in the feed. A website visitor should not need to read months of posts to learn whether you service their car. Reuse helpful photos with permission and an explanation of the work.
