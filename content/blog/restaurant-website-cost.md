You need a restaurant website, but the quotes may describe very different jobs. One might cover a readable menu and directions; another might include custom ordering, reservations, and photography. Decide what a guest needs to do before comparing the price tags.

## Define the guest actions you need to support

For a neighborhood restaurant, the first questions are usually concrete: What is on the menu? Are you open? Where do we park? Can we order pickup or reserve a table? Put those actions in your website brief.

List your current systems too. If you already have a reliable way to take reservations or online orders, a clear link may be enough. Building a new system changes the project far more than adding a page about your dining room.

Think about distinct customer situations. A person buying lunch needs a different path from someone arranging catering for an office. A large party may need to send an inquiry rather than book a normal table. Your [restaurant website guide](/blog/website-restaurant/) can help you separate those needs without making the site complicated.

Write down the pages, links, and forms you actually require. That gives every provider the same scope to price.

## Understand the basic buying options

As of September 2026, Wix Light costs $17/month billed yearly, or $204/year, and Squarespace Basic costs $19/month. Treat these as starting website prices when comparing DIY options. Confirm the costs and capabilities of your intended setup rather than assuming every restaurant function is included.

DIY can work if someone on your team will build and maintain the pages. That person must enter the menu, prepare photos, check phone layouts, and keep holiday hours current. A builder subscription does not remove those tasks from the restaurant's workload.

[Front Step Sites](/) costs $99 a year with the domain included and no setup fee. The $99 plan includes two change requests a month, made within two business days. The Grow plan is $29/month or $290/year for unlimited changes the same business day and Google reviews shown on the site.

Typical freelancer or agency projects can range from a few thousand dollars to substantially more. Original photography, a larger content project, custom design, and connections between systems can expand the work. A freelancer may suit a defined website project, while an agency may suit a restaurant coordinating broader marketing work. Request clear scopes rather than assuming the labels tell you what is included.

## Menu upkeep deserves its own budget question

A menu is not a one-time decoration. Dishes, prices, serving times, and availability change. Ask who updates the website menu, how you send changes, and when they appear.

Readable page text is useful because guests should not need to enlarge a photograph of a printed menu. A downloadable menu can be an additional option, but the main menu should be easy to use on a phone. Confirm whether entering and formatting your menu is included in the initial work.

Decide how you will handle specials. If dishes change frequently, avoid a workflow that requires rebuilding several pages every time the kitchen adjusts the offer. Agree where current specials belong and who is responsible for removing old ones.

Make sure menu changes reach every relevant place. The website, printed menu, and any ordering destination should not tell guests different things without an explanation. See the [restaurant website mistakes](/blog/restaurant-website-mistakes/) for the common gaps to check.

## Separate website work from ordering and reservations

An "Order pickup" button can simply take guests to your existing ordering destination. That is different from creating a custom cart, taking payments, sending kitchen tickets, and managing collection times. Ask your provider exactly which job the quote covers.

The same distinction applies to reservations. Linking to a working reservation page is not the same as designing rules for table availability, deposits, party sizes, and cancellations. Use the process your front-of-house team can manage reliably.

Before paying for additional work, ask:

- Does the button send guests to the correct restaurant location?
- Can guests distinguish pickup from delivery?
- Is the destination usable on a phone?
- What happens when orders or reservations are unavailable?
- Who helps if a connection breaks?

Do not assume the website price includes charges from other services. Ask for those costs separately in writing without relying on a general promise that "everything is included."

Catering also needs a clear path. An inquiry form may only need an event date, headcount, contact details, and the type of service requested. Complex menu selection can wait if your team still discusses every event personally.

## Check launch work and the following year

Ask whether the proposal includes writing, menu entry, photo preparation, mobile checks, and testing links. Supplied materials can affect the work: a current typed menu is easier to use than several conflicting photographs of old menus.

Get written answers about domain ownership, recurring website costs, and the process for leaving. The [domain ownership guide](/blog/who-owns-your-domain-name-and-why-it-matters/) is useful when your address appears on signage, packaging, and printed menus. Your business should understand who controls it.

Before accepting the site, test three guest journeys from a phone. Find tonight's dinner menu and hours, start a pickup order, and send a catering inquiry. Confirm that the inquiry reaches the correct person and that every destination belongs to the right location.

Compare the launch year and an ordinary later year separately. A cheaper build can be a poor fit if routine menu changes become expensive or difficult. A higher quote needs a clear explanation of the extra work it buys.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does my restaurant need custom online ordering?

Not necessarily if you already have a system that works for your kitchen and guests. Start by checking whether a clear website link solves the problem. Custom work needs a defined benefit and someone responsible for ongoing support.

### Should food photography be included?

Ask explicitly, because preparing images you supply is different from arranging a photo session. Clear photos of your actual dishes can help guests understand the menu. Avoid paying for a large shoot before you know which images the site needs.

### Which plan suits a frequently changing menu?

Choose based on how often changes are needed and how quickly they must appear. Ask each provider to explain the update allowance and turnaround in writing. A menu that changes every day needs a process designed for that reality.
