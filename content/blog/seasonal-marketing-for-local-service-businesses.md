What should you change on your website when your busy season is approaching? Start with the service customers will need next, the work you want to book, and the capacity you actually have. A useful seasonal update answers a timely question instead of just swapping in a holiday picture.

## Build your calendar from actual jobs

Look back at your completed work and inquiries. Note when customers first asked about a service, when they wanted it done, and when your schedule became difficult to fill or manage. Those dates give you a better starting point than a generic calendar of promotional holidays.

A landscaping company in a cold climate may need to discuss spring cleanup while customers are still planning their outdoor work. A pool service in a warmer area will have a different calendar. Even neighboring businesses can need different schedules if one sells recurring maintenance and the other installs larger projects.

Choose a small number of seasonal priorities. For each one, write down:

- The service you want to book.
- The customer situation that makes it timely.
- When people should inquire.
- What might affect the schedule.
- Who will update and later remove the temporary message.

Separate predictable demand from events you cannot schedule, such as a storm. You can prepare clear contact information in advance without publishing alarmist claims or promising immediate help you cannot provide.

## Match the message to the service and climate

Seasonal advice should sound like it came from someone who understands the job. "Get ready for summer" gives a homeowner little reason to act. Explain what can be arranged and why the timing matters to the booking process.

For example, adapt ideas like these to your actual work:

- **HVAC:** Explain how to book a cooling maintenance visit before your schedule fills, and distinguish maintenance appointments from calls about equipment that has stopped cooling.
- **Lawn care:** State when you are accepting recurring mowing customers and which routes you cover. Do not advertise lawn treatments or timing that do not fit local conditions and your services.
- **Exterior painting:** Explain that scheduling depends on suitable conditions and preparation, and tell customers how you confirm a start date.
- **Cleaning:** Describe holiday hosting cleanups or move-out appointments, including what the package covers and what needs a separate request.

Keep safety and technical advice within your knowledge. A website update about storm-related tree work should explain your assessment process and availability, not invite homeowners to inspect hazardous branches themselves.

## Update the service page before promoting it

Send people to a page that answers the current questions. If your homepage announces fall cleanup but the linked page only describes weekly mowing, the customer still has to call to find out what you mean.

Add the scope, service area, booking process, and any real timing limits. For leaf cleanup, explain whether the work includes garden beds, collection, and haul-away. For holiday house cleaning, clarify whether ovens, refrigerators, or interior windows need separate booking.

Keep an ongoing service page at its usual address and update the seasonal details. You do not need a new page for every year if the service is essentially unchanged. Remove expired offers while keeping useful explanations available.

If you publish a deadline, make it real. Distinguish "request an estimate by this date" from "work completed by this date." The guide to [writing service pages](/blog/how-to-write-a-service-page-that-ranks-and-converts/) can help you make that distinction clear before you promote the page.

## Make your listings and hours match

Check the business profiles your customers use, beginning with the information that affects a visit or call. Review hours, service descriptions, website links, and current photos. A fresh seasonal photo is useful only if the basic details are right.

Google allows businesses to update their profile information, including services and hours. Its [profile editing guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617?hl=en) explains the available changes. Review the live listing after an update rather than assuming the customer sees what you entered.

For individual holidays or short exceptions, review Google's [special hours guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6303076?hl=en). If you close for an entire off-season, follow its [seasonal business guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) for that situation instead. Keep your website equally clear about when customers can reach you.

An emergency service claim needs particular care. If you only answer the phone during office hours, a seasonal promotion should not imply continuous coverage. Use the guide to [showing when an emergency service is open](/blog/emergency-service-businesses-how-to-show-you-re-open-now/) to align the wording with your operation.

## Fit the promotion to your capacity

Before encouraging more inquiries, check who will answer them and when the work can happen. A full calendar may call for a waiting list or a clear next available window. It does not call for an offer that makes your current commitments impossible.

You can promote useful planning without cutting prices. A painter might invite estimates for an upcoming exterior season. A lawn service might announce that it is reviewing new requests along an existing route. A cleaning company might explain how far ahead customers should request a special appointment, based on its actual schedule.

If you do offer a discount, state the service, conditions, and end date. Check that every place mentioning it is updated when it ends. Avoid a permanently resetting deadline or an unsupported claim that only one appointment remains.

Keep a process for missed calls and form requests. The article on [handling website leads](/blog/how-to-handle-leads-from-your-website-fast/) can help you connect the promotion to a response that your team can deliver.

## Review the season and prepare the next one

Keep a simple record of what you changed and when. Note the service promoted, inquiries received, suitable jobs booked, and the questions customers kept asking. You can learn from that record without claiming every new job came from one website edit.

Look for practical corrections. If customers ask whether leaf bags are collected, make haul-away terms clearer. If you get calls outside your route, move the coverage information higher on the page. If inquiries arrive after your booking window closes, prepare the next announcement earlier.

At the end of the season, remove temporary notices and check forms, hours, and photos. Leave useful service information in place with honest availability wording. Add the next review date to your calendar so an expired offer does not survive until the following year.

## Frequently asked questions

### How early should I start seasonal marketing?

Work backward from when you need customers to book and how long your quoting process takes. Use your past inquiries and current capacity to choose a practical lead time. There is no single schedule that fits both urgent repairs and planned projects.

### Do I need a seasonal discount?

No, useful timing and clear availability can be the message. Explain what customers can arrange now and what the service includes. Offer a discount only when it fits your costs and scheduling goals.

### What if the weather changes the plan?

Explain that weather can affect relevant outdoor appointments and describe how you communicate changes. Update the website when an advertised window is no longer realistic. Do not leave a guaranteed completion claim in place if you cannot honor it.

## The short version

Plan seasonal updates around real customer needs, local conditions, and the work you can accept. Keep your service page, listings, and hours consistent, then remove temporary messages when they expire. Use each season's questions to make the next update more useful.
