What should your handyman homepage say when you do a little of many things? Start with the repairs people recognize, then explain how you decide whether a job fits. Your copy should help a homeowner with a loose cabinet door or a weekend punch list contact you with useful details.

## Open with real repairs and a real area

The first heading should identify the service and location. An adaptable example is "Small home repairs and punch-list visits in Oak Park." Replace the place and service description with the area and work you actually cover.

Follow it with a short explanation: "Help with sticking doors, damaged trim, wall patches, and furniture assembly. Send your repair list to ask about an estimate." That is more informative than saying you can handle anything a home needs.

Choose one main next step. "Request a repair estimate" works when you need to review the job. "Call about your project" fits an operation where the first conversation happens by phone.

Avoid promising same-day work unless you have a real process for offering it. If availability changes, invite people to check the schedule rather than embedding a permanent promise in the headline.

## Group your services like a homeowner's to-do list

A homeowner does not necessarily know the trade term for a loose door casing. Name recognizable problems, then describe the work you provide. Group services so people can scan them without reading a long wall of small tasks.

Possible groupings include:

- Doors and trim: adjustments, hardware replacement, and minor trim repairs.
- Walls: small drywall patches and associated finish work you offer.
- Assembly and mounting: furniture, shelving, and similar installations within your scope.
- Punch lists: several suitable small repairs arranged in one visit.

Only include services you perform. Describe material or surface limitations where they affect the work. If mounting jobs require an assessment of the wall and load, say that before promising a fixed installation.

## Explain estimates, minimums, and materials

A useful pricing section tells customers how you reach an estimate. If you charge by an agreed job scope, explain that. If you have a minimum visit charge or use an hourly approach, state the actual policy and the circumstances that affect it.

Do not invent a starting price just to put a number on the page. A low number without a clear scope can cause more confusion than a plain explanation of how you quote. The [website pricing guide](/blog/should-you-put-prices-on-your-website/) offers ways to handle variable work.

Tell customers who supplies materials and how you approve them. For example: "We discuss parts and materials before confirming the work. Please mention anything you have already purchased." Use this only if it matches your process.

Explain that photos help you assess a request but may not reveal concealed conditions. Say how you handle additional work discovered during a visit, including the conversation needed before changing the agreed scope.

## Set boundaries without sounding unhelpful

Clear limits help the right customers contact you. If you focus on small repairs, say so. If you do not handle structural alterations, major remodeling, or specialist trade work, make that visible near the service list.

A practical example is: "We focus on small household repairs and assembly projects. Send your list and we'll confirm which tasks fit our service." That gives the customer a route forward without suggesting you can take every job.

State credentials, insurance, and experience only when you can support the claims. Do not imply that the word "handyman" automatically allows every type of work. Check with your state about requirements for the services you offer.

Describe your travel area honestly. If you consider jobs outside the normal area based on scope, explain how people should ask. Avoid a long list of towns that you cannot realistically fit into the working day.

## Show useful proof and explain the visit

Use real project photos with permission. A caption such as "Replaced damaged baseboard after the underlying issue was addressed" is more useful than a vague claim about a home transformation. Keep the caption limited to what you actually did.

Add a short description of how a visit works. Tell customers whether someone needs to be present, how you confirm arrival arrangements, and how they should prepare the work area. Do not request door codes through a public-facing inquiry field.

Landlords and property managers may need a different explanation. If you accept rental punch lists, say how you coordinate approval and access with the responsible person. Avoid promising entry arrangements on behalf of tenants or owners.

Use authentic customer feedback without inventing quotations or editing a comment into a different claim. Trust comes from a consistent picture of the work, not from filling the page with unsupported badges.

## Ask for a useful first message

Your contact section can reduce back-and-forth with a simple instruction: "Tell us your town, list the repairs, and attach clear photos if you have them. We'll review the request and explain the next step."

Ask for one wider photo showing the location of a problem and a closer photo showing the detail when that helps. Make photos optional if the customer cannot provide them. Avoid asking someone to climb, remove a cover, or disturb something just to get a better image.

Keep the form short enough to use from a phone. Test it yourself and check the inbox where requests arrive. Explain whether submission requests an estimate or actually confirms a visit.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I say no job is too small?

Only if your actual booking and pricing policies support it. A minimum visit charge or travel limit may make that phrase misleading. Explain the kinds of small jobs you take and invite customers to combine suitable tasks into one request.

### Can I publish example repair prices?

Yes, when they reflect your real pricing and clearly defined work. Explain what changes the quote and whether materials are included. Avoid making an unusual easy job look like the standard price for every situation.

### Do I need a long personal story?

No, a short introduction with relevant experience and a real photo can be enough. Focus on what customers need to know about letting you work in their home. Keep unrelated biography from crowding out services and contact instructions.
