Your website gets visits, but is it getting you customers? The useful answer sits in your call records, inquiry messages, and booked jobs. You can build a clearer picture without spending your evenings sorting through complicated reports.

## Decide what a useful result looks like

Start with the work you want the website to bring. For a repair business, that might be a call from someone in your service area with a problem you handle. For a remodeler, it might be an estimate request that matches your project size and schedule.

Separate these stages:

- **Visit:** someone reaches your website.
- **Inquiry:** someone calls, submits a form, or asks about booking.
- **Suitable inquiry:** the location and requested work fit your business.
- **Booked job:** you and the customer agree to the work.
- **Completed job:** the work is done and its result is recorded.

This prevents a misleading success story. A busy form inbox may contain spam, job applications, or requests from towns you do not serve. Those messages still need handling, but they do not prove that the website is bringing the right customers.

## Keep one simple inquiry record

Use a notebook, spreadsheet, or the customer system you already have. The format matters less than recording inquiries consistently. Give each new request a date and enough information to connect it to the eventual job.

Record the service requested, general location, how the customer found you, and what happened next. Useful outcome notes include booked, outside coverage, price declined, no response, or scheduling conflict. Avoid storing unnecessary personal information in a shared marketing sheet.

Ask, "How did you first hear about us?" Then, when helpful, ask whether they looked at your website before calling. Someone may have received a neighbor's recommendation and used the website to check your work. Record both parts rather than forcing that customer into a single source.

Leave an unknown category for people who cannot remember. A truthful incomplete record is more useful than assigning every uncertain inquiry to Google. Have everyone who answers the phone follow the same routine.

## Check that calls and forms really reach you

Before judging results, test the customer's route yourself. Open the website on your phone and tap the number. Check that it opens the right number, then place a test call and confirm that it reaches the expected person or voicemail.

A recorded phone-button tap only shows that someone pressed the link. It does not prove that the call connected, that you answered, or that the caller wanted a job. Use actual call records and your inquiry notes to fill in those gaps. The guide to [click-to-call phone numbers](/blog/click-to-call-making-your-phone-number-work-on-every-page/) covers practical checks.

Submit a test form with clearly labeled test details. Confirm that the success message appears, the inquiry reaches the right inbox, and your reply can reach the sender. If you measure submissions, ask your provider to count successful submissions rather than every press of the send button.

Remove your own tests from the customer totals. Repeat the check after changes to forms, email routing, or phone service. See [contact forms that get filled out](/blog/contact-forms-that-actually-get-filled-out/) for ways to reduce unnecessary friction.

## Use Search Console to understand search visits

Google Search Console helps explain how people reach your site through Google Search. Its performance report includes clicks, impressions, and the searches and pages associated with them. An impression records an appearance in search results; a click records a visit from those results. [Google's performance report guide](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553) explains those measures.

Look at the search phrases and landing pages together. Are people reaching your drain cleaning page when they need drain cleaning, or arriving at an unrelated article? Searches for your business name can reflect people who already know you, so review those separately from searches for a service.

These reports do not connect an individual searcher to an invoice. Pair the search picture with your own inquiry record. Our guide to [setting up Search Console](/blog/how-to-set-up-google-search-console-for-a-small-business/) walks through getting access if you do not have it yet.

## Follow inquiries through to booked work

Review open inquiries regularly and update their outcomes. A website can bring a suitable customer who later books elsewhere because your schedule is full. That is different from a page attracting the wrong kind of work.

Consider a hypothetical flooring installer. The site brings requests for small repairs, but the installer wants full-room replacement work. The next change might be clearer service descriptions and a stated project minimum, if the business has one. Buying more traffic would not fix that mismatch.

Now consider a cleaning company receiving suitable requests but replying after the customer has hired someone else. Its immediate problem is follow-up. The guide to [handling website leads quickly](/blog/how-to-handle-leads-from-your-website-fast/) focuses on that handoff.

Count a customer who calls and later submits a form once in your inquiry totals. Keep repeat customer work distinct from first-time customer work. When reviewing value, remember that job revenue and profit are different, and a paid invoice is stronger evidence than an unaccepted quote.

## Make a small monthly review and one useful change

Set a regular time to review search visits, suitable inquiries, booked work, and missed opportunities. Compare periods of similar length. Note holidays, weather, seasonal demand, time off, advertising changes, and any period when you stopped accepting work.

Look for a specific bottleneck. If visits arrive but nobody contacts you, check the offer and contact path. If inquiries arrive from the wrong area, make coverage clearer. If suitable inquiries fail to book, read the outcome notes before blaming the website.

Choose one change that addresses the pattern and write down when you made it. Give it enough time to collect meaningful inquiries before judging it. Small businesses can have quiet stretches, so do not rebuild a page because one week looks different from the last.

Keep the same definitions from month to month. If you change how inquiries are counted, note that in the record so an apparent improvement does not come from different counting.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many website visits should my business get?

There is no useful universal target. A specialist with a narrow service area may need fewer suitable inquiries than a business booking many small appointments. Start with the work you can take and whether visits lead to those inquiries.

### Can I track results without paying for analytics software?

You can start with your phone records, form messages, customer questions, and a simple inquiry log. Search Console adds information about Google search visits. More tools are worthwhile only when they answer a question your existing records cannot.

### What if a customer says they found me on Google?

Record that answer, then ask naturally whether they used your website before calling. They may have called from a business listing or visited the site first. Do not pretend the source is more precise than the customer's answer.

### Does my website count if a neighbor referred the customer?

It may have helped the customer decide to contact you. Record the referral as the first source and the website as part of their decision when they mention it. You do not have to give all the credit to one step.

## The short version

Measure the path from inquiry to booked work, and use traffic reports to explain what happens before contact. Keep honest source notes, test your forms and phone links, and improve the stage where suitable customers get stuck.
