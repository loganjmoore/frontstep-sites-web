What should you write on your roofing homepage besides "quality roofs at fair prices"? A homeowner needs to know whether you handle their roof type, whether you serve their town, and what happens after they call. Build the page around those questions, using examples below only when they match your business.

## Lead with the roof work and the location

The first heading should identify your main work and service area. For example, adapt "Roof repairs and replacements in Cedar Grove" with your actual town. If you only install asphalt shingles, say so instead of implying you work on every roof system.

Follow with a short explanation of who you serve. An example for a residential company is: "We inspect roof leaks, explain repair options, and prepare replacement estimates for local homeowners." This tells the reader more than a claim to be the area's most trusted roofer.

Avoid claims such as "permanent leak fixes" or "storm damage fully covered." A homepage cannot establish the cause of a leak or the terms of an insurance decision. Keep the opening focused on what your crew does and how a customer starts.

Put a useful action beside that explanation. "Call about a roof leak" and "Request a replacement estimate" can serve different visitors. If both actions reach the same office, explain that the team will confirm the next step.

## Give repair callers and replacement shoppers different answers

A roof repair section should acknowledge the customer's situation without pretending to diagnose it. You might write: "Noticed a ceiling stain after rain? Contact us to discuss an inspection and the next available visit." Use your real inspection process and availability.

Then name the repair work you actually perform. Depending on your crew, that may include flashing repairs, damaged shingle replacement, or specific low-slope roof repairs. Avoid a long list of materials and services you do not handle.

A replacement section can focus on planning. An adaptable line is: "We'll review the existing roof, discuss the proposed scope, and provide a written estimate." Add what your real proposal addresses, such as material choices, tear-off, ventilation work, and cleanup.

Don't describe every damaged roof as a replacement opportunity. Tell customers how you discuss repair and replacement options after assessment. That leaves room for an honest recommendation based on the property rather than a sales claim made before the visit.

Commercial roofing needs its own explanation if you provide it. Property managers may need information about tenant access, scheduling, and occupied buildings that a residential shingle paragraph won't answer.

## Explain the visit, estimate, and approval process

A short process section helps a homeowner picture the next step. Use stages that match how your office actually operates:

1. Contact the office with the property location and the concern.
2. Confirm that the job and location fit your service.
3. Arrange the appropriate visit and discuss any fee beforehand.
4. Review findings and the proposed scope in writing.
5. Agree on the work before scheduling the project.

Don't promise a free inspection unless that is genuinely your policy for the relevant visit. If some assessments have a charge, explain when customers will hear about it. Avoid "no obligation" language unless you can clearly stand behind it.

Explain how unknown conditions are handled in your normal proposal process. You don't need to put a contract on the homepage, but customers should know that additional findings will be discussed rather than silently added to an invoice.

Keep scheduling language realistic. Roof work can depend on weather and project conditions, so don't promise a completion date on a general homepage. Tell customers when your team will discuss their particular schedule.

## Use project evidence instead of oversized promises

Show real work with permission and give each project a useful caption. "Asphalt shingle replacement on a detached home" identifies the work. Add verified scope details if they help, without publishing private customer information.

A before-and-after pair needs context. Explain what changed and avoid implying that an attractive final photo proves every hidden condition was corrected. If you mention an inspection result or warranty, confirm the documentation and exact wording first.

A short project story can follow a simple sequence: the customer's request, your agreed work, and the documented result. Do not invent a quote about how delighted the homeowner was. If you have permission to use genuine feedback, preserve its meaning.

Credentials should be specific and current. Explain an actual qualification or relationship without using badges that imply a broader endorsement. For general advertising and licensing questions, check with your state or a qualified adviser.

You can find more ways to collect appropriate feedback in the [roofing review guide](/blog/roofing-get-more-reviews/). Evidence should support your service explanation rather than replace it with a wall of praise.

## Make coverage and contact details easy to act on

List real towns or neighborhoods you serve. If you travel farther for full replacements than for small repairs, explain that difference. A huge map shaded across the state can create expectations your crew cannot meet.

Write the form introduction plainly: "Tell us where the property is and whether you need a repair assessment or a replacement estimate." Ask for basic contact information and a short description. Let customers say they aren't sure about the roof material or age.

If photos are optional, say so. Customers shouldn't feel they must climb onto a roof to submit an enquiry. Your form can accept information they already have without asking them to collect difficult images.

Describe the confirmation accurately. "We've received your request; our office will contact you to discuss availability" is appropriate when no appointment has been booked. Add a response timeframe only if your team can support it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should the homepage mention insurance claims?

Only describe services you genuinely provide, such as documenting your inspection findings. Don't promise claim approval or tell customers what their policy will pay; direct coverage questions to their insurer.

### Do I need to put roof replacement prices on the homepage?

Not if an accurate quote requires an assessment. Explain what the estimate involves and when any visit fee is discussed instead of publishing a misleading universal price.

### Can I say we are the best roofer in town?

A concrete description of your work is more useful than an unsupported superlative. Show real projects, accurate credentials, and a clear process so visitors can judge for themselves.

### How much company history belongs near the top?

Use only the details that help someone decide whether to contact you. Keep a longer company story on a separate page so repairs, coverage, and estimates remain easy to find.

## The short version

Write the roofing homepage around service, location, evidence, and the next step. Give leak callers and replacement shoppers different useful answers. Keep promises limited to the process your business can deliver.
