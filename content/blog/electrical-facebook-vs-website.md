You already share lighting projects on Facebook, and local homeowners sometimes recommend you there. Is that enough for an electrical business? It can support referrals, but new customers may need a clearer explanation of your services and credentials before they contact you.

## Look at how your electrical work reaches you

Think about your last few suitable jobs. Some may have come from a neighbor's recommendation, a remodeler you work with, or a homeowner who found a project photo. Facebook can support those relationships by making your name and recent work easy to share.

For a small business working mainly through established referrals, maintaining a useful page may be a reasonable starting point. You can show a completed kitchen lighting project, explain a scheduling opening, or introduce a new member of your crew. You do not need to turn every business improvement into a large website project.

The limitation appears when a stranger needs details that are scattered through old posts. Someone planning a home charging installation may want to know whether you provide assessments, which towns you cover, and how to request an estimate. A friend tagging your page starts the conversation, but it does not necessarily answer those questions.

## Use Facebook for timely, shareable project updates

Electrical work offers useful visual stories when you explain them well. A finished porch lighting project can show how the space changed. A remodel update can describe your role in coordinating electrical work with the other trades.

Keep captions factual and specific. State what you installed or assessed, without claiming a project proves expertise in unrelated work. Get permission before posting a customer's property, and remove identifying or sensitive details.

Useful posts might include:

- A completed lighting project with a short explanation of the request.
- A note about which planned installations you are currently scheduling.
- An introduction to the person who answers estimate inquiries.
- A link explaining your EV charger assessment process.

Avoid publishing unsafe demonstrations or encouraging homeowners to open equipment for photos. Your page can explain what your business does without becoming a do-it-yourself electrical instruction channel.

## Give permanent service information a website home

A website lets you organize information around customer needs rather than posting dates. Your main pages can explain electrical repairs, lighting installations, panel assessments, remodel wiring, and other services your team actually offers.

Keep the differences meaningful. A troubleshooting page should describe arranging a service visit and how work is approved. A remodel page should discuss project scope, coordination, and what information you need to begin an estimate. Those visitors are making different decisions.

## Make credentials and estimates easy to understand

A prospective customer may want to verify who will work on their home before asking about availability. Put accurate business information and current credentials in a predictable place. Check with your state or local authority about any applicable display requirements.

Do not rely on an old social post announcing a credential to communicate its current status. Likewise, avoid copied badges or manufacturer logos that imply relationships you do not have. Show what is true for your business now.

Explain the first conversation. For an EV charger inquiry, you might ask for the intended location, general property information, and the charger details if the customer has them. State that installation scope and pricing depend on assessment rather than suggesting every garage has the same requirements.

For electrical repairs, explain any diagnostic or visit charge using your actual policy. These are stable details that belong on a service page and can be linked from Facebook whenever the question comes up. The guide to [writing a service page](/blog/how-to-write-a-service-page-that-ranks-and-converts/) can help you keep the explanation concise.

## Choose an inquiry process you can actually manage

Facebook messages can work for non-urgent questions if someone checks them consistently. They become a problem when your page implies immediate attention but you only open the inbox after a full day of site work.

Tell visitors the best route for different requests. A phone call may fit a service inquiry, while a form may suit a planned lighting project. Describe your real response routine without promising a turnaround you cannot reliably meet.

A useful first inquiry asks for:

- The customer's name and contact details.
- The town or general project location.
- A brief description of the electrical work.
- Requested timing, without treating it as a confirmed appointment.

Do not ask customers to post their home addresses or project documents in public comments. Move the conversation to the appropriate private business channel. Keep scheduling promises consistent between the website, social page, and whoever answers the phone.

## Maintain both without creating two separate workloads

Write enduring explanations on your website and use social posts to introduce or point to them. A Facebook post about a finished charger installation can link to your charging service page. A post about a kitchen remodel can link to the page explaining your remodel work.

You still need to check both places when something changes. Update coverage, hours, phone numbers, and service availability together. Remove or clarify old posts when they make an ongoing offer that no longer applies.

Test the customer path on a phone. Can someone open your social page while signed out, find the website, understand your electrical scope, and contact you? Then test the website directly, because some customers will arrive without seeing Facebook at all.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Facebook enough if all my work comes from builders?

It may be enough for your current referral process, especially if you are not seeking more work. A small website can still make your scope and contact details easier for a new builder or homeowner to check. Choose based on the information your customers actually need.

### Should I copy every Facebook post onto my website?

No. Use the website for durable service information and selected project examples. Temporary availability updates do not all need permanent pages, and outdated offers should not accumulate unchecked.

### Can a website replace answering messages promptly?

No. Good information can reduce repeated questions, but someone still has to handle inquiries. Pick contact channels you can maintain and tell customers what happens after they reach out.
