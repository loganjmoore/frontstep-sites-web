If neighbors tag your handyman business whenever someone needs a repair, Facebook may already be doing useful work. You might wonder whether a website adds anything beyond another bill and another place to update. Look at the questions and missed opportunities in your current process before deciding.

## Recognize what already works on Facebook

A recommendation from a local customer can start a useful conversation. Pictures of a repaired door, assembled bookcase, or finished wall patch give people a quick sense of your work. Comments also let you answer simple questions about whether a job fits your service.

Facebook can suit a handyman who wants a limited schedule and mainly works through repeat customers. If people already understand your job types, service area, and minimum visit arrangement, a direct message may be enough to begin. A website is not a requirement for every successful referral relationship.

Keep your page current if you rely on it. Show the actual business name, contact method, working area, and examples of the work you currently accept. Remove or clarify old posts that make an expired offer look available.

Be careful with customer privacy in community discussions. If someone posts a picture of damage inside their home, move detailed address and access arrangements to a private conversation. A public comment thread is a poor place to collect the information needed for a visit.

## Find the questions your page keeps making you answer

Review recent messages and look for repetition. Customers may repeatedly ask whether you take small jobs, supply materials, work in a certain town, or repair the finish after a patch. Those questions reveal information that is hard to find before contacting you.

Imagine a homeowner with several minor tasks: a loose towel bar, an interior door that rubs, and a flat-pack desk. They need to know whether you can combine them in one visit and what photographs help you estimate. A picture of your last project does not explain that process.

Another customer may ask for work outside your scope because your page says "all home repairs." You then spend time explaining limits after they have already expected a quote. Clear service descriptions can prevent that mismatch earlier.

A website gives these answers a stable place. You can still discuss each job, but the conversation starts with a better understanding of what you offer. That is especially useful when you cannot reply during a working appointment.

## Build only the website content your customers need

Start with a clear service list, coverage, contact details, and the estimate process. Include real project photos with captions about what was completed. You do not need a separate page for every hinge, bracket, and shelf you have ever installed.

Organize the work into sensible groups:

- Doors, handles, and small hardware adjustments.
- Small drywall repairs and the finish work you include.
- Shelves, curtain rods, and mounting jobs within your scope.
- Furniture assembly and other agreed small projects.
- Combined maintenance visits, if you offer them.

Explain important limits close to the relevant service. If you do not undertake electrical, plumbing, roof, or structural work, do not let broad language suggest otherwise. Check with your state and local authorities where work restrictions are involved.

## Compare the upkeep as well as the benefits

Facebook-only has a practical advantage when it is already your normal communication channel. You can maintain one place and keep working through familiar conversations. That may suit a small operation with steady referrals and no desire to expand its workload.

A website helps when you need information organized by service rather than by the order you posted updates. It can explain estimate terms, material arrangements, and project preparation in one place. Customers can return to those details without searching through past posts.

Neither option maintains itself. Old prices, an unanswered form, or an outdated service list can waste time. Decide who will update the website and where requests will arrive before adding it to your process.

Think about ownership too. Choose a domain you can retain if your website arrangements change. Our guide to [who owns your domain name](/blog/who-owns-your-domain-name-and-why-it-matters/) explains the practical questions to ask. A simple site with clear ownership and current information is a better fit than a complicated site you avoid touching.

## Connect recommendations to a usable estimate process

If you use both, let Facebook show recent work and let the website hold the standing details. A post about a repaired interior door can link to the doors and hardware page. That page can explain the kinds of issues you inspect and how customers request an estimate.

Ask for a wide photograph, a close view of the issue, the town, and a short description of the desired result. For assembly, request the product information. Do not ask customers to dismantle anything or put themselves in an awkward position to take a picture.

Keep the first reply practical. You might say you need one more photo, can estimate the visible scope, or need to visit before quoting. Clearly distinguish an inquiry, an estimate, and a confirmed appointment so nobody takes time off work for a visit you have not accepted.

Test the entire route on a phone, including the photo upload and the message you receive. The [contact form guide](/blog/contact-forms-that-actually-get-filled-out/) helps you keep that first step short enough for a customer standing beside a broken cabinet door.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I build a website if I am already fully booked?

It may still help explain your services and reduce repeated questions, but you do not need to rush into it for more inquiries alone. Start by deciding whether better job fit or less quoting admin would be useful.

### Can a Facebook album replace a project gallery?

It can show work, especially to people already browsing your page. A website gallery becomes useful when you want photos grouped beside the service details and estimate process they support.

### Do I need online booking for handyman work?

Not necessarily, because scope and time often need review before you accept a job. A clear estimate request can be more appropriate than offering appointment slots for repairs you have not assessed.
