If regular customers already follow your restaurant on Facebook, another website may seem like extra work. You can post a lunch special, show a busy dining room, and tell people about a closure. The question is whether a first-time diner can also find everything needed to plan a visit without asking staff.

## Keep Facebook for the updates it handles well

Facebook can be useful for the life of the restaurant: today's soup, a seasonal dish, a change to service, or photographs from an event. Regular customers may already look there for updates and share them with friends. That makes it a practical place to stay in touch.

It can also suit a small operation with a simple, stable offer and customers who already know the routine. If you serve a short menu from one location and take orders in person, you may be able to communicate the essentials clearly through your page.

Keep those essentials current. A visitor should be able to identify the restaurant, location, hours, and contact method. When you post a time-sensitive special, make the date clear so an older post is not mistaken for today's menu.

The limitation appears when standing information competes with all those updates. A diner trying to find the regular dinner menu should not need to interpret a series of specials, event photographs, and announcements before deciding whether to visit.

## Follow the first-time diner's questions

Imagine someone arranging dinner before a theater show. They want to know when the kitchen opens, what the menu costs, whether they can reserve, and how far the entrance is from where they will park. Those questions form one task, even if your information is spread across several posts.

A parent planning a meal may need a different set of details: seating arrangements, menu options, and whether the restaurant accepts walk-ins. Someone collecting dinner needs the ordering link and the pickup location. Neither should have to wait for a message reply to find basic instructions.

Read the questions your staff keep answering by phone. If people repeatedly ask for kitchen hours, menu prices, or whether bookings are required, the information is either missing or difficult to find. That is a practical reason to consider a website.

Do not assume everyone arrives through your latest social post. A friend might send the restaurant name in a text, or a visitor might search while already nearby. Give that person a clear place to check the facts.

## Give a small website a clear purpose

A restaurant website can start with a current menu, hours, location, contact details, and the appropriate order or reservation links. Add more only when it supports a real dining decision. A complicated site is not necessary to tell someone where to collect their food.

Keep the menu readable as page text rather than making a photograph the only version. Label lunch, dinner, brunch, or takeaway menus when availability differs. Prices and item descriptions should match the current offer.

Explain the service arrangements plainly:

- Whether you accept reservations, walk-ins, or both.
- How larger parties should contact the restaurant.
- When food service ends if the premises stay open later.
- Where pickup customers should go on arrival.
- How diners can ask about ingredients or preparation.

## Choose based on your actual service routine

Facebook-only can be a workable choice when customers understand your simple offer and staff are not spending much time clarifying it. It may also be a sensible temporary arrangement while a new restaurant settles its menu and service pattern. Keep checking whether unfamiliar visitors can find accurate information.

A website becomes more useful as the decisions multiply. Separate meal periods, catering, private dining, online pickup, and group reservations all need clearer organization. A stable page can explain each route without burying it under daily news.

Consider who will maintain it. Menu changes should trigger updates wherever customers read the menu. If nobody owns that task, an outdated website can create disputes over dishes, prices, or hours. Choose a setup that fits the time and support available to your team.

## Let each channel point to the same facts

Use the website as the home for stable information and Facebook for timely updates. A post about weekend brunch can link to the current brunch menu. A private-dining announcement can point to the page explaining group size, inquiry details, and how the restaurant confirms arrangements.

You do not need to repeat the full menu in every post. Keep the caption useful, make the destination clear, and send people to the page that matches the offer. A generic homepage link is less helpful when the customer specifically wants catering information.

When a service changes, update the main information first, then share the announcement. Check the order and reservation destinations as part of that routine. A working link to the wrong restaurant location is still the wrong customer experience.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use a menu photo instead of a website menu?

It can help as a quick social update, but it may be difficult to read on a small screen. A readable text menu gives customers a more convenient place to compare dishes and check current prices.

### Will a website make Facebook unnecessary?

No, the two can serve different purposes. Keep using Facebook for timely updates and conversations if it works for your customers, while the website holds the information people need repeatedly.

### Do I need my own ordering system?

Not necessarily, and a website can simply direct customers to the ordering route you already use. Check that the link reaches the correct location and that customers understand what happens after they place an order.
