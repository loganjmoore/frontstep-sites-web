What should a roofer check first when the website is not bringing useful local inquiries? Start with the details that help someone confirm you handle their roof and serve their property. Then work through listings, service pages, real project evidence, and the route to requesting an assessment.

## Put your business information in order

Create a reference list of your business name, phone number, website, public location details, hours, and normal service territory. Compare it with your website and the listings where customers already find you. Fix old contact details before adding more places to maintain.

If you have changed offices or inherited an older business, search for previous numbers and locations. Keep a short record of corrections you request. Recheck important listings rather than assuming a submitted edit has appeared publicly.

Make sure the business owner retains access to key accounts. A helper can maintain information without becoming the only person able to update it. Keep account recovery information somewhere secure and accessible to the owner.

Treat business-directory mentions, sometimes called citations, as records to keep accurate. Do not buy a long list of obscure entries merely to reach a target count. The [business information consistency guide](/blog/nap-consistency-why-your-name-address-and-phone-must-match-everywhere/) explains how to start with the records that matter.

## Match your Google profile to the roofing business you run

Check your Google Business Profile's phone, link, hours, and service information. Use your actual business identity. Choose available categories that describe your main work rather than every adjacent trade a roof project might involve.

Represent your location honestly. A service-area business that does not receive customers at its address should hide that address; a rented mailing address is not a substitute for a real operating location. Follow [Google's business representation rules](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en).

Describe the services you really provide, such as residential roof replacement, roof repairs, or particular commercial roof work. Do not advertise tile repairs if your crew only installs asphalt shingles. Remove seasonal availability claims when they stop matching your schedule.

Review what customers see after they click through to your site. If the profile emphasizes repairs but the landing page only offers replacements, fix the mismatch. The first page should confirm that they reached the right business.

## Give roof types and services useful pages

Plan pages around actual customer decisions. Roof repair, replacement, and commercial roofing may need separate explanations if you offer all three. Specific materials deserve their own pages when you have enough experience and information to make them useful.

A repair page should explain assessment, the kinds of work you take, and how you discuss findings. A replacement page should describe the proposal process and the scope customers should expect you to clarify. A low-slope roofing page should identify the systems within your capabilities without pretending they all use the same process.

Write a readable page title with the service and main location where appropriate. Use headings that answer customer questions, not strings of repeated search phrases. Link related pages when the reader needs to understand another option.

Google recommends clear, useful content and descriptive links in its [SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide). Apply that principle to the questions you hear during estimates. The [roofing website guide](/blog/website-roofing/) helps organize the pages around those questions.

## Make local evidence more useful than a town list

Publish permission-approved project summaries with a general location, roof type, and factual description of the work. Explain a real project detail such as access planning, flashing work, or a change discovered during tear-off. Leave out private addresses and unsupported performance claims.

Use that evidence when deciding whether a town deserves its own page. A useful local page might include relevant project examples, the services available there, and practical coverage details. If the only difference is a town name, keep the information on your main service-area page.

Do not claim local storm damage merely because a storm appeared in the news. Describe conditions you actually assessed and avoid implying every roof in a neighborhood needs replacement. Publish project observations with their context intact.

The [service-area page guide](/blog/service-area-pages-when-they-help-and-when-they-hurt/) explains how to avoid thin location pages. Your goal is to help a property owner decide whether to contact you, not to build a directory of town names.

## Check photos, speed, page access, and markup

Roofing galleries can become heavy. Resize large photos for the space where they appear and avoid making visitors wait through an opening video to find your number. Test the site on a phone using a normal connection, including the estimate form.

Ask your provider to check that important service pages are accessible to search engines. Review page indexing information if you have Search Console set up, and make sure the site map reflects the pages you want discovered. Keep important service links available through normal navigation.

Structured data gives search engines an additional description of your business. The name, phone, website, and other supported details should agree with what people can read on the site. Do not add invented ratings or a private address simply to complete a field.

Ask your provider to validate the markup and explain any warnings that matter. [Google's LocalBusiness documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) describes the supported format. Adding it does not promise a special search display or a ranking increase.

## Ask for reviews and track the right requests

Make an optional review request part of normal project closeout after the scope and remaining questions have been discussed. A repair customer deserves the same opportunity as a replacement customer. Let each person describe their own experience in their own words.

Reply professionally without publishing project disputes, insurance details, or private customer information. Take individual concerns into a direct conversation. A public reply should show that you are reachable and willing to address the issue.

Track inquiries by service, town, and outcome. Separate small repair requests from replacement estimates and calls outside your territory. If the wrong inquiries keep arriving, fix the scope wording before adding more content.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I create a page after every storm?

Only publish one when you have useful, accurate information to share. Avoid repeated pages that make broad damage claims or imply you have assessed properties you have not visited.

### Can project photos replace written service pages?

Photos help show the work, but they do not explain coverage, quoting, or scheduling on their own. Give them short factual descriptions and connect them to the relevant service information.

### How do I know local SEO is helping?

Look for appropriate inquiries and booked assessments alongside search performance information. A change in rankings alone does not tell you whether the work fits your business.
