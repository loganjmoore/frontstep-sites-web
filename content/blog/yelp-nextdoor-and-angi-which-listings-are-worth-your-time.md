Should you spend your evening updating Yelp, answering a neighborhood thread, or looking into Angi? The useful choice depends on what you sell, where you work, and whether you can handle the inquiries that follow. You can maintain an accurate listing without deciding that the platform deserves your advertising budget.

## Start with the job you want more of

Write down a service, a customer, and a realistic service area. A house cleaner looking for recurring visits in nearby subdivisions has a different goal from a remodeler looking for bathroom renovations across the county. A brake repair shop needs drivers who can bring a car to its location.

Ask recent customers where they first heard your name and where they checked you before calling. Those may be different places. Someone might hear about you from a neighbor, read reviews, and then use your website to confirm that you serve their town.

Search the platforms as a customer. Look for your actual services, read the questions people ask, and notice whether relevant businesses have current information. Treat that as a starting clue, not proof of future sales. Fix conflicting details with the [business listing consistency checklist](/blog/nap-consistency-why-your-name-address-and-phone-must-match-everywhere/) before adding more places to maintain.

## Use Yelp to help customers check your business

Yelp lets business owners claim a page without buying advertising, then manage details, photos, messages, and review replies. Those are general business presence tools, separate from a decision to promote the page. See [Yelp's explanation of claimed business pages](https://www.yelp-support.com/article/What-is-a-claimed-business?l=en_US).

Consider Yelp if customers in your area use it to compare businesses like yours. For an auto repair shop, show the entrance, explain whether you handle diagnostics and brake work, and give accurate opening hours. A mobile detailer should make the service area and appointment process clear so customers do not drive to a home address expecting a shop.

Read the page as someone who has never met you. Can they tell what you do and how to contact you? Answer genuine inquiries and address complaints calmly. Our guide to [replying to a bad review](/blog/how-to-reply-to-a-bad-review/) can help you keep the response useful without arguing about a customer in public.

## Consider Nextdoor when neighborhood fit matters

Nextdoor centers on nearby communities and recommendations. It offers business pages and paid advertising, so a presence there does not have to start with an ad purchase. Its [local business overview](https://business.nextdoor.com/en-us/local-1) explains the general options.

A lawn care operator trying to fill a mowing route in a particular neighborhood has a clear reason to investigate it. So does a house cleaner whose customers recommend them to nearby households. These are examples of a possible fit, not claims that either trade will automatically get work there.

Useful information beats a repeated sales pitch. Explain whether you offer weekly mowing, one-time overgrown yard cleanup, or both. If you clean houses, distinguish recurring cleaning from a move-out clean with empty cabinets and appliances. Only discuss services you actually provide.

Be helpful within the platform's rules and keep customer details private. A neighbor's recommendation is not permission to publish their address, photos of their house, or the circumstances of their job.

## Look at Angi for clearly defined home projects

Angi focuses on finding home service professionals, researching businesses, and requesting project quotes. It also offers paid promotion for eligible professionals. Its [frequently asked questions](https://www.angi.com/faq/) describe those broad functions; check the current written terms before buying anything.

That makes Angi worth investigating for businesses such as painters, roofers, and bathroom remodelers. The important question is whether the work available matches your operation. A painting crew that wants full interior repaints may not benefit from inquiries about touching up one door.

Before considering a paid arrangement, ask how project type and location are handled, what triggers a charge, and whether other businesses may receive the same inquiry. Get the answers for the particular offer in front of you. Do not assume that a directory profile and a paid lead arrangement work the same way.

An inquiry still needs qualifying. Confirm the job, town, timing, and whether your crew is suited to it before spending an afternoon on an estimate.

## Separate useful free upkeep from a paid test

Start with accurate contact details, real job photos you have permission to use, and a clear description. Keep those basics current wherever you maintain a page. They help customers understand the business even when you decide against advertising.

Before paying for promotion, write down these questions:

- What exactly am I buying, and when does billing happen?
- Can I limit the work types and areas involved?
- What happens with irrelevant inquiries or disputed charges?
- How do renewal, cancellation, and any spending limits work?
- Who will respond when a customer gets in touch?

Choose a spending limit you can afford to lose and a review date before starting. Avoid testing several new paid sources together if you cannot tell which one produced each job. Also fix missed calls and unanswered messages first. The [guide to handling website leads](/blog/how-to-handle-leads-from-your-website-fast/) covers the same follow-up habits that matter with directory inquiries.

## Keep the channels that produce suitable work

A simple notebook or spreadsheet is enough to start. Record the source, requested service, location, whether you reached the person, whether they booked, and what you spent. Note the staff time used to answer questions and prepare estimates.

Review the work itself. A gutter cleaner may prefer several nearby cleaning jobs to a larger inquiry outside the usual route. A remodeler may welcome a longer sales process if the project fits the crew and calendar. Do not judge both businesses by the number of messages received.

Keep your website ready for people who want more detail after seeing a listing. It should explain services, areas, and the next step. Use [customer tracking basics](/blog/how-to-tell-if-your-website-is-getting-you-customers/) to connect those visits with real inquiries without pretending that every customer's path is perfectly trackable.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to be active on all three platforms?

No. Start with the one that best matches your customers and your available time. Correct inaccurate information on existing listings even if you are not planning regular activity there.

### Does claiming a listing mean I have to buy advertising?

A claimed business page and paid promotion are separate decisions. Read each screen and agreement before accepting an offer, especially if it introduces billing information or a trial. Ask for written terms when the distinction is unclear.

### Which platform is best for a contractor?

There is no reliable answer that fits every trade and town. A remodeler quoting planned projects has different needs from a plumber handling urgent leaks. Look at local customer behavior and the suitability of inquiries before committing money.

### Should I remove a listing that sends no jobs?

First consider whether it helps people verify your business after a referral. An accurate page may still be useful without requiring much attention. You can reduce your effort or stop paid promotion while keeping basic information current.

## The short version

Choose listings around the work and neighborhoods you want to serve. Maintain accurate pages, treat paid promotion as a separate decision, and judge results by suitable jobs after costs and time. Keep a clear website available for customers who want to check the details.
