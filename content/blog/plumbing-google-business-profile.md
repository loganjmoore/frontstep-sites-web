## Plumbing Google Business Profile: the short version

A homeowner searching for an emergency plumber needs to know whether you can help with the water spreading across their kitchen floor. Someone comparing water heater installers has different questions about equipment and estimates. Your Google Business Profile should help both people understand what your plumbing company actually does.

## Get your business identity and coverage right

Search for your business name and phone number before creating a profile. You may already have a listing that needs to be claimed. Use an account your business controls and complete the verification process Google offers; keep access when a marketing helper moves on.

Use the name customers see on your van and invoices. Don't add strings such as "best drain cleaning emergency plumber" to the business name. Follow [Google's business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) for your name, location, and categories.

If you travel to homes and don't receive customers at your address, set up as a service-area business and hide that address. A storage unit full of pipe fittings isn't a customer-facing plumbing showroom. Don't create pretend offices in nearby towns to look closer to callers.

Choose coverage your dispatcher can support. A water heater replacement across the county might be practical tomorrow, while an urgent sewer backup there tonight isn't. Explain those differences on your website instead of implying identical availability everywhere. The [plumber website guide](/blog/website-plumbing/) covers how to make that information easy to find.

## Choose categories and services that match your jobs

Choose the available primary category that best describes your core plumbing business. Add other categories only when they describe substantial work you actually provide. Categories describe the business; the service list explains individual jobs.

Build that service list from recent invoices and the work you want more of. Depending on your crew, it could include:

- Drain clearing for sinks, tubs, and main sewer lines.
- Tank water heater repair and replacement.
- Tankless water heater installation and maintenance.
- Toilet repair, faucet replacement, and fixture installation.
- Leak diagnosis and accessible pipe repairs.
- Sump pump replacement and backup pump installation.

Remove anything you subcontract without a clear customer process or no longer offer. If you don't perform sewer excavation, don't leave customers assuming that a camera inspection includes a repair crew ready to dig.

Write descriptions in customer language. "We investigate recurring drain backups and discuss the next step after inspection" is more useful than "complete wastewater solutions." Explain whether you handle homes, commercial kitchens, rental properties, or a specific mix. A restaurant manager with a grease-related backup should know whether calling you makes sense.

## Make hours and emergency claims believable

Your profile hours should reflect real customer-facing availability. A phone that takes voicemail overnight doesn't by itself mean you provide round-the-clock emergency plumbing. Use your website to explain any difference between office hours, scheduled appointments, and on-call service.

Write a clear sentence about after-hours calls. For example, if true: "Evening emergency calls are subject to technician availability. Call to confirm dispatch." Don't promise an arrival time your team cannot consistently honor.

Before holidays, check your hours and the person responsible for answering. During freezing weather, review any message that implies immediate availability. Customers dealing with burst pipes need accurate expectations more than an optimistic banner.

Test the published number from another phone. Listen to the greeting and follow the same route a new customer would take. Check that the website link opens a working page with the same phone number and service area. This practical connection matters throughout the process of [getting plumbing calls from Google](/blog/plumbing-more-calls-from-google/).

## Show plumbing work customers can understand

Use photos of your real team, clearly marked vehicles, and completed work with customer permission. A tidy water heater installation, protected flooring beside a repair, or a clean fixture replacement can explain your standards without a long sales pitch.

Choose photos deliberately. A close-up of unfamiliar pipework may need a plain caption explaining what changed. Don't photograph invoices, addresses, valuables, or residents in the background. Avoid dramatic sewage photos that embarrass a customer or distract from the job.

Keep a small approved photo folder so the person updating the profile doesn't have to search technicians' phones. Label each image internally by service and permission status. Only describe work visible in the photo or confirmed in the job record.

Where your profile offers posts, use them for useful updates: seasonal scheduling, a service you genuinely provide, or an explanation of your water heater estimate process. Check them when availability changes. Posting on a schedule isn't a promise of better rankings, and a stale emergency offer can create unnecessary calls.

## Answer questions and keep a repeatable routine

If questions appear on your profile, answer what you can confirm. Typical plumbing questions include whether you work on tankless systems, offer sewer camera inspections, or accept appointments from tenants. Keep the same answers on your website so customers can find them even if profile features change.

Reply to reviews without discussing private repair details. Thank a customer for their feedback rather than repeating their address or the condition of their home. The [plumbing review guide](/blog/plumbing-get-more-reviews/) explains a consistent way to request honest feedback.

Give one person a short recurring checklist: verify the number and hours, look for incorrect changes, answer new feedback, and replace outdated information. After a change, view the public listing instead of assuming it saved correctly.

Keep a separate note of calls you couldn't serve. Repeated requests for septic work when you only handle indoor plumbing may reveal unclear wording. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I have a profile if I run the business from home?

Yes, a plumber who travels to customers can qualify as a service-area business. Hide your home address when customers aren't served there, and follow Google's verification instructions.

### Should I list every nearby town?

List the places you genuinely serve. Adding distant towns doesn't create a local office there or guarantee that you appear for searches in those towns.

### What if I don't have professional job photos?

Clear phone photos of real, completed work are a sensible starting point. Get permission, check the background, and choose images that help a homeowner recognize the service.

### How often should I update the profile?

Check it regularly and whenever hours, coverage, or services change. Accuracy matters more than finding something new to publish when nothing has changed.
