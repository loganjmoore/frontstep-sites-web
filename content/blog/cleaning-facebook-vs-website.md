Is a Facebook page enough for your cleaning business if referrals already keep you busy? It can be, especially when you want a small client list and can answer every inquiry yourself. A website becomes useful when you keep explaining the same service boundaries or want homeowners and office managers to request work more easily.

## Follow the customer's actual decision

A homeowner looking for weekly help wants to know whether you cover their neighborhood, what a normal visit includes, and whether the same cleaner usually returns. A renter preparing to move wants an entirely different answer: what gets cleaned in an empty property and whether you have a suitable appointment.

An office manager may need information to share with a supervisor before requesting a walkthrough. A social post can introduce your company, but a structured service page gives that person something specific to pass along.

Review your last few inquiries. If you repeatedly send long messages explaining oven cleaning, pet arrangements, and first-visit scope, those answers are candidates for a website. The [cleaning website guide](/blog/website-cleaning/) helps organize them.

## Use Facebook for familiarity and timely news

Facebook works well for introducing the people customers will welcome into their homes. A team photo, a useful preparation reminder, or a local customer recommendation can start a conversation. Keep the focus on information that is appropriate to share publicly.

Short-lived updates fit there too. You might announce that you are accepting new recurring clients along a particular route, explain holiday scheduling, or share a newly available appointment. Remove or clarify old availability posts when they no longer apply.

If you participate in neighborhood groups, follow each group's rules and answer the question being asked. Repeated sales posts can be less useful than a clear explanation of the service someone needs.

Facebook-only can be a sensible choice for an owner who wants to stay small, has enough suitable referrals, and reliably handles messages. Do not add a website simply to create a task you cannot maintain.

## Put lasting service details on a website

A website is a good home for the information that should not disappear beneath new posts. Give recurring housekeeping, deep cleaning, move-out work, and commercial cleaning their own explanations where those services differ.

Describe the scope rather than relying on package names. Include details such as whether inside cupboards, ovens, refrigerators, or interior windows are standard or optional. Say if a move-out clean requires an empty property and whether utilities need to be available.

For commercial clients, explain how you arrange a walkthrough, define the work, and coordinate an appropriate cleaning schedule. Office managers need to know whether you understand their setting, not just whether you can make a domestic kitchen shine.

## Compare the ongoing work honestly

Both options need maintenance. A Facebook page with an old phone number is a problem, and so is a website advertising a route you dropped months ago. The important question is who keeps the information accurate.

Make a short list of facts that change:

- Service area and route availability.
- Estimate and booking instructions.
- Cleaning tasks you include or exclude.
- Holiday schedules.
- Contact details and response expectations.

Keep a current master version of those facts so you can update both places together. You do not need to turn every website change into a social post.

A simple site may be enough. Start with services, coverage, trust details, and an estimate request. Add complicated booking features only if your pricing and schedule can support them. A quote based on property condition may still need a conversation.

## Protect privacy in photos and inquiries

Cleaning puts you inside personal and working spaces. Before publishing a photo, get permission and look for visible mail, family portraits, client files, security details, or reflections. Consent to clean a property is not the same as consent to use it in marketing.

Do not invite customers to put door codes or key locations in public comments. Collect access instructions privately when needed for a confirmed visit. Your website's first estimate form can ask for a general location without requesting every detail about entering the building.

Treat testimonials with the same care. Use authentic feedback and avoid identifying a household's circumstances or an office's security arrangements. A client should not have to trade privacy for a pleasant relationship with your company.

These choices matter on both channels. A website gives you room to explain your process, but the process itself still needs to be thoughtful.

## Make Facebook referrals easy to act on

When someone recommends you, give the prospective customer one clear next step. Link to the relevant service page or estimate form instead of asking them to search through the feed for an old checklist.

A post about move-out availability can point directly to your move-out scope. A post about new recurring routes can point to coverage and first-visit information. That lets the customer read the essentials before contacting you.

Keep the phone option visible for people who prefer a conversation. Explain when inquiries receive attention, and distinguish an estimate request from a confirmed appointment. Test the form yourself to make sure replies reach the right person.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will a website replace my Facebook referrals?

It does not need to. Keep the channel that brings useful recommendations and give people a clearer place to check the details. A referral can begin on Facebook and become an estimate request through your site.

### Can I just post my cleaning checklist on Facebook?

Yes, but customers may still need help finding the current version. Keep the checklist accurate and provide a direct route to it. A website service page is useful when you need a lasting link for repeated inquiries.

### Should I offer instant booking for every clean?

Only if your service scope and schedule make that practical. Deep cleans, move-out properties, and office contracts may need assessment first. A clearly labeled estimate request is better than promising a booking you later have to change.

Facebook can support a small cleaning business through referrals and local updates.
