Should you put an interactive Google Map on your website, or is a directions link enough? Either can work, provided it takes customers to the right place and leaves your phone number easy to find. Start with the job the map needs to do: get someone to your entrance or explain where you work.

## Decide whether a map helps your customer

A map is useful when someone needs to arrive at your premises. A salon inside a shopping center may need a clear entrance description. An auto repair shop may need to explain which driveway accepts vehicle drop-offs. A restaurant may want diners to see its location before making plans.

A mobile pressure washing business has a different problem. Customers need to know whether you cover their town, not how to drive to the owner's house. A written service-area list may answer that question better than an interactive map.

Choose between these options based on the visit you expect:

- A directions link for a quick route to a customer-facing location.
- An embedded map when seeing nearby roads helps explain the location.
- A clear service-area description when you travel to customers.

If your coverage needs more explanation, use [useful service-area pages](/blog/service-area-pages-when-they-help-and-when-they-hurt/) to describe boundaries and service differences. A colored map alone cannot explain that you offer routine mowing in one town but only larger landscaping jobs farther away.

## Find the exact listing before copying anything

Open Google Maps and search for your business name and town. Check the listing's name, street address, phone number, and location before sharing it. Do not copy the first result merely because it has a familiar name.

This matters after a move. Imagine a bakery that relocated from a shared kitchen to a storefront. A link to the old premises can look convincing while sending customers to a locked door. Check the destination itself, not just the words displayed on your website.

If you cannot find the right profile or someone else controls it, resolve that through the [Google Maps setup guide](/blog/how-to-get-your-business-on-google-maps/) before relying on the listing for directions.

Compare the contact page, footer, and business listing while you are there. The real business details should agree. Our guide to [name, address, and phone consistency](/blog/nap-consistency-why-your-name-address-and-phone-must-match-everywhere/) gives you a simple way to catch old information that has survived a move or phone change.

## Add a link or embed using Google's sharing tools

On a computer, open the correct location in Google Maps and use its sharing options. Copy the location link if you want a button or ordinary text link. For an interactive map, choose the embed option and copy the HTML provided. Google's [map-sharing instructions](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/7101463?hl=en) describe the current desktop steps.

For a link, use a label such as "Get directions to our shop" so customers know what will happen. Paste the shared location link into your website editor's link field. Avoid linking a whole map screenshot, where the destination may be unclear and the image may become outdated.

For an embed, paste the supplied material into the editor's supported embed or HTML block. Do not paste it into an ordinary paragraph. If someone maintains the site for you, send the copied embed and identify the page where you want it.

Preview the page before publishing. If the editor strips the embed or displays raw text, use the working location link while your provider checks the supported method. You do not need a custom mapping project just to help people find a single shop.

## Put useful directions beside the map

Place the map on the contact or location page, close to the information someone needs for a visit. Keep the business name, public address, hours, and phone number in readable text. A customer should be able to copy the address without manipulating the map.

Add a brief arrival note when the map does not tell the whole story. Useful details might include:

- The customer entrance is on the side facing the parking lot.
- Vehicle drop-off uses the marked lane beside the office.
- Appointments take place upstairs, with access instructions provided when booked.
- Deliveries use a different entrance from customer visits.

Only describe arrangements you have checked. If accessibility details matter, confirm the actual route and facilities rather than guessing from a satellite view.

Keep a separate directions link available even when you embed a map. That gives people another route if the embedded map does not load. Ask your provider to give the map a descriptive accessible title and check that keyboard users can move past it to the next part of the page.

## Keep the page quick and usable on a phone

An embedded map loads outside content into your page. It should earn that extra work by helping the visitor. Avoid placing the same large interactive map on every page when a contact-page link would do.

For a map farther down the page, ask your provider about lazy loading, which delays loading it until it approaches the visitor's screen. Google's web.dev guidance explains [lazy loading embedded frames](https://web.dev/articles/iframe-lazy-loading). Keep this as a practical performance choice rather than a promise about search rankings.

Test the contact page on your own phone with ordinary mobile data. Can you read the address before the map finishes? Does the map fit without sideways scrolling? Can you move down the page without accidentally dragging the map instead?

If the page feels heavy, compare it with a version that uses only a directions link. The guide to [small business website loading speed](/blog/how-fast-should-a-small-business-website-load/) explains other common causes, so you do not blame the map for oversized photos elsewhere on the page.

## Protect private locations and maintain the destination

Do not use a home-address pin as decoration for a business that does not receive customers there. Google's [service-area guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en) says businesses that do not serve customers at their address should remove it from the public profile. Check your website separately for information that could reveal the same private location.

If you show a general area map, label it as coverage, not an office or shop. Explain the boundaries in text and invite customers near the edge to ask before booking.

Review your map whenever you move, change entrances, or change your service area. Open the published link and follow the destination, since editing the visible address does not necessarily change the map underneath it. Add this check to your moving checklist alongside signs, invoices, and customer notices.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need an embedded map to appear on Google Maps?

No, embedding a map and managing your Business Profile are separate tasks. Add a map for customers who need directions, and manage your profile through Google's own process.

### Is a directions link good enough?

Yes, a clearly labeled link can be enough when the address and arrival instructions are easy to find. Test the published link on a phone to confirm that it opens the intended destination.

### Should I put the map in every page footer?

Only if it serves a real need on those pages. A written address and contact-page link can give customers what they need without loading an interactive map repeatedly.

### What if my map sends people to the wrong entrance?

Check whether the listing itself needs correction and use Google's available correction process. Meanwhile, put accurate arrival instructions beside the map and tell customers which entrance to use.

## The short version

Use the correct listing, choose a link or embed based on customer needs, and keep directions readable without the map. Check the destination on a phone and protect addresses that are not open to customers.
