Does your business really need the same name, address, and phone number everywhere online? Yes, the facts should agree, so a customer can recognize you and reach the right place. An old shop address or disconnected number deserves your attention before a difference between “Street” and “St.”

## Know what needs to match

NAP stands for name, address, and phone. A directory entry that mentions these details is often called a citation. Your own website, a chamber of commerce page, a supplier's contractor directory, and a map listing can all contain them.

Start with the name customers actually know. If your truck, invoices, and website say “Cedar Creek Plumbing,” use that established name when managing listings. Do not turn it into “Cedar Creek Plumbing Best Emergency Plumber” just to squeeze in search terms. Google's [business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) require the real-world business name and accurate location information.

Treat consistency as an accuracy job, not a punctuation contest. A missing suite that sends customers to another shop is a practical problem. A directory that displays parentheses around your phone's area code is not the same kind of problem.

## Make one reliable business information sheet

Put the approved details in a document your office can find. Include the public business name, main phone number, website address, customer-facing hours, and real service area. Add the street address only where publishing it is appropriate.

Keep separate notes about information used privately for account verification. That prevents a helpful employee from copying a home address into every public directory simply because it appears on a registration document.

For each genuine location, record:

- The name and address customers should see.
- The phone number that reaches that location or its responsible team.
- The page on your website describing that location.
- Whether customers can visit, and during which hours.
- Who can approve future changes.

An auto repair shop with a reception desk needs clear driving information. A mobile mechanic who works at customers' homes needs clear coverage and booking instructions. Both need truthful information, but they do not need identical listing setups.

## Audit the places customers can actually find

Search your current business name with your town. Then search an old business name, former address, and previous phone number if you have used them. Open the results instead of relying only on the short search description, which may lag behind a corrected page.

Use a simple list with the page address, wrong detail, account owner, action taken, and date checked. Prioritize these places:

1. Your website's contact page, footer, and service pages.
2. Google Business Profile and the other map services you use.
3. Established directories where you already have a listing.
4. Your social profiles, trade associations, and local chamber.
5. Supplier or manufacturer directories that refer customers to you.

Suppose you moved an appliance repair workshop but left your previous address on a parts supplier's referral page. A customer arriving there is a clearer loss than an obscure directory using an old abbreviation. Fix the page people rely on first.

## Correct the source and check the public result

Start with your website, where you control the information. Update the contact page and shared footer, then look for older addresses in page text, downloadable brochures, and appointment instructions. The guide to [what belongs in your website footer](/blog/what-to-put-in-your-website-footer/) explains which details should remain easy to find.

Next, sign in to each listing account and correct its record. If someone else manages it, ask them to make the specific correction and confirm when it appears publicly. Keep the request concrete: the old number, the correct number, and the exact page.

For an unclaimed or duplicate listing, use that platform's ownership or correction process. Do not create another entry just because the first one is difficult to access. For a move, describe the move accurately instead of treating an active business as permanently closed without checking the platform's instructions.

After edits are accepted, open the public result again. Tap the call button and check directions where customers visit. Saving a change in an account is only the first half of the job.

## Handle home addresses and multiple numbers carefully

Consistency does not require a home-based electrician to advertise a private front door. Google says businesses that travel to customers and do not serve them at their business address should remove that public address and use a service area. Its [service-area guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en) explains the distinction.

Follow each platform's current eligibility rules. Do not substitute a mailbox or pretend a borrowed address is a staffed shop. Your website can explain the towns you serve and how customers request work without offering directions to your home. See [when service-area pages help](/blog/service-area-pages-when-they-help-and-when-they-hurt/) for a useful way to describe that coverage.

If you use call tracking, keep a record of every number and where it appears. Ask whoever manages the setup how callers reach you, what happens if you cancel, and which number remains yours. Test each route. A tracking number that stops forwarding can break the customer journey even when the business name is correct.

## Make future changes a short checklist

A business move, new phone system, or ownership change is the right time to reopen your audit list. Give one person responsibility for coordinating updates so the website, listings, invoices, and email signatures do not tell different stories for months.

Keep access to important accounts under business control. Record which employee or provider manages each listing, without storing passwords in an ordinary shared document. When a provider leaves, confirm you can still sign in and make corrections.

Accurate records can also reduce the contradictions people encounter in automated answers. That connection is covered in [why consistent business information matters for AI answers](/blog/why-consistent-business-information-matters-for-ai-answers/). Neither clean listings nor matching contact details guarantee a particular search position.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does “Road” versus “Rd.” need to be fixed?

Use a consistent format when you can, but first fix differences that change the meaning. A wrong street number, missing unit, or former location is more urgent than a common abbreviation.

### Should I put my home address in every directory?

No. If customers do not visit your home, check each platform's service-business rules and public address options before listing it. Keep private verification information separate from the details customers should see.

### Will fixing NAP consistency put me first on Google?

No one can promise that result. Correct information helps customers reach you and removes avoidable confusion, but search visibility depends on more than your contact details.

### What if a directory will not correct my old number?

Save the correction request and any support response, then check whether the page names an upstream data source you can contact. Continue fixing the listings you control rather than letting one stubborn entry stop the whole cleanup.

## The short version

Choose accurate business details, keep them in one reference document, and check the websites and listings customers use. Fix wrong locations and broken phone numbers first, then verify the public results. Protect private addresses and repeat the checklist whenever your business changes.
