A homeowner is looking for someone to repaint a lived-in kitchen, not just a company with attractive wall photos. What should your painting website show before they feel comfortable calling? Put the work you do, the places you travel, and your estimate process ahead of slogans about quality.

## Start with the work you want to quote

Your first screen should identify you as a painting contractor and name your main service area. Say whether you paint interiors, exteriors, cabinets, or commercial spaces. A visitor searching for "cabinet painter near me" should not have to guess whether your house painting service includes cabinets.

Use a short opening such as "Interior and exterior house painting in your town and nearby communities." Add a visible phone number and a button labeled "Request a painting estimate." Replace the example location with your real coverage and avoid listing distant towns just because you would travel there for an unusually large job.

If you only take particular projects, make that clear early. You may focus on occupied homes rather than new construction, or handle rental turnovers but not decorative finishes. Helpful limits save you quoting time and spare homeowners a call that goes nowhere.

A phone number should work when tapped on a phone. The estimate button should open a short, usable form. Those basics matter more to a person with peeling porch trim than a video that takes over the page.

## Separate services that need different explanations

An interior repaint, an exterior repaint, and cabinet refinishing involve different concerns. Give each major service room to explain its scope. You do not need a separate page for every small task, but one paragraph labeled "all painting services" leaves too much unanswered.

For interior work, describe how you agree on walls, ceilings, trim, doors, and repairs. Explain who moves furniture and removes wall hangings. For exterior work, discuss inspection, preparation, access, and how weather can change scheduling. Avoid publishing a fixed weather rule that ignores the coating manufacturer's instructions.

Cabinet customers want to know whether you remove doors and drawers, where finishing happens, and how kitchen access is affected. Describe your actual process without turning the page into a technical manual. Explain what you inspect before deciding whether an existing finish is suitable for the proposed work.

A useful service page answers the questions raised during estimates. If you repeatedly explain why damaged trim repair is separate, put that distinction online. ## Show preparation as well as the finished room

A freshly painted wall does not reveal what happened before the last coat. Include project photographs that show protected floors, carefully prepared trim, and the finished result. Get permission before publishing a customer's home, and keep identifying details out of view.

Give each project a short, factual caption. Describe the surface, the problem, and the agreed work. "Living room repaint with patched nail holes and refreshed baseboards" tells a homeowner more than "another happy customer."

Keep before-and-after photographs comparable. Similar angles and lighting make the work easier to judge. Do not edit away defects, exaggerate colors, or use someone else's project as evidence of your own workmanship.

If you lack a large portfolio, use a few well-explained jobs. A small collection showing the kind of painting you want to book is more useful than a huge gallery of unrelated work. Ask your crew to capture project stages while respecting the customer's space and the job's safety procedures.

## Explain what an estimate includes

Homeowners may compare two estimates without realizing they cover different preparation, surfaces, or cleanup. Your website can explain the categories you discuss before work begins. Present these as your process, not as a claim that every competitor leaves something out.

Useful estimate topics include:

- Which rooms, elevations, doors, and trim are included.
- What preparation and minor repairs are included or priced separately.
- How paint products, colors, and sheen are selected.
- Who handles furniture, fixtures, and access arrangements.
- What cleanup and final walkthrough involve.

Do not promise that a photo alone always produces a final price. Pictures may help you understand the job, while an inspection reveals access problems or surface conditions. Tell people whether the first step is a call, photo review, or site visit.

If you publish starting prices, explain the assumptions. An honest description of how you quote is useful even when you cannot give a reliable price online.

## Make the request easy to handle

Ask for the person's name, a working contact method, the town, and a brief project description. A choice between interior, exterior, and cabinets can help route the request. Keep room dimensions, exact colors, and detailed product preferences for the follow-up unless you truly need them immediately.

Allow an optional photo if your form supports it reliably. Someone standing in a room may have a useful picture, but another homeowner may be contacting you from work. Requiring uploads can block an otherwise good inquiry.

Explain what happens after submission. Say that you will contact them to discuss the project and arrange the next step, using a response expectation you can actually meet. Do not imply that sending the form reserves a place on your schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to list every paint brand I use?

No, but mention products when that information helps explain your service and reflects your actual practice. Avoid implying a manufacturer endorsement you do not have. You can discuss the final product choice during the estimate.

### Should I show my next available start date?

Only if someone will keep it current. Explain that the schedule is confirmed after you agree on scope, especially for exterior work affected by weather. An old promise of immediate availability creates unnecessary frustration.

### What if I do both residential and commercial painting?

Give visitors clearly labeled routes to each service. A homeowner needs information about living through a repaint, while a business owner may ask about access, working hours, and keeping areas usable. Use examples that fit each situation.
