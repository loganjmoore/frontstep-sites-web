Someone has recommended your salon, but the person looking you up has never sat in your chair. What should your homepage say to help them book the right haircut, color appointment, or beard service? Start with the questions your receptionist answers every day, including the ones clients feel awkward asking.

## Put the service and location in the first line

A headline such as "Feel like your best self" could belong to almost any business. Give the visitor something useful instead. Example wording to adapt: "Haircuts, lived-in color, and gray blending in your town." Replace the location with your actual town, and name only services you offer.

A barbershop needs a different opening. Try "Skin fades, scissor cuts, and beard trims in your neighborhood." Follow that with a practical sentence about appointments or walk-ins. If walk-ins depend on availability, say so rather than making someone drive over expecting an open chair.

Write the next line for the client you actually serve. You might welcome first-time color clients, specialize in textured hair, or offer a quiet appointment on request. Be specific about the arrangement and avoid promising an experience your whole team cannot consistently provide. ## Help people choose an appointment

Your service names make sense to your team, but they may mean little to a new client. "Signature refresh" does not tell someone whether they are booking a haircut, toner, or blowout. Put a plain description beside each main service and explain anything that must be booked separately.

For example, adapt these descriptions to your actual menu:

- Haircut and finish: explain whether the appointment includes a wash and styling.
- Root color: describe the regrowth service and whether a finish is included.
- Highlights: explain how a partial appointment differs from a full appointment.
- Color correction: direct new clients to a consultation before selecting a service.
- Beard trim: say whether shaping is a separate booking or an add-on.

Avoid requiring visitors to understand salon terminology before they can ask for help. Add a line such as "Unsure which color service to book? Contact us before choosing an appointment." Name the contact method your staff actually checks.

If your online menu uses different wording from the homepage, fix the mismatch. Someone clicking "Book a haircut" should not land on a list of unexplained internal codes. Read the menu aloud to someone who does not work behind a chair.

## Explain prices without hiding the variables

Show current prices or honest starting prices where you can. If the price varies by stylist, hair length, density, or time required, explain the relevant differences beside the service. Do not display an attractive starting price with no explanation of who qualifies for it.

A useful example is "Color pricing depends on the work needed and the time reserved. We confirm the plan and price during your consultation." Use this only if it describes your actual process. Say whether that consultation has a fee and whether it can be completed before the appointment day.

Separate optional extras from the basic service. Clients should not discover at checkout that the blow-dry they expected was a separate charge. The guide to [putting prices on your website](/blog/should-you-put-prices-on-your-website/) can help you decide how much detail belongs on the homepage versus the full menu.

Keep any deposit, cancellation, and late-arrival information easy to find before booking. Use plain language and link to your complete policy from the booking journey. You do not need to crowd the opening paragraph with every condition, but important payment expectations should not be a surprise.

## Introduce the people and show relevant work

A stylist introduction should help someone choose a person, not read like an award ceremony. Give the stylist's name, their main services, and the way to book with them. Mention specialties accurately, without implying everyone on the team provides every service.

Use real examples of your work with the client's permission. Label a photo with useful context such as "soft highlights on naturally dark hair" or "short scissor cut with a tapered neckline." Avoid promising that another client can achieve the same result in the same visit.

For major color changes, explain that starting color, previous treatments, and hair condition affect the plan. This is more helpful than a gallery of dramatic transformations with no context. Keep photo captions short enough that the work remains easy to browse.

Only publish genuine client comments that you have permission to use. Never write a sample testimonial that looks like a real review. A brief, accurate explanation of your consultation process can build confidence while you are still gathering feedback.

## Make booking and arrival straightforward

Use a button that describes the next step: "Book an appointment," "Request a color consultation," or "Call about walk-ins." Do not label a contact form "Book now" if it only sends a request for staff to review. Tell people when their appointment is actually confirmed.

Place your street address, current hours, and phone number where a phone user can find them easily. Include practical arrival information, such as the entrance you use or where clients may park, when it is accurate. If you are inside a salon suite building, include the suite and instructions for finding it.

Answer accessibility questions factually. Describe the entrance or facilities you can verify, and invite clients to contact you about specific needs. Avoid broad promises that leave people guessing about stairs, seating, or space around the chair.

Keep these details consistent with your [salon Google Business Profile](/blog/salon-google-business-profile/). A homepage saying appointments only and a listing implying open walk-ins creates an avoidable problem before the visit begins.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I write for a salon and barbershop audience together?

Yes, if your business serves both, but separate the appointment choices clearly. A client looking for a fade should not have to read through a color consultation menu to find the right booking. Use service descriptions instead of expecting people to choose by label alone.

### What if every stylist sets their own prices?

Explain that prices vary by stylist and point people to the relevant menu before booking. Keep each stylist's booking link clearly named. Check that the homepage does not imply one fixed price across the team.

### Can I say that we welcome all hair types?

Only use that wording if your team can support it in practice. Describe the services and experience you can verify, and help clients choose the appropriate stylist. Specific information is more useful than a broad promise.

## The short version

Write your salon homepage around choosing a service, choosing a stylist, and knowing what happens next. Clear descriptions, honest pricing information, and accurate arrival details help a new client book with fewer questions.
