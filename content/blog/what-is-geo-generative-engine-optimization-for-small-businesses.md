GEO is generative engine optimization, the practice of improving your business information so AI search tools can understand it and use it when they generate an answer. Someone offers to make your business show up in AI answers and calls the service GEO. Do you need a whole new marketing job, or is this work you already do? Start by checking the actual tasks behind the label, because a clear service page can be useful even when an AI recommendation never follows.

## What does GEO actually mean?

Instead of only hoping someone clicks your website in a results list, you also want accurate information about your business to appear when someone asks a longer question.

Imagine a homeowner asking for a contractor who can replace a damaged wooden porch railing in their town. Your website says only that you provide quality home improvements. A useful revision would state that you repair and replace porch railings, identify the materials you work with, and explain which towns you serve.

That revision gives the homeowner something concrete to evaluate. It also supplies clear information that a search system might use. The second benefit is possible, not promised. An answer can omit you, misunderstand a page, or recommend a business that better matches the particular request.

## How does GEO relate to local SEO?

Search engine optimization, or SEO, is the work of helping search engines find and understand your website. Local SEO adds the location and business information needed for nearby searches. GEO focuses attention on generated answers, but the work often overlaps.

[Google's guidance on generative AI search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) says its AI search experiences rely on its existing search systems and that SEO remains relevant. It does not describe GEO as a separate shortcut around those systems.

For a small business, use one practical plan. Improve your service pages, correct your listings, and make contacting you straightforward. Don't pay twice for the same page edits because one invoice says SEO and another says GEO.

If the terminology is getting in the way, our guide to [how AI search is changing local discovery](/blog/how-ai-search-is-changing-how-customers-find-local-businesses/) explains the customer side. The useful question remains whether a stranger can establish that you handle their job in their area.

## Which information should you improve first?

Start with the information that decides whether a customer should call you. Look at your most important service page and check these details:

- The specific work you take on, such as repairing a dishwasher that will not drain.
- The work you decline, such as commercial kitchen equipment.
- The towns or neighborhoods you actually serve.
- How an appointment is arranged and when calls are answered.
- What affects the estimate and what information you need from the customer.

For an appliance repair business, list the appliance types and brands you genuinely service. Explain whether the customer should have the model number ready. Do not claim every repair can be completed on the first visit if parts sometimes need ordering.

Write these details as readable text. A photograph of a service menu should not be the only place your services appear. Keep the same facts on your main business listings so customers do not have to choose between conflicting hours or phone numbers. Our guide to [consistent business information in AI answers](/blog/why-consistent-business-information-matters-for-ai-answers/) covers that cleanup.

## How should you answer customer questions?

Use the questions you hear before booking a job. Answer the question immediately, then explain the condition that could change the answer.

For example, a mobile detailer might answer whether customers need to provide water: "Our mobile detailing appointments require access to an outdoor faucet. Tell us before booking if your apartment building does not provide one." That is sample wording for a business with that policy, not a rule for all detailers.

A tree service could explain whether stump grinding is included in a removal estimate. A cleaner could distinguish a move-out clean from a recurring visit. These are different buying decisions, so the answers should describe the actual work.

Put each answer beside the relevant service. Add a link to a fuller explanation when the details need more space. You can use [FAQ pages that answer customers and AI search](/blog/faq-pages-that-answer-customers-and-ai-search/) to organize questions that span several services.

Avoid publishing several nearly identical pages just to cover slight changes in wording. One accurate explanation of your process is easier to maintain than a pile of repetitive promises.

## Do you need special files or a technical rebuild?

Ask the person maintaining your site to check whether important pages can be found and indexed. If search engines cannot access a service page, polishing its wording will not fix the access problem. Have them show you the affected page and the repair in plain language.

Structured data means labels in a page's code that identify facts such as your business name. It can be part of ordinary website maintenance, but the labels should agree with what customers can read.

Google's [AI search guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) says special AI files and special schema markup are unnecessary for its search features. That does not settle what every other service uses, but it is a reason to question a pitch built entirely around a secret file.

Before approving paid work, request named pages, specific changes, a total cost, and a way to inspect the result. "Improve our service-area information" is reviewable. "Make AI love the business" is not.

## How can you check whether the work helped?

Keep a small record of questions a real customer might ask. Include your town and the service, then note the date, tool, answer, and any sources shown. Check the sources themselves before assuming the answer represents your business correctly.

If a recommendation lists an old phone number, look for that number on the cited page and your listings. Correct the source you control. If a directory owns the error, request a correction there. Our guide to [checking what AI assistants say about your business](/blog/how-to-check-what-ai-assistants-say-about-your-business/) walks through this process.

Treat these checks as spot checks, not proof of your position for every customer. Separately, ask new customers how they found you and record qualified inquiries and booked work. A screenshot of your name in an answer is interesting; it does not tell you whether the job was a good fit or whether anyone called.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can someone guarantee that AI will recommend my business?

A promise of guaranteed recommendations deserves skepticism. Ask what the provider can actually control, which pages will change, and how you can inspect the work. You are buying those deliverables, not control over another company's answers.

### Should I stop working on Google Business Profile?

Keep your profile accurate while improving your website. Customers still need the right service area, hours, phone number, and booking information. GEO is not a reason to neglect those details.

### Do I need to rewrite every page?

Start with your most important service and contact pages. Fix missing facts and misleading statements before rewriting good explanations. Expand only where a real customer question remains unanswered.

## The short version

GEO is a name for improving your chances of appearing accurately in AI search answers. Start with clear service details, honest answers, and consistent business information, then check what customers actually find. Treat promises of guaranteed recommendations as a reason to ask harder questions.
