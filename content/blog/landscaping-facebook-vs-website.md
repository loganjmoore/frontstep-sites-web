Is a Facebook page enough for your landscaping business, or do you need a website too? A small mowing route built on referrals may get useful enquiries from Facebook without a larger site. The decision changes when customers need to compare your services, understand your coverage, or request more involved projects.

## Where Facebook works well for a landscaping crew

Facebook is useful for showing work while it is recent. A cleanup before-and-after, a finished mulch job, or an update about seasonal scheduling can help people see what your crew does. You can share those updates without turning each one into a permanent website page.

It can also support existing relationships. A customer may recognize their neighborhood, remember a crew member, or share a post with a friend. Keep those posts truthful and get permission before showing identifiable properties or people.

For a small business offering a narrow service in a compact area, a clear page may handle early enquiries reasonably well. If customers already know you through referrals, they may only need a phone number and a few examples of work.

That doesn't make every social interaction a suitable lead. Someone liking a planting photo might be outside your route or looking for a service you don't offer. Put enough context in posts to identify the work and actual coverage.

## Where a social page becomes harder to use

A feed changes as you publish. The post explaining your mowing area can move away from the post about cleanup estimates. A new customer may need to piece together several updates to understand what your company currently offers.

Seasonal content can also become confusing. An old spring opening announcement may still be discovered after your route is full. Check important page information and make current availability clear rather than assuming the newest post replaces every earlier statement.

A larger project needs more explanation than a quick caption. Someone considering new beds or a patio may want to understand consultation, scope, access, and the estimate process. Repeating that conversation in private messages takes time and can produce inconsistent answers.

Visitors may also prefer to read your services without interacting on a social platform. Your potential customer should not need to learn how you organize posts just to discover whether you provide debris hauling with a cleanup.

## What a useful landscaping website adds

A website gives your main services stable pages. You can separate recurring lawn care from seasonal cleanup and installation projects. Each page can explain the work, coverage, and next step without competing with unrelated updates.

It also gives you a consistent destination to share. A referral, business card, vehicle sign, or social post can send someone to the same service information. The [landscaping website guide](/blog/website-landscaping/) describes the essentials without assuming you need a large site.

A project gallery can provide more context than a feed. Pair photos with a description of the customer's request and the actual work. Make clear whether an image shows a newly installed bed or a later stage of growth.

A website isn't automatically effective because it exists. It still needs accurate copy, readable mobile pages, and working forms. A neglected site with missing coverage details can be just as confusing as a neglected Facebook page.

Start with a scope you can maintain. A focused site explaining mowing and cleanups may be enough for one crew. Add more detailed project pages when they reflect services you actually provide.

## Decide based on your customers and your capacity

Look at recent enquiries. Are people asking the same questions about your route, visit scope, or cleanup process? Are you repeatedly explaining that mowing and landscape installation use different estimates? Those are good reasons to create clear website pages.

Consider how much work you can accept. If your recurring route is full and you do not want additional clients, a large marketing project may not be the immediate priority. Keeping your current information accurate still matters.

If you want more installation work, prospective customers may need deeper examples and a clearer estimate process. A website can organize those details while Facebook shares the newest project. Neither channel should promise work your crew cannot take on.

Budget for time as well as money. Someone must update route availability, remove expired offers, and respond to enquiries. The [landscaping website cost guide](/blog/landscaping-website-cost/) helps you consider different ways to handle that work.

Don't decide based on a promised number of leads. Ask which option makes it easier for a suitable customer to understand your service and reach the right person.

## Give each channel a specific job

Use the website for information that needs to remain easy to find. Use Facebook for fresh work, seasonal reminders, and appropriate local conversation. Connect them with links to the relevant service page rather than always sending people to the homepage.

For example, a leaf cleanup post can point to a page explaining the scope and quote process. A planting project can link to your installation service. A mowing route update can explain current coverage and direct customers to the right enquiry form.

Keep the basics consistent:

- Use the same business name and main phone number.
- Describe the same services and coverage.
- Check that current availability is clear in both places.
- Use photos you have permission to publish.
- Give customers an honest expectation for a reply.

The [landscaping local SEO checklist](/blog/landscaping-local-seo-checklist/) covers keeping business information consistent beyond Facebook. This matters when a person checks several places before contacting you.

Choose one person to keep an eye on enquiries. If messages and website forms go to different staff, agree on who responds and how they record the request. Avoid asking customers to repeat their property details because nobody checked the other inbox.

## Test the journey before spending more effort

Ask someone unfamiliar with the business to find your mowing area and request a cleanup estimate. Watch where they become uncertain. You may discover that a short service explanation solves more than a new batch of posts.

Test on a phone. Open the social link, follow it to the website, and submit a real test form. Confirm that the right person receives the information and that the customer sees an accurate next step.

Review the kinds of enquiries you receive, not just reactions or page views. A useful channel brings conversations that match your work and route. Clear information can also save time by helping unsuitable customers recognize the mismatch early.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I start with Facebook and add a website later?

Yes, that can be a reasonable approach for a focused business. Keep your services and contact information clear, then add a site when you need a more stable explanation of the work.

### Should I stop posting after the website launches?

Not if Facebook is useful for your customers and you can maintain it. Give posts a clear purpose and link to the service information people need next.

### Do I need separate photos for both channels?

No, you can reuse approved project photos where your permission allows it. Adjust the caption to explain the work clearly in each place.

### Will a website guarantee more landscaping jobs?

No, it cannot guarantee enquiries or booked work. Its practical job is to make your services understandable and your business easier to contact.

## The short version

Facebook can be useful for recent work and local relationships. A website is useful for stable service details, project explanations, and estimate requests. Choose a manageable setup and test how a new customer moves through it.
