You want to leave your web designer without losing your business address, website, or customer email. Start by finding out which parts you control and what can actually be moved. A calm, written handover plan gives both providers a clear job and helps keep customers reaching you while the work happens.

## Separate your domain from the rest of the website

Your domain is the address customers type. Your website is the collection of pages, images, software, and settings they reach there. Hosting runs the site, while email may use the same domain through a separate service.

These parts can sit with different companies even if one designer sends you a single invoice. Moving one does not automatically move the others. Begin with an inventory naming the provider, account holder, renewal date, and business contact for each service.

Include your domain registration, DNS management, website editor, hosting, email, booking system, form service, and search accounts. If any item is unclear, ask the designer to identify it. Our guide to [domain ownership](/blog/who-owns-your-domain-name-and-why-it-matters/) explains the difference between the registered holder and the company managing registration.

## Check the agreement before promising yourself a copy

Read the original proposal, contract, and later amendments. Look for ownership terms, cancellation notice, outstanding payments, export rights, and licenses for photos, fonts, themes, or plugins. Paying for a website does not by itself tell you which files or licenses transfer.

Ask concrete questions:

- Can I receive the site files and any required database?
- Can the site run with another host, or does it depend on your platform?
- Which content and images can I reuse?
- Which licenses or subscriptions need replacement?
- What handover assistance is included, and what requires a separate agreement?

A proprietary website builder may require a rebuild rather than a move of the existing site. Have your next provider review the export options before assuming a downloaded file will recreate everything. A folder of photographs is useful, but it is not a working website backup.

If the terms are disputed, ask a lawyer to review them. Our [website contract red flags guide](/blog/website-contracts-red-flags-for-small-business-owners/) gives you questions to raise without guessing what your agreement legally permits.

## Put domain access under your business's control

Confirm the registrar account, domain registration details, renewal method, and recovery email. Use an account your business controls, protect it with two-step verification, and keep recovery details somewhere you can access if your business email stops working.

If a registrar transfer is needed, ask the receiving registrar for its process. Eligible transfers commonly involve unlocking the domain, obtaining an authorization code, and approving the request. ICANN's [authorization code explanation](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/auth-2013-05-03-en) describes the code's role in transfers for applicable domains. Treat that code like a password and share it only through the agreed secure process.

Check transfer eligibility before changing registration details. Certain changes or recent registration events can trigger a transfer lock; ICANN's [domain transfer FAQ](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/name-holder-faqs-2017-10-10-en) explains common restrictions. Ask the registrar how these apply to your domain rather than assuming it can move today.

You may not need a registrar transfer at all to use new hosting. The [domain move guide](/blog/how-to-move-a-domain-to-a-new-provider/) helps you separate that decision from pointing the website address somewhere new.

## Ask for a specific handover package

Send a clear request with a proposed handover date. For example: "Please confirm the cancellation steps and provide the account transfers, files, and exports available under our agreement. Please also identify anything that will stop working when your service ends."

Ask for the material the next provider actually needs:

- Current page addresses, text, and original permitted images.
- Website files and database export, where supported and included.
- A copy of existing DNS records.
- Redirect rules and the current sitemap address.
- Form destinations, booking connections, and notification settings.
- A list of third-party services and licenses requiring attention.

Request access invitations through each service where possible rather than shared passwords. Keep your own secure copy of the handover material, then have the next provider test whether it is complete and usable.

Decide what happens to stored inquiries and customer records. Export only what your business needs through an appropriate secure method, and agree on handling remaining copies. Do not move a customer list around as an ordinary email attachment merely because it is convenient.

## Build and test the replacement before the cutoff

Give your new provider the approved scope and handover package. If rebuilding is necessary, preserve useful page addresses and content where practical. Our guide to [switching providers while protecting search visibility](/blog/how-to-switch-website-providers-without-losing-google-rankings/) covers redirects and post-launch checks.

Walk through the preview like a customer. A pest control customer should be able to find the pests you treat, confirm the service area, and request an appointment. Test the telephone link and form yourself, then confirm that the inquiry reaches the correct person.

Before the domain points to the new site, agree on who changes DNS and who checks email. If DNS management moves, the new records must preserve the email service and other connections you still use. Switching the website does not transfer old mailboxes or messages.

Choose a handover time when the people doing the work are available. Keep the old services running until launch checks pass and both providers confirm what remains dependent on them. Put the cancellation date in writing so nobody assumes that a preview approval means permission to shut everything down.

## Close the handover and choose clearer terms next time

After launch, test the live site, send and receive business email, and open important old page links. Confirm that your business can access the registrar, editor, billing, and reporting accounts it needs. Record renewal dates and who handles future changes.

Then remove the former provider's access that is no longer required. Ask the new provider to review verification methods and connected integrations as well as user lists. Keep copies of final invoices and the completed handover checklist.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I keep my domain if the designer bought it?

Check who is recorded as the domain holder and what your agreement says. Ask for access or a proper transfer through the registrar. If ownership is disputed, get advice on the agreement rather than assuming possession of an invoice settles it.

### Will transferring the domain move my website too?

No. A domain registration transfer changes the company managing the registration. Your website files, hosting, and mailboxes need their own arrangements.

### What if the designer will not respond?

Keep a written record of your requests and gather contracts, invoices, and account information. Contact the registrar about its account recovery or transfer process if you are the legitimate domain holder. Ask a lawyer about unresolved contractual or ownership disputes.

### Can my new provider copy the site from the internet?

They may be able to recreate permitted public content, but that does not prove you have rights to reuse every element. Public pages also omit items such as databases and form settings. Review permissions and missing functionality before relying on a rebuild.

### What is the best option for a web site?

There is no single best option. A builder such as Wix or Squarespace fits if you want to make the pages yourself and can set aside the time. A done-for-you service fits if you want a provider to build the site and make changes when you ask, and Front Step Sites is one example at $99 a year with the domain included.
