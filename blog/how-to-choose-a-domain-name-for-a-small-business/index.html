Choosing a domain name for your business

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Choose a domain that closely matches your business name and is easy to say, spell, and remember. Prefer a simple address over a string of services and towns. Check for confusingly similar businesses, review renewal terms, and make sure the registration and account remain under your control rather than a designer’s personal account.

A domain name is the web address customers type or tap to reach your business online. You need one, but your first choice may already be taken. Should you add your town, use a different ending, or put a hyphen in the name? Choose an address that customers can repeat correctly and that you can keep as the business grows.

Start with the name customers already know

Write down your business name as it appears on your signs, estimates, and invoices. That is the natural starting point because a referred customer will probably remember the business before remembering the exact web address. Avoid choosing a completely different online name unless you have a clear reason.

Try a short version that still identifies you. You can often leave out punctuation and a formal business suffix without losing recognition. Do not remove so much that the result becomes a vague abbreviation nobody outside the company understands.

Read the candidate aloud as though you were leaving a voicemail. Then ask someone to write it down without seeing it. If you need to explain several spellings or repeat where the words split, keep looking.

Also read the words together in lowercase. A harmless business name can become difficult to parse when spaces disappear. Check what the address would look like on a truck, a receipt, and a small phone screen before committing to it.

Choose a simple ending and avoid awkward fixes

A familiar .com is a sensible first option when an appropriate name is available. It is not worth turning your address into a long jumble merely to obtain that ending. A clear alternative can be preferable to an address that people repeatedly mistype.

If the exact name is taken, consider adding a relevant word from your real business, such as the service you provide. A town name may help if the business is firmly tied to that place, but think about whether it will still fit after an expansion or move.

Be cautious with hyphens and numbers. You will have to explain "with a hyphen" or whether a number is written as a digit. If a number is already part of your recognizable brand, test it aloud rather than assuming it will be confusing or clear.

Our guide to whether your business should use a .com explores that choice further. Do not assume a particular ending guarantees trust, search placement, or better customers. The whole address needs to fit the business.

Check for confusion before you register

Search the proposed name and close variations. Look for businesses using similar wording, especially in the same industry or area. If a customer could easily land on a competitor's site after hearing your address, consider another candidate.

Domain availability is not permission to use a name. Check relevant business and trademark resources, and ask a lawyer if you are unsure about a potential conflict. This is a general naming check, not a substitute for advice about your particular situation.

Look at nearby spellings without treating every one as a domain you must buy. You are trying to understand likely mistakes, not create an expensive collection. If the simple version points to another active business, adding a small punctuation change may leave the confusion unresolved.

Check social account names as well if you plan to use them. Exact matches everywhere are convenient, but do not let one unavailable username force a bad website address. A clear, consistent business identity matters more than squeezing the same awkward abbreviation onto every platform.

Understand what you are buying and renewing

A domain is the address, while hosting is the service that makes the website available. Buying one does not automatically mean you have bought the other. Email may also require separate arrangements, so ask what is included before assuming your new address provides a mailbox.

Read the registration and renewal terms. Compare the renewal cost with any introductory offer, check the registration period, and look for extra services added to the order. You do not need to accept every suggested add-on to secure an ordinary business domain.

If an address is already registered, do not build your entire launch around obtaining it. A different clear name may be easier to manage than negotiating for a particular one. Never assume an unused-looking website means the domain has been abandoned.

The guide to hosting in plain English explains the separate pieces. Ask for a simple written explanation of the domain, hosting, email, and renewal arrangements so you know which provider handles each responsibility.

Keep registration and recovery under your control

Make sure you or your business are recorded as the registrant, and keep access to the account used to manage the domain. A designer can help set it up without putting the registration under their own personal identity. Know how to contact the registrar, which is the company managing the registration.

Use a strong, unique password and additional sign-in protection where available. Keep recovery information current, and use a recovery address you will still be able to access if the business website or its email stops working. Store the essential details somewhere the business can reliably retrieve them.

Set renewal reminders and check payment details even if automatic renewal is enabled. An expired card or abandoned mailbox can undermine a plan that seemed automatic. ICANN's domain registrant resources cover registration management, renewal, and protecting your domain.

Before a provider takes over, read the guide to who owns your domain. Ask what happens if you leave and how you retain control. These questions are easier to settle at the start than after a disagreement or a staff change.

Frequently asked questions

Should my domain include every service I offer?

No, the website pages can describe your full range of services. Choose a domain that identifies the business clearly without becoming a long list. Think about whether the name will still fit if your services change.

Should I change my existing domain just to get a shorter one?

Not automatically, especially if customers already know the current address. Consider the work involved in updating printed materials, email, and website links. A modest improvement in length may not justify the disruption.

Can my web designer register the domain for me?

Yes, but confirm that the registration is in your name and that you retain control. Keep the account and recovery arrangements documented. Do not rely only on an informal promise that it will be handed over later.
