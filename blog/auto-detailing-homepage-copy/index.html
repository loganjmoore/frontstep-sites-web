Auto detailing homepage: what to write

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Your detailing homepage should say what you clean, where you work, and how customers get a quote. Describe packages by the work included, explain condition-based pricing, and show real examples. Give mobile customers the parking, water, and power requirements before they book, and keep the next step easy to find.

What should you write on your detailing homepage when "we make cars look great" could describe any shop? Start with the jobs you actually do and the decisions customers need to make. Good copy helps someone with a muddy SUV or scratched daily driver choose the right conversation.

Write a first line that identifies the service

Your first heading should tell visitors whether you work at a shop, travel to them, or do both. Include your real town or service area. Keep the promise grounded in the work rather than reaching for phrases such as "the ultimate automotive experience."

Here are example lines to adapt only if they match your business:

  • "Mobile interior and exterior detailing in Cedar Park."
  • "Interior cleaning for family cars, trucks, and SUVs."
  • "Paint correction and ceramic coating consultations at our local studio."

Follow the heading with a sentence that helps people act: "Tell us about your vehicle and send photos for a condition-based quote." If you price through an inspection instead, explain that process.

Do not copy the example town or services without changing them. The detailing website guide explains how this opening fits into the rest of the site.

Describe packages by what the customer gets

Package names are labels, not explanations. Under each one, describe the included work in terms a driver can picture. "Seats and carpets vacuumed, interior surfaces cleaned, and inside glass cleaned" says more than "complete cabin renewal."

If extraction, pet hair removal, or odor work costs extra, put that information beside the package. Do not hide it below the booking form. Explain where you need to assess condition before committing to a price or result.

For exterior work, separate a maintenance wash from a service involving decontamination or polishing. If you offer ceramic coatings, explain the preparation and assessment in everyday language. Avoid describing a coating as a way to make a car immune to damage.

A short service introduction might read: "Our interior detail covers everyday dirt and buildup. Heavy pet hair, spills, and neglected interiors need a separate assessment before we confirm the work." Use that wording only when it accurately describes your scope.

Explain the price without creating a surprise

Customers want to know whether they are considering a quick tidy-up or a substantial appointment. You can provide useful context without inventing one price that covers every vehicle.

Write down the factors you actually use when quoting:

  • Vehicle size and seating layout.
  • Current interior or exterior condition.
  • Requested services and optional work.
  • Travel arrangements for mobile appointments.

If you publish a starting price, describe what that starting price covers. Place the qualifier next to the number. "Final price confirmed after we review vehicle condition" should appear before someone assumes the price is fixed.

Explain what happens if the car arrives in a different condition from the photos. Customers should know you will discuss changed work before proceeding. The website pricing guide covers ways to communicate those boundaries clearly.

Put mobile logistics before the booking button

A mobile detailer needs more than a street address. Your homepage should tell customers whether you need a driveway, access to electricity, or an available water supply. If you bring your own equipment and supplies, state the facts without assuming every parking situation will work.

Explain your arrangements for apartments, shared parking, and workplace visits. For example: "Please confirm that detailing is permitted at your parking location before requesting a workplace appointment." Do not imply that permission is automatic.

Weather also belongs in the booking explanation. Say how you contact customers if conditions prevent the work, and what happens to their appointment. Avoid promising outdoor service in every situation just because your van can reach the address.

For a fixed shop, replace those details with drop-off instructions, collection arrangements, and whether the customer can wait. Keep the instructions specific to how your business actually operates.

Let proof explain the result honestly

Choose a few photographs that demonstrate your main services. Pair each with a short, factual caption. "Interior detail on a family SUV, including carpet extraction" explains the work better than "Another flawless transformation."

Use customer-approved images and keep personal belongings, plates, and identifying location details out of view. Comparable before and after angles help people understand what changed. Avoid filtering or lighting choices that suggest a result the service did not produce.

Customer feedback can support the same practical points. Use authentic reviews with accurate attribution and permission where needed. Do not invent a quotation about how a coating held up or imply that one result is guaranteed on every vehicle.

If your photos need improvement, the phone photography guide offers a straightforward starting point.

Write the contact section like the next conversation

Tell customers what you need first, then explain your response. A useful example is: "Send your vehicle make and model, the service you're considering, and your town. We'll review the details and explain the next step before confirming an appointment."

Choose button labels that match what happens. "Request a detailing quote" fits a form that starts a conversation. "Book an appointment" should lead to a process that actually books one. If you offer both, explain which option fits a customer who is unsure about the service.

Read the page aloud as if you were answering the phone. Cut phrases you would never say to a customer. Then ask someone unfamiliar with detailing to explain the difference between your packages after reading them.

Before publishing, test the form on your phone and verify that the message arrives. A clearly written request still fails if it disappears into an inbox nobody checks.

Frequently asked questions

Should I promise to remove every stain?

No, describe the work you perform and explain that results depend on condition. Customers need honest expectations about older stains, wear, and damage. Use an assessment when you cannot judge the job from a description.

Can my homepage focus on luxury cars?

Yes, if those are the vehicles and services your business is built around. If your schedule mainly serves family cars and work trucks, show those too. People should be able to recognize their own needs in your examples.

How much text belongs above the first button?

Use enough to identify your service, location, and next step. A clear heading and a short explanation are a sensible starting point. Put package details farther down so customers can investigate without delaying someone ready to contact you.
