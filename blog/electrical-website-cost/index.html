Electrical: what a website costs in 2026

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

An electrician's website budget should reflect the services you need to explain and the help you want maintaining it. DIY plans, done-for-you services, and custom projects cover different amounts of work. Compare content, ongoing changes, domain ownership, and contact tools alongside price, especially if panel upgrades, EV charging, or commercial projects need separate explanations.

What should an electrician pay for a website in 2026? Start with the jobs you want it to explain, such as residential troubleshooting, panel work, EV charger installations, or commercial lighting. A useful price comparison shows who will write those pages, keep them accurate, and make sure customers can reach you.

Define the work before asking for a price

An electrician doing small residential jobs has different website needs from a contractor bidding commercial fit-outs. Write a short brief describing your customers, services, coverage, and preferred inquiry method. Give the same brief to each provider so you can compare the resulting quotes.

For a residential electrical business, the initial pages might cover everyday repairs, panel and service work, EV charging, lighting, and contact details. Combine smaller services where that helps readers. Separate work that brings different questions or needs a different estimate process.

Explain any important boundaries. If you install chargers but do not handle public charging networks, say so. If your commercial work is limited to small shops and offices, do not ask for a site that suggests large industrial capabilities.

Include readable mobile pages, a working phone link, and a short inquiry form in the scope. Our electrician website essentials guide helps identify the information a potential customer needs before contacting you.

Compare the main ways to get the site built

A DIY builder can suit you if you enjoy editing pages and can make time for setup. You are taking responsibility for the words, photos, layout, and ongoing checks. The subscription alone does not produce a complete description of your electrical business.

As of September 2026, Wix Light costs $17/month billed yearly ($204/year), and Squarespace Basic costs $19/month. Treat those as plan prices, not finished-site quotes. Confirm current terms and the full cost of your intended setup before buying.

A done-for-you service can fit an owner who prefers to supply business information and request changes. Ask who writes service descriptions, how revisions work, and what happens when you add a new line of work. Compare those answers with the time you would spend managing a builder yourself.

Typical freelancer or agency projects often cost a few thousand dollars, with more involved custom work running considerably higher. This is a broad planning range, not a quoted fee. Custom work can make sense for complex project portfolios, multiple audiences, or a larger marketing program.

The done-for-you versus building-it-yourself guide can help you choose the level of involvement that fits your working week.

Pay for explanations that help customers choose

Good electrical service pages need more than a list of technical terms. A homeowner considering an EV charger wants to know what you assess before quoting. Someone arranging panel work wants to understand the visit, coordination, and written scope.

Ask whether the quote includes an interview about your process and original service copy. You should review the finished words for accuracy, especially statements about credentials, equipment, and the jobs you accept. A provider should not invent those details to fill space.

Useful content work could include:

  • Explaining a diagnostic visit and when repair work is authorized.
  • Describing the information needed for an EV charging estimate.
  • Separating panel replacement from broader service work.
  • Preparing project photos and factual captions.
  • Explaining access and scheduling for commercial lighting jobs.

Those deliverables may justify more effort than a decorative layout change. Get them named in the quote. "SEO copy included" is too vague unless you know which pages will be written and who checks the claims.

Keep inquiry tools proportionate to the job

You may not need online booking for every electrical service. A homeowner with an intermittent fault needs a conversation, while a planned lighting project may start with a form. Use the contact method that matches how you actually arrange work.

A practical form can ask for name, contact details, town, and a short job description. For project inquiries, optional photos or product details may help. Do not require customers to open panels or expose wiring to supply a picture.

If someone proposes a detailed estimating calculator, ask what reliable answer it will provide. An instant price may be misleading when the existing installation, access, and scope need assessment. A clear explanation of the estimate process could serve the customer better.

Commercial document uploads or links to an existing scheduling process can be useful when your office already handles them. Add such tools because they solve a real need, not because they make a proposal sound more complete. Confirm any associated upkeep before agreeing.

Ask about recurring work and ownership

A finished site still needs attention. Your service area may change, an old credential may need updating, or a new lighting project may deserve a page. Find out who handles these changes and what the arrangement costs over time.

Ask the provider to explain:

  • The first-year total and normal recurring charges.
  • Who controls the domain and where it is registered.
  • What content changes are included.
  • How new pages are requested and priced.
  • How quickly routine changes are normally handled.
  • What happens to your domain and website when you leave.

Keep domain ownership and website portability separate in the discussion. Access to your web address does not automatically mean you receive every design file or software component. Get the actual terms rather than assuming what "ownership" means.

The website provider hiring questions give you a fuller checklist. For legal questions about an agreement, ask a lawyer rather than relying on a sales explanation.

Review a small finished site before expanding it

Test the customer journey you are paying for. Open the site on a phone, find EV charging or panel work, check coverage, and try the contact options. Submit a real test inquiry and verify that it reaches the person who responds.

Read the site as someone who does not know electrical terminology. Does it explain what you do without suggesting a diagnosis before inspection? Are credentials stated accurately? Does the commercial page describe work your team actually accepts?

Fix those basics before commissioning a large library of town pages or elaborate animations. If you later add a separate commercial division or a substantial project portfolio, expand around that real change. You can spend deliberately without trying to predict every future need at launch.

Frequently asked questions

Does an electrician need a custom website?

Not always. A straightforward site can explain services, show real projects, and collect inquiries well. Custom work becomes more useful when you have requirements a simpler setup cannot comfortably handle.

Should EV charging have its own page?

It often deserves one if it is a meaningful service for your business. Customers have specific questions about equipment, parking location, and the estimate process. A separate page gives those questions room without overwhelming the homepage.

Is a bigger site worth paying more for?

Only when the extra pages contain useful, distinct information. Repeated paragraphs with different town names do not make a proposal more valuable. Ask what each page helps a customer understand or do.

What if I do not have professional photos?

Start with clear, truthful pictures of your team and completed work where you have permission. Ask whether the provider will prepare the images for the web. Do not substitute stock photos and label them as your projects.

The short version

Set an electrical website budget around the services, explanations, and upkeep you need. Compare complete scopes, then check the finished contact process and ownership terms. Add specialized tools only when they support real work.
