How fast should your website load?

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Aim for the main visible content to load within 2.5 seconds, the good threshold for Google's Largest Contentful Paint measure. That is not a promise that every page element finishes loading then. Your business details, phone link, and booking route should also respond promptly and stay steady while customers use the page on a phone.

How fast should your small business website load before you need to fix it? Aim for a page that quickly shows useful information and lets someone call, book, or request a quote without waiting or fighting moving buttons. A speed score helps you find problems, but the customer's task is the reason to solve them.

Use a clear target without confusing it with everything loading

Google's Core Web Vitals use several measures of page experience. The loading measure, Largest Contentful Paint, looks at when the largest visible content element appears. Its good threshold is 2.5 seconds or less.

The other measures cover response to interaction and unexpected movement. The good thresholds are 200 milliseconds or less for Interaction to Next Paint, and 0.1 or less for Cumulative Layout Shift. These targets are assessed at the 75th percentile of visits, separately for mobile and desktop. See Google's Web Vitals guidance.

You do not need to memorize those names to manage your site. Ask your provider whether the main content appears quickly, whether controls respond, and whether the layout stays steady. Those are different questions from whether every gallery image has downloaded.

For a local service business, put the service, coverage area, and contact route early in the page. A fast decorative banner is not enough if customers still cannot find your number. The phone-first website guide explains that broader customer experience.

Test a real customer task on your phone

Start with a simple check you can do yourself. Open your site using a mobile connection and behave like someone visiting for the first time. Test a service page as well as the homepage, because a search result may send customers directly there.

Try these tasks:

  • Find the service you need and confirm the business serves your area.
  • Tap the phone number and check that it opens the expected call action.
  • Open the menu and move to another service page.
  • Fill out and submit a test inquiry.
  • Follow the booking link through to the correct service or location.

Watch for blank space, delayed text, buttons that shift, and controls that appear ready but do not respond. Note the page address and the exact action that failed. "The quote button moves when the reviews load" is more useful to a provider than "the website feels bad."

Do not use only your own fast office connection. Also remember that a page you visit repeatedly may have files saved by your browser, so a fresh visit can behave differently.

Use PageSpeed Insights to identify the next question

Run your important page addresses through PageSpeed Insights. Review the mobile results and the explanations of slow elements. Save the page address and test date so you can compare the same page after a change.

The tool can show both real-user data and a simulated test. Real-user data reflects collected visits; the simulated test helps investigate a page under controlled conditions. A small or new site may not have enough real-user data, and some reports use information for the wider site instead of just the page. See Google's PageSpeed Insights documentation.

Treat the report as a set of clues. If it highlights a large image, ask which image and whether it delays useful content. If it flags script work, ask what feature that script supports. You should be able to connect the proposed fix to something on the page.

Do not assume a single good score proves that your booking form works. Automated loading tests and a real customer journey answer different questions.

Fix oversized media and unnecessary extras first

Start with visible, explainable problems. A homepage video may be attractive but should earn the time it takes to load. A huge phone photograph does not need to remain huge when displayed as a small project card.

Ask your provider to review:

  • Images that are much larger than their displayed size.
  • Automatically playing videos and oversized slideshows.
  • Extra fonts that add little to readability.
  • Repeated tracking tools or features nobody uses.
  • Embedded feeds, maps, chat tools, and review widgets.

Do not delete useful features blindly. A booking tool may be essential, while a second animated gallery may not be. Ask whether a feature can load when needed or be reached through a clear link without delaying the first screen.

Keep the main opening image available promptly; loading every image late is not the goal. Lower-page galleries can be handled differently from the content people need immediately. The business photo guide helps you choose useful images instead of simply adding more.

Protect calling, forms, and booking while improving speed

The important question is what a visitor can accomplish. A garage door caller should be able to find a number quickly. A salon client should be able to reach the correct booking calendar. A painting customer should be able to send an estimate request and know it arrived.

After every meaningful speed change, repeat those tasks. Check that phone links still work, required form fields are understandable, and submissions reach the right inbox. If you removed a script, confirm that it was not responsible for the form or booking tool.

Pay attention to content that moves after the page appears. A late-loading banner should not push a contact button away while someone taps it. Ask your provider to reserve space for images and embeds when they are causing this problem.

The contact form guide can help you check the inquiry experience. A quick page with an unusable form still leaves the customer unable to contact you.

Give your provider a short, testable request

Send the affected page addresses, what you observed on your phone, and the relevant speed report. Ask for the main cause, the proposed change, and how they will confirm the customer path still works.

A useful request might be: "On mobile, the contact button moves when the gallery appears, and the service page takes a long time to show its text. Please check those issues and retest the quote form after the changes."

Retest the same pages after the work. Compare the experience and the report without expecting every test run to be identical. Real-user reporting also reflects previous visits, so it may not show the full effect immediately.

Keep a simple note of what changed. Recheck after adding a large gallery, switching booking tools, or redesigning the opening section. Routine checks are easier when you know which customer tasks matter most.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a perfect speed score?

No, a perfect test score is not the business goal. Use the report to identify meaningful delays, then confirm that people can read, call, and book comfortably. Do not break a useful feature just to improve one number.

Why does my site feel fast to me but slow to someone else?

Devices, connections, and previously saved browser files can differ. Test a fresh visit on a phone and compare specific pages and actions. That gives your provider a clearer problem to investigate.

Will a faster website guarantee more customers?

No, speed alone cannot guarantee calls or bookings. It can remove delays that make the site harder to use. Your services, availability, trust information, and contact process still need to make sense.

The short version

Use the published speed targets as a starting point, then test what customers actually need to do. Fix delays and moving controls while keeping calling, forms, and booking reliable.
