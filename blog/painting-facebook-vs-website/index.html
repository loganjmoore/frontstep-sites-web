Painting: Facebook page vs. a website

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A Facebook page can help a painter share recent work and stay visible to people who already know the business. A website gives new homeowners a clear place to check services, preparation, coverage, and estimate details. Facebook alone can suit a referral-led schedule, but using both makes the information easier to organize.

Your painting jobs come from neighbors, repeat customers, and photos you post on Facebook. Do you really need a website as well? The answer depends on whether new customers can find the details they need without messaging you to ask the same questions every time.

Recognize what Facebook already does well

Painting produces useful visual stories. A dark dining room becomes brighter, tired porch railings get refreshed, and worn cabinets look cared for again. Facebook can be a practical place to share that work with people who follow your business or hear about you from a neighbor.

It also suits timely updates. You might share a recently completed exterior, explain what your crew is working on, or announce that you are taking interior estimates for the coming season. Keep availability claims current and avoid presenting a temporary opening as a standing promise.

If referrals already fill your schedule, a well-maintained page may cover your immediate needs. That is especially true if you handle a narrow service area, quote a limited range of work, and reliably answer messages. A website should solve an actual information problem, not become another neglected task.

Review the questions in your inbox. If people repeatedly ask whether you paint cabinets, travel to their town, or include preparation, your current page is not doing enough of the explaining. Those questions tell you what should be easier to find.

Understand the limits of a project feed

A homeowner planning an interior repaint needs more than a finished-room photo. They want to know who moves furniture, whether ceilings are included, and how long rooms may be out of use. A caption about one project may answer some of that, but it is not a reliable guide to your whole service.

The same problem appears with cabinet work. A visitor might see a cabinet photo and assume you offer on-site refinishing, door replacement, or a finish you do not actually provide. Clear service descriptions help prevent the wrong inquiry before you invest time in an estimate.

Older posts can also create confusion. A past discount, a fully booked month, or an old phone number may remain visible after the situation changes. Check your public information from the perspective of someone who knows nothing about the company.

Do not assume every prospective customer wants to start a conversation through Facebook. Give them a clear phone number and another practical contact route. A person comparing painters during a lunch break may simply want the facts before choosing whom to call.

Use a website for the details that stay useful

A small painting website can put the essential information in a predictable order. Start with the services and coverage, show relevant projects, explain how estimates work, and offer a direct way to contact you. It does not need a large collection of pages to be helpful.

Give distinct painting services their own explanations. Someone searching for "exterior house painter" has different concerns from someone arranging a rental turnover. Explain the preparation and access questions each project raises, using your actual process.

Publish a few project examples with context. Describe the original condition, the agreed scope, and the completed result without identifying the homeowner. The painting website guide outlines which details deserve space before you add decorative extras.

You also gain a stable address to print on your truck, estimate paperwork, and business cards. Register the domain in your own name and keep control of the account. It should be a business asset you can retain if you later change website providers.

Compare the upkeep you can realistically handle

Facebook and a website both need accurate information. Choosing both does not mean writing two separate streams of daily content. Let your website hold the service details and let Facebook carry selected updates from recent work.

The real cost includes your attention. If you spend evenings answering questions that a clear service page could handle, consider that workload. If your current setup already produces suitable inquiries and you are fully booked, start small rather than building pages you cannot maintain.

Ask a website provider who updates services, prices, photos, and availability, and how you request changes. Confirm who owns the domain and what happens if you leave. Our guide to website builders versus done-for-you websites helps compare the work involved.

Avoid choosing based on promised rankings or a fixed number of leads. A website can clarify your offer and make contact easier, but it cannot make every visitor need a repaint or fit your schedule. Evaluate whether it reduces confusion and supports the kind of jobs you want.

Give each channel a clear job

When you share an exterior project, point readers toward the page that explains exterior painting estimates. When you share cabinet work, link to the cabinet service details. This gives interested people a sensible next step beyond liking a photograph.

Keep the website connected to your Facebook page for visitors who want to browse recent activity. Do not make the homepage depend on a busy social feed loading correctly. Important details such as coverage, services, and your phone number should be readable on the website itself.

Use a simple routine after a completed project:

  • Get permission for photographs before sharing them.
  • Select a few pictures that show the relevant work clearly.
  • Add a useful project description to the website when appropriate.
  • Share a short Facebook update pointing to the relevant service.
  • Check that any availability statement still reflects your schedule.

Keep one main route for tracking estimate requests, even when they start in different places. The painting local SEO checklist can help you keep your website and other business information aligned. Do not let a Facebook message sit unanswered because you assumed it arrived through your site.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a mistake to start with only Facebook?

No, it can be a reasonable starting point for a small referral-led painting business. Keep your services, contact details, and recent work clear. Add a website when customers need information that your page makes difficult to find.

Should I delete my Facebook page after launching a site?

Usually there is no reason to discard a page that customers use. Keep it as a place for recent projects and direct people to the website for detailed service information. Choose an update routine you can sustain.

Can I use the same project photos in both places?

Yes, if you have permission to publish them. Use a short caption on Facebook and a fuller explanation on the relevant website page. Make sure each caption accurately describes the work and does not imply a result every house will achieve.

The short version

Facebook is useful for showing recent painting work and keeping in touch with an existing audience. A website gives prospective customers an organized place to understand services and request an estimate.
