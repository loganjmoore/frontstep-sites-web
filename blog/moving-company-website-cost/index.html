Why the moving industry needs a website that does real work

Moving is a bigger market than most owners realize. According to the U.S. Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey, 39.6 million people, about 11.8% of the U.S. population, moved that year. Of those, roughly 29.9 million moved within the same state and about 7.1 million moved to a different state. That in-state versus interstate split matters for your website, because it's two different services with two different sets of rules.

Price matters just as much as reach. According to This Old House's 2025-2026 moving survey, a local move averages $1,489, while a long-distance move averages $3,124. Full-service movers in that survey charged around $1,037 for moves under 50 miles and $6,164 for moves between 1,101 and 1,400 miles. When the range is that wide, a customer needs your site to tell them which end of it they're likely to land on, or at least how to get a real number.

The specific pages a mover's site needs

A moving company site isn't a single "book now" page. Customers researching a move are comparing options carefully: This Old House's survey found that 43% of homeowners got quotes from two moving companies and 29% got quotes from three before choosing one, with 40% naming cost as their top factor and 16% naming reviews. Your site is competing in that comparison round, so it needs to hold its own on a few specific pages:

  • A service area page that states plainly which towns or metro areas you cover, since "we move anywhere" reads as vague to a shopper trying to narrow down options.
  • Separate local and long-distance pages. A local move and an interstate move are different products with different pricing logic, and lumping them together makes both harder to explain.
  • A plain-English page on how your estimates work, including whether you offer binding or non-binding estimates. Movers who are clear about this upfront save themselves disputes and bad reviews later.
  • A commercial or office-moving page, if that's part of your business, since it's a different buyer with different questions than a household move.

The interstate registration your website should reflect

If you move households across state lines, federal law requires you to register with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration and hold a USDOT number and MC operating authority. FMCSA notes that this requirement applies specifically to interstate moves; a purely local, in-state operation is exempt. If you're registered, your USDOT number belongs on your website, usually in the footer or on your long-distance page. It's a small detail that a shopper comparing quotes can actually verify, and skipping it looks worse than not mentioning it at all.

Getting quotes without overpaying

Write your real page list before you ask for prices: home page, local moving, long-distance moving, commercial moving if applicable, an estimates page, and a quote-request form. Hand that same list to every freelancer or agency you talk to. If one quote covers three pages and another covers eight, you're not comparing prices, you're comparing different projects.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need separate pages for local and long-distance moves?

Yes, if you offer both. They're different services with different pricing and different customer questions, and combining them into one vague page makes it harder for either kind of customer to find what they need.

Should my USDOT number be on my website?

If you're registered for interstate moves, yes. It's a small trust signal that costs nothing to add and lets a cautious customer verify you're a legitimate, regulated mover.

How much does a custom moving company website cost from a freelancer?

Typical freelancer pricing for a small business site often runs a few thousand dollars, but it varies with scope. Get a written page list and compare quotes against that same scope.

Do I need an online booking form, or is a quote request enough?

A quote-request form is usually enough to start, especially if your estimates depend on an in-home or video walkthrough. Add real booking later if your process genuinely supports confirming a date online.
