How often should you update your website?

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Update your business website whenever a customer-facing fact changes, especially your hours, prices, services, contact details, or availability. A monthly check is a useful routine for catching mistakes, with a deeper review before busy seasons. You do not need to rewrite good pages just to make the website look active.

How often should you update your business website when you already have a full work schedule? Change important facts as soon as they change, then use a regular check to catch anything you missed. The aim is to keep customers correctly informed and able to contact you.

Fix changes that affect today's customer first

Some updates should not wait for a monthly website session. A wrong phone number, expired offer, or service you no longer provide can send someone down the wrong path right now.

Make an immediate update when you change:

  • Business hours or a planned closure.
  • Phone numbers, email addresses, or your business location.
  • Published prices, fees, or offer terms.
  • Services offered or towns you cover.
  • Appointment availability that the site specifically advertises.

For example, an HVAC contractor who stops taking weekend repair calls should remove that promise wherever it appears. A message on the contact page will not correct "weekend repairs available" on the homepage.

Search your site for the old information and check the footer, service pages, and downloadable documents. Update your business listings too. The guide to consistent business names, addresses, and phone numbers explains why keeping one current set of details makes this work easier.

Use a monthly check as a practical starting point

A monthly review is a suggested working routine, not a search engine requirement. Pick a date that fits your existing office work, such as the day you review invoices or next month's schedule.

Open the site on your phone and act like a new customer. Start from a service page as well as the homepage. Ask whether you can tell what the business does, where it works, and how to reach it.

Then run a short check:

  1. Tap the phone number and confirm it shows the correct number.
  2. Submit a clearly marked test inquiry and confirm it reaches the right inbox.
  3. Check the booking link, if you have one.
  4. Read hours, prices, and service area details.
  5. Look for broken links and missing pictures.
  6. Remove outdated announcements.

Follow the test inquiry through to the person who normally answers it. A confirmation on the screen does not prove your office received the message. Use the contact form guide if that handoff needs attention.

Plan seasonal updates before the season starts

Seasonal businesses have a reason to revisit their main pages before demand changes. Put those dates on the calendar while you are planning staffing, supplies, or appointment capacity.

A landscaper might switch the homepage emphasis from mowing to fall cleanups and leaf removal. A heating contractor might review furnace maintenance information before cooler weather. An auto detailer might need to explain which services can still be done outdoors when the weather changes.

Check more than the headline. Review booking expectations, preparation instructions, photos, and any offer dates. If you advertise limited seasonal appointments, assign someone to keep that statement accurate.

Keep useful service pages available when appropriate instead of rebuilding them each season. A customer researching irrigation winterization early still needs a clear explanation of the service. You can update when bookings open without deleting the page.

Use seasonal marketing for local service businesses to connect website changes to the work you actually want to book.

Add new photos and answers when they help

You do not need to replace every photo each month. Add pictures when you have something relevant to show: a service customers ask about, a better example of finished work, or a current view of the people arriving at their property.

Check older pictures for misleading details. A team photo may include people who have left. A van may show an old phone number. A workshop photo may direct customers toward a location you no longer use.

Keep a small folder of possible updates as you work. Note what the project involved and whether you have permission to publish the photos. Choose useful examples rather than uploading every image from a job.

Do the same with customer questions. If callers repeatedly ask whether your cleaner brings supplies, add the answer to the relevant service page. If customers misunderstand what an inspection includes, clarify that section. Follow the phone photography guide when the best update is a clearer picture of your work.

Improve useful content without chasing freshness

A page does not become unhelpful just because you wrote it last year. If it accurately explains your service and answers customer questions, keep it and improve specific weaknesses when you find them.

Google's guidance on helpful content warns against changing dates without substantial changes or adding and removing content simply to make a site seem fresh. Treat a published update date as a record of a meaningful review or change, not a decoration.

When you review a page, ask what has become inaccurate, unclear, or incomplete. Perhaps you now service tankless water heaters, use a different booking process, or need to explain access requirements for gutter cleaning. Those are useful reasons to edit.

Avoid rewriting every page at once unless there is a clear need. Small, specific corrections are easier to check. Keep the page address unchanged when the service and purpose remain the same, and ask your provider before removing an established page.

Give upkeep an owner and check completed changes

Decide who notices changes, who requests them, and who checks the result. In a small shop, the same person may do all three jobs, but the responsibility still needs a name.

Send exact instructions when someone else edits the site. "Change our Saturday hours to closed starting next month" leaves room for confusion. Include the effective date, the pages involved, and the final wording you want customers to see.

After the update, open the live page on your phone. Confirm the change appears and that nearby links, buttons, and text still work. Keep a simple list of what changed and what remains to be checked.

Content checks also do not replace technical upkeep. Ask your provider who handles software updates, backups, renewals, and recovery if something breaks. Keep those responsibilities clear even when the public pages rarely need rewriting.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to publish a blog post every week?

You do not need a weekly post just to meet a calendar. Publish when you have a useful answer for your customers, and keep essential service information accurate first.

Should I change the year on every page in January?

Review information that genuinely depends on the year, such as dated offers. Do not change a page's date to imply a substantive update when you have not made one.

What if nothing about my business has changed?

Check the customer journey anyway, especially phone, form, and booking links. If the information and functions remain correct, you can leave the wording alone.

How do I know whether I need updates or a redesign?

Start with the specific problems customers face. Incorrect hours or an old photo usually calls for an edit, while widespread navigation or usability problems may need a broader review.

The short version

Update important facts immediately, check the site monthly, and prepare seasonal changes before customers need them. Add photos and answers when they make the business easier to understand.
