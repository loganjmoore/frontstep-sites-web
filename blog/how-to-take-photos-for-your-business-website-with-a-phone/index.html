Take website photos with just a phone

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

You can take useful business website photos with a phone by cleaning the lens, using steady natural light, and showing real people and finished work. Plan the shots around questions customers ask, keep before-and-after views consistent, and get permission before sharing customer property or faces. Save the originals and check how each photo looks on your website.

You need photos for your business website, but hiring a photographer may not be today's priority. Your phone can show customers who will arrive, what your work looks like, and whether you handle their kind of job. Start with a small set of clear, honest pictures that answer those questions.

Decide what each photo needs to show

Write a short shot list before your next suitable job. Otherwise, you may come home with plenty of pictures of equipment and none that help someone choose your business. Give each photo a job on the website.

  • A friendly team photo helps customers recognize the people coming to their home.
  • A finished project shows the type of work you want more of.
  • A close view explains a detail that separates careful work from a rushed job.
  • A work vehicle or entrance helps customers identify you when they arrive.
  • A simple process photo shows how you protect the property or prepare the work area.

A painter could photograph clean trim lines and protected flooring. An auto detailer could show a cleaned cloth seat in ordinary daylight. A garage door company could show a finished door from the driveway, with enough context to see how it fits the house.

Choose pictures that support the service described on that page. Your About page needs recognizable people, while a project page needs evidence of the work.

Use good light and a steady phone

Wipe the camera lens with a clean, soft cloth. A fingerprint can make a bright window look hazy and soften the whole picture. Check the background before you start, especially for bins, invoices, vehicle plates, and anything unrelated to the job.

For people, try open shade outdoors or a spot facing a window indoors. Avoid placing a person directly in front of a bright window. Move yourself or the subject until their face is easy to see without harsh shadows.

Hold the phone with both hands and pause before pressing the shutter. Tap the main subject on the screen to focus if your camera allows it. Take several frames, then enlarge them to check sharpness before everyone leaves.

For room photos, stand where vertical door frames look straight. Avoid an extremely wide view that makes a small bathroom look huge or bends the edges of cabinetry. For distant details, move closer when safe instead of relying on heavy digital zoom.

Do not climb onto roofs, enter work zones, or distract someone using equipment to get a better angle. Photograph safe, completed work from a stable position.

Capture context, detail, and both orientations

For each project, take a wide shot that explains the setting and a closer shot that shows the result. A photograph of a single tile says little by itself. A view of the finished shower, followed by a close view of the grout lines, tells a clearer story.

Take a horizontal version and a vertical version when practical. A wide image may fit a desktop page, while a taller crop may work better on a phone. Leave some breathing room around the important subject so a website crop does not cut off a person's head or the edge of the installation.

If you want a team photograph, ask people to stand together naturally in clean work clothes. Check that everyone is comfortable having the picture published. You do not need matching poses or a dramatic backdrop.

Think about the question behind a customer's search. Someone looking for cabinet refinishing wants to see the finish and the room. Someone seeking mobile detailing wants to understand the finished interior and what your setup looks like at a customer's location.

Keep before-and-after photos honest

Before you start work, choose a position you can return to later. Note the height of the phone, the direction you face, and a fixed landmark such as a doorway. Use similar framing and lighting for the after photo whenever possible.

A pressure-washed driveway can look darker while wet. Let it dry before using the finished photograph to represent the result. A repainted room can look like a different color under different bulbs, so avoid making a lighting change part of the apparent improvement.

Do not use filters that change paint colors, hide scratches, or smooth away damage. Basic cropping and a modest brightness adjustment can help a photo read clearly, but the result should still look like the job a customer would see in person.

Label the pair plainly. Explain what you actually did, such as cleaning the upholstery or replacing the damaged door panels. Do not suggest the photograph proves a hidden repair or a result outside the scope of that job.

Check permission and private details before posting

Ask permission before publishing customer property, faces, or identifying details. Explain that the pictures would appear on your public business website, and keep a record of the agreement. Respect a refusal without making the customer explain it.

Look closely for house numbers, mail, family photographs, security equipment, computer screens, and paperwork. A neat kitchen can still reveal a school schedule on the refrigerator. Crop out identifying details or choose another picture when there is doubt.

Check whether location information is attached to the image before sharing it. If you do not know how your phone or publishing tool handles that information, ask whoever maintains the website. Avoid publishing customer addresses in captions.

Use photographs you took or have permission to use. Downloading a competitor's project picture does not make it yours. Real, relevant photos support a trustworthy local business website without pretending your company completed someone else's work.

Prepare the pictures for the page

Keep the original files in a clearly labeled folder, then make separate copies for the website. Use understandable names such as the service and project type, without customer names or street addresses. This makes later updates easier.

Ask your website provider to size and compress the copies for their intended placement. A large camera original usually contains more image data than a small project card needs. Check the finished page using a phone on a normal connection; our website loading guide explains what to look for.

Add a short caption when the image needs context. For an informative photo, provide alternative text that describes the relevant subject for someone who cannot see it. Follow these website accessibility basics rather than stuffing a list of towns into the description.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a new phone?

Try the phone you already have before buying anything. Clean the lens, improve the light, and check the actual website-sized result before deciding that equipment is the problem.

Can I use stock photos?

You can use properly licensed stock photos where they genuinely help explain something. Do not present them as your team, your premises, or your completed projects.

Should every job go on the website?

No, choose work that represents the services you want customers to request. A few clear, relevant projects are more useful than a long gallery of nearly identical pictures.

What if the customer does not want photos published?

Respect that choice and use another project. You can photograph your own team, tools, or preparation area without making that customer's property the subject.

The short version

Plan useful shots, find steady light, and show work as it really looks. Keep before-and-after views comparable and check permission before publishing. Review the final crops on your phone, not just in your camera roll.
