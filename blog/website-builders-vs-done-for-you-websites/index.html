Short answer

A done-for-you site starts at $99 a year with the domain included, and a builder starts at about $17 to $19 a month, before your time. A builder fits if you want to make your own changes and can set aside time to write, arrange, and check the pages. A done-for-you website fits if you would rather hand off that work.

Should you build your business website yourself or pay someone to handle it? A website builder is a do-it-yourself tool for making your own pages, and a done-for-you website is a site a provider designs, writes, and maintains for you. A builder makes sense when you have the interest and time to use it; a done-for-you service makes sense when you want help getting the work finished. The important difference is who does the writing, checking, and updating after you choose a design.

Start with what your customers need to do

Write down the main job of your website before looking at templates or asking for quotes. For a mobile mechanic, that might be helping someone describe a car that will not start and confirm whether you travel to their location. For a house painter, it might be showing completed interior work and getting an estimate request.

That gives you a short, useful list of requirements:

  • Explain the services you actually offer.
  • Name the towns or neighborhoods you serve.
  • Show real examples of the work.
  • Make calling or requesting an estimate straightforward.
  • Explain what happens after someone contacts you.

If you also need an unusual booking process or a customer portal, put that in the brief. Ask for a demonstration of any essential feature. Do not assume either a builder or a service includes it because the sample site looks good.

Count the work behind a finished page

With a builder, you still have to decide what each page says. You choose photographs, replace sample text, enter business details, and check the result. Learning the editor is only part of the job.

Try a small exercise before committing. Draft a service page explaining what you do, what you do not do, where you travel, and how an estimate works. Add a real job photo with a useful caption. Our guide to writing website text when you are not a writer can help you get started.

A done-for-you provider can take on agreed parts of that work, but you still supply accurate business information and approve the result. Ask who writes the text and who checks it. You are the person who knows whether your crew installs fence gates, repairs them, or only replaces whole fences.

Do you need HTML to have a website?

No. A small business does not need to write HTML to get a working site. A builder uses menus and an editor in place of code, and a done-for-you provider writes the words and builds the pages for you. For the wider trade-offs, read done-for-you website vs building it yourself.

Decide how you want changes to happen

The strongest reason to choose DIY may be control over everyday edits. If you enjoy updating project photos and changing descriptions, doing it yourself can feel simpler than explaining the request to someone else. You still need to check the published page after each change.

A managed service can suit an owner who wants to send a clear request and get back to the job. Before choosing one, ask about request limits, turnaround, and what counts as a change. A new phone number and a new service section may involve different amounts of work.

Think about your actual calendar. A lawn care company might add spring cleanup details, remove a fully booked service, and update holiday hours. Use the guide to how often to update a business website to turn those needs into a realistic maintenance list.

Test the customer experience either way

Do not judge a site only by its desktop preview. Open it on your phone and pretend you have never heard of the business. Can you understand the service area, read the main information, and find the right contact method without hunting?

Tap the phone number. Send a clearly labeled test inquiry and confirm it arrives where someone will see it. Check that a customer asking about a leaking skylight does not have to guess whether your roofing business handles repairs or only replacements.

Our guide to building a small business website for phones first explains the practical checks. Apply them to your own build or to a provider's draft. Neither a paid designer nor a familiar builder name excuses a broken form or an unreadable page.

Check control and the exit before signing

Keep a record of who manages the domain, the website account, and any business email. Ask what happens when the relationship ends. Moving a domain and moving a complete website are separate jobs, so get separate answers.

For a managed service, ask which files or content you can take and what help, if any, is available. For a DIY platform, check what can move elsewhere before assuming a future switch will copy every page and feature. Keep your original photographs and business text in your own records.

Frequently asked questions

Is building my own website always cheaper?

No, because the subscription is only one part of your decision. You may spend time creating content or pay someone for tasks you cannot finish yourself. Compare your likely charges and the work involved without treating your own time as either worthless or automatically expensive.

Can a done-for-you website still sound like my business?

Yes, if you provide specific information and review the writing. Give the provider examples of questions customers ask, services you turn down, and how you explain estimates. Ask them to replace vague claims with those details before you approve the pages.

Should I hire someone if I already started a DIY site?

First list what is unfinished and what is stopping you. You may only need help with photographs, writing, or checking the contact form. Compare that limited help with a complete service instead of discarding useful work automatically.

Which option is better for getting local customers?

Neither approach guarantees customers. A useful site needs accurate service information, clear contact details, and someone who responds to inquiries. Choose the approach that helps you publish and maintain those essentials reliably.
