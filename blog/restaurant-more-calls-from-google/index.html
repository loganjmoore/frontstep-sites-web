Restaurant: get more calls from Google

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Restaurants can encourage more useful calls from Google by keeping hours, menus, phone numbers, and booking instructions accurate. Show the food and dining experience you actually offer, request honest feedback, and make pickup, reservations, and group inquiries easy to distinguish. Track the requests that become visits or orders, while reducing calls caused by missing information.

How do you get more useful restaurant calls from Google while reducing questions the website should already answer? Make it easy to check the menu, confirm today's service, and choose the right next step. A diner booking an anniversary meal needs different information from someone ordering sandwiches for an office lunch.

Decide which calls you actually want

More calls are not automatically better during a busy service. A ringing phone that only answers "Are you open?" can interrupt the host without creating a reservation. Aim for useful inquiries and make routine facts easy to find.

Decide what matters to your restaurant: table bookings, takeout orders, catering requests, private dining, or another service you actually provide. Keep those paths separate enough that customers understand what to do.

Someone searching "pizza takeout near me" should find pickup instructions and the current menu. Someone searching "restaurant for a birthday dinner" needs group arrangements and a way to discuss the occasion. Do not make both people start with a generic contact form.

Check the business profile against this week's service

Review your Google Business Profile for the phone number, website, address, and current opening hours. Check upcoming closures and special schedules before a holiday or private event. Make sure the public information agrees with what the host would tell a caller.

Be precise on your website when dining room hours, kitchen service, and pickup times differ. A bar that remains open later than the kitchen should not leave hungry visitors guessing. Avoid using one vague closing time to explain several different services.

Choose business categories that reflect the restaurant you operate. Keep descriptions focused on the cuisine and experience customers can expect. Use Google's local business guidance as a reference for maintaining complete, current information.

Give diners a current menu they can read

Publish the menu as readable website text, with current prices and clear sections. A photograph of a paper menu can be difficult to enlarge and navigate on a phone. If you also offer a download, keep the main information available on the page.

Separate lunch, dinner, takeout, or catering menus when they differ. Explain when each is available. Do not let a visitor assume a weekday lunch special is offered during Saturday dinner service.

Mark sold-out or changing items in a way your team can maintain. If an item depends on daily availability, say so. Avoid leaving an old promotional menu as the first result a customer reaches from your site.

Describe dishes in language that helps a diner choose. For dietary or allergy questions, direct customers to a staff conversation and describe only options and procedures the restaurant can accurately support. Avoid broad assurances that overlook kitchen conditions.

Make reservations, pickup, and group inquiries distinct

Test every main button from a phone. "Reserve a table" should lead to your actual reservation process. "Order pickup" should lead to the correct location and ordering path. If you take orders only by phone, make that clear before someone spends time searching for a checkout.

Explain how larger parties are handled. If they need to contact the restaurant directly, state what information to provide, such as date, approximate group size, and preferred meal period. A group inquiry should not appear to confirm a reservation.

For catering, collect the event date, general location, guest count, and service needs. Do not make the host handle a detailed catering discussion in the middle of seating dinner guests if another person manages those inquiries.

Show practical arrival information too. An entrance behind the building, shared parking, or a pickup counter separate from the dining room can change whether a customer reaches the right place without calling.

Show the real food and invite real feedback

Use current photographs of dishes you actually serve. A clear picture of the dining room helps customers judge whether the setting fits a quick family meal or a quieter dinner. Avoid stock photos that create the wrong expectation about portions, decor, or seating.

Get permission before featuring identifiable diners. Check backgrounds for private events or people who did not agree to appear in marketing. Keep photo sessions from disrupting service.

Invite honest reviews after a genuine visit without offering a discount or choosing only customers who already praised the meal. A brief request on an appropriate follow-up or receipt can be enough. Let diners choose their own words.

Google's review guidance explains review links and the prohibition on incentives. Reply to feedback calmly and keep private booking details out of public responses.

Measure visits and orders, not just ringing phones

Ask staff to note recurring questions and useful inquiry types. You may discover that most calls concern an outdated menu, parking, or whether reservations are accepted. Fixing those gaps can reduce unnecessary calls while making the restaurant easier to choose.

Track whether group inquiries receive follow-up, whether booking links work, and whether pickup customers reach the intended ordering route. Test after any change to your menu, phone system, or booking provider.

If you want to review search visibility more broadly, use the restaurant local SEO checklist. Keep expectations realistic: a listing update or new menu page does not guarantee a ranking or a full dining room.

Choose improvements based on the actual customer journey. If people find you but cannot tell how to reserve, fix that step before writing another article about your restaurant.

Frequently asked questions

Is a PDF menu enough?

A PDF can be an extra option, but put the current menu in readable website text too. Diners should be able to find dishes and prices without downloading or pinching a large document. Keep both versions consistent if you maintain both.

Should every visitor be told to call?

No, send people to the appropriate booking, ordering, or inquiry route. Keep calling available when a conversation is useful. Clear self-service information can reduce interruptions while helping customers complete their plans.

Do food photos need professional equipment?

No, a well-lit, accurate photo of the dish can be useful. Show food as customers receive it and avoid heavy editing that changes the appearance. Update pictures when a featured dish leaves the menu.
