Landscaping website mistakes

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Landscaping websites lose useful inquiries when customers cannot tell what services you offer, where you work, or how to request a quote. Separate recurring lawn care from larger projects, show real work with context, and explain pricing clearly. Test the site on a phone and follow every inquiry through to the receiving inbox.

Your landscaping website looks respectable, but callers keep asking whether you mow lawns or only build patios. Others request work far outside your routes. Which mistakes should you fix first? Start with the information that helps a customer recognize the right service and make a useful inquiry.

Mixing lawn maintenance and landscape projects together

"Full-service landscaping" can mean different things to different homeowners. One may expect weekly mowing and edging. Another may assume you design planting beds, build retaining walls, install irrigation, or remove trees.

Name the services your team actually performs. If you offer recurring lawn care and larger installation projects, give them distinct sections or pages. A lawn-care customer needs route coverage and service scope; a design customer wants to understand consultation, planning, and installation.

Be clear about exclusions too. If tree removal or irrigation work goes to a separate contractor, do not present it as an in-house service. If you only maintain properties you installed, explain that before inviting general maintenance inquiries.

Rewrite vague headings using the customer's task. "Weekly mowing and lawn maintenance" or "Planting design and installation" helps more than "Outdoor solutions." The landscaping website guide can help organize the pages without making the navigation complicated.

Showing attractive pictures without explaining the work

A large image of a beautiful garden does not tell the customer whether you designed it, installed it, or mow the lawn beside it. Stock photographs can create expectations that have little connection to your actual work. Use real projects where you have permission to share them.

Add short captions describing your role and the customer's request. A mulch refresh, a new planting bed, and a patio installation are different services, even if each produces a pleasing photograph. Tell the reader what happened.

Before-and-after images need fair presentation. Show comparable views and explain the stage of the project. Do not pass off a newly installed bed as a mature garden or use unrelated properties as a pair.

Include ordinary work too. A tidy recurring maintenance visit can matter to someone who wants reliable lawn care. The guide to taking business photos with a phone helps you gather useful evidence without waiting for a professional shoot.

Hiding the route area and scheduling limits

A general statement such as "serving the whole region" may bring inquiries that are impractical for your crew. Weekly lawn care especially depends on where the property sits in relation to your routes. Give customers a clear coverage list before they complete the form.

If you consider larger installations beyond the routine mowing area, explain the difference. Do not show a broad map that implies every service is available everywhere. You can say that some projects are assessed individually without promising that you will take them.

Keep seasonal availability accurate. A spring cleanup page should not silently advertise openings from last year. If you are taking inquiries for a later period, say that plainly and avoid displaying an outdated appointment promise.

Use one clear coverage page as the starting point. Separate town pages need useful information beyond replacing the place name. A genuine route distinction or project example can help, while repeated generic paragraphs simply add more text to maintain.

Saying nothing useful about pricing

You do not have to publish one price for every lawn or landscape project. You do need to explain how customers get a quote and what information affects it. Silence can leave people assuming that an ordinary maintenance inquiry requires a lengthy paid design process.

For recurring care, explain whether you assess the property before quoting and what the quoted service includes. Details such as edging, clippings, leaf removal, and visit frequency should reflect your actual offer. Do not let a low headline price imply that every possible task is included.

For installations, explain the consultation and proposal process. Site access, preparation, materials, drainage considerations, and scope can affect the work, but you do not need to invent sample project prices to make that point.

If you publish a starting price, state the applicable scope and limitations clearly. The guide to showing prices on a website helps you decide what gives useful clarity without pretending every property is the same.

Making a quote request feel like homework

A first inquiry should help you establish whether the job fits. It should not require a homeowner to measure every bed or identify each plant before they can reach you. Ask for details you will actually use at this stage.

A practical form might request:

  • Name and preferred contact details.
  • Property location or town, depending on your quoting process.
  • The service wanted, such as mowing, cleanup, or installation.
  • A brief description and requested timing.
  • Optional photos if they help and the form supports them well.

Make the phone number visible for customers who prefer to talk. Test the form on a small screen and check that labels remain readable. Do not make a customer create an account just to ask whether you serve the street.

After submission, explain what happens next. "Your request has been received" is different from "your visit is booked." The contact form guide covers how to keep that first step manageable.

Letting presentation get in the way of contact

A slideshow of gardens may look appealing on a large office screen but delay the useful information on a phone. Resize images, keep text readable, and make the call or quote option easy to find. Review the site on a mobile connection as well as your office Wi-Fi.

Remove expired promotions and outdated service claims. A winter service banner left up through summer can make customers wonder whether anyone maintains the business information. Staff photos and descriptions should also match your current team and capabilities.

Then follow an inquiry all the way through. Submit a test request and confirm that it reaches the right person, receives an appropriate reply, and does not disappear into an old employee's inbox. A functioning button is only the beginning of that process.

Use a short monthly review to look at unsuitable requests and unanswered questions. If several homeowners expect irrigation repair you do not offer, correct the wording. If mowing customers cannot find your routes, move the coverage information where they can see it before the form.

Frequently asked questions

Should I remove all stock photos?

Prioritize real work wherever an image is meant to demonstrate your services or results. Do not imply that a stock garden is a project you completed. If you use a general decorative image, keep the distinction clear and avoid letting it replace actual evidence.

Is a long list of services good for the homepage?

Only if it remains clear and accurately reflects your work. Group related services and provide useful detail on separate pages where needed. A long list of loosely related jobs can attract inquiries your crew cannot support.

What should I fix if I only have time for one change?

Choose the biggest obstacle in the current customer path. That may be a broken form, missing phone number, or unclear service area. Test those basics before spending time on decorative changes.

The short version

Make services, coverage, pricing expectations, and contact options clear before adding more visual decoration.
