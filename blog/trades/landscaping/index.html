  • Landscaping: what your website needs

    A landscaping website should distinguish recurring lawn care from one-time projects and show the areas your crews actually serve. Explain each service, display real work with useful captions, and collect enough property information to start an estimate. Make seasonal availability and the next step clear so suitable customers can contact you without guessing.

  • Landscaping: what a website costs in 2026

    A landscaping website can cost $99 a year with a done-for-you service, while custom work often costs a few thousand dollars or more. The right scope depends on whether you sell recurring lawn care, larger landscape projects, or both. Compare ongoing updates and ownership as well as the initial price.

  • Landscaping: get more calls from Google

    Landscapers can make Google inquiries more useful by keeping their Business Profile accurate, explaining services and coverage on their website, and requesting honest customer reviews. Separate recurring lawn care from one-time landscape projects so people know what to request. Then test the call and form process, and track whether inquiries match the work and routes you can serve.

  • Landscaping Google Business Profile guide

    A landscaper Google Business Profile should show the work you actually offer, the places your crew serves, and how customers request an estimate. Choose categories that fit your main business, separate recurring care from projects, and keep seasonal details current. Use real photos and honest reviews to help people understand your work before they contact you.

  • Local SEO checklist for landscapers

    Landscaping local SEO starts with accurate business details, clear service pages, and a service area that matches your real routes. Support those basics with genuine project photos, honest reviews, and a website that works on phones. Check that search visitors can request the right service, then use actual enquiries to decide what needs improvement.

  • Landscaping website mistakes

    Landscaping websites lose useful inquiries when customers cannot tell what services you offer, where you work, or how to request a quote. Separate recurring lawn care from larger projects, show real work with context, and explain pricing clearly. Test the site on a phone and follow every inquiry through to the receiving inbox.

  • What to write on a landscaper homepage

    A landscaper homepage should name the work you do, the places you serve, and how customers request an estimate. Separate recurring lawn care from one-time projects, explain what each service includes, and show real examples. Use plain sample lines only as starting points, replacing every service, location, and promise with details that match your business.

  • Landscaping: get more Google reviews

    Ask landscaping customers for an honest Google review after a meaningful service milestone, such as a cleanup, project walkthrough, or established mowing routine. Make the request short, optional, and easy to follow. Ask consistently, never reward reviews or filter customers by satisfaction, and respond to concerns without sharing private property details.

  • Landscaping: Facebook page vs. a website

    A Facebook page can help a landscaper share recent work and keep in touch with local customers, but it is a limited home for detailed service information. A website gives you a stable place to explain routes, project scope, and estimates. Use Facebook for timely updates and point interested customers to clear pages for the work they need.

  • Landscaping blog post ideas

    Useful landscaping blog posts answer questions customers ask before booking lawn care, cleanups, planting work, or larger projects. Start with your actual services and local conditions you can verify. Use real job notes and appropriate local sources, explain the next step, and avoid invented results, rigid seasonal dates, or unsupported plant advice.
