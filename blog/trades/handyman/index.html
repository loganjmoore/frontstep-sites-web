  • Handyman: what your website needs

    A handyman website needs a specific job list, your service area, real project photos, and a straightforward estimate request. Explain minimum charges, materials, and how you assess the work. Help customers describe the repair and send useful pictures, while clearly stating which jobs require a different contractor or specialist.

  • Handyman: what a website costs in 2026

    A handyman website can start at $99 a year with a done-for-you service. Typical custom freelancer or agency work can range from a few thousand dollars to much more, depending on the scope. Prioritize a clear list of accepted jobs, your working area, real project photos, and a practical estimate request before paying for complicated features.

  • How handymen get more calls from Google

    To get more useful handyman calls from Google, describe the small repairs you actually accept, keep your business details current, and make it easy to send a job list. Show completed work with permission and request honest reviews. Explain your service area, minimum visit policy, and work limits so inquiries fit your skills and schedule.

  • Handyman: Google Business Profile guide

    A handyman's Google Business Profile should show the small repairs and installation jobs you actually accept, the area you serve, and how customers request an estimate. Use accurate business details, clear project photos, and realistic hours. Explain your limits so people can tell whether their punch list fits your work before they call.

  • Local SEO checklist for handymen

    Improve handyman local SEO by making your business details consistent and describing the repairs you actually offer in the towns you serve. Support those details with real project photos, honest reviews, and working contact links. Prioritize clear service pages and useful inquiries before creating more location pages or chasing broad search terms.

  • Handyman website mistakes that cost calls

    A handyman website should show the repairs you accept, where you work, how estimates happen, and how to contact you. Vague service claims, missing visit terms, misleading project photos, and long forms make that harder. Start by helping a homeowner describe an ordinary small job without needing to understand construction terminology.

  • What to write on a handyman homepage

    A handyman homepage should name the repairs you handle, the places you serve, and the next step for requesting work. Explain visit minimums, materials, and job limits before customers contact you. Use real project examples and invite a short task list with photos so you can judge the work without promising a quote from incomplete information.

  • Handyman: how to get more Google reviews

    Ask for an honest Google review after walking the customer through the completed handyman work and explaining any remaining tasks. Use the same optional invitation across your completed jobs, provide a direct review link, and keep follow-up brief. Never exchange discounts or repairs for a review, and do not screen customers by expected rating.

  • Is a Facebook page enough for a handyman?

    Facebook can be enough for a handyman whose referrals already fill the schedule and whose services are easy to explain. A website helps when customers need to check job scope, service areas, visit minimums, and estimate requirements before contacting you. Use the two together if Facebook starts conversations but leaves you repeating the same details.

  • Handyman: blog post ideas for customers

    Useful handyman blog posts answer questions homeowners ask before hiring: whether you take a small job, what photos to send, who supplies materials, and how to prepare for a visit. Focus on services you actually provide. Explain the decision and your process without turning the article into unsafe repair instructions or a promise about unseen damage.
