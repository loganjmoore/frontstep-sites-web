  • Restaurant: what your website needs

    A restaurant website needs a readable current menu, accurate kitchen hours, directions, and clear actions for ordering, reserving, or calling. Make the main choices easy on a phone. Explain pickup, large-party, and dietary inquiry arrangements so diners know whether your restaurant fits their plans before they arrive or contact you.

  • Restaurant: what a website costs in 2026

    A restaurant website can start at $99 a year with a done-for-you service, while typical custom freelancer or agency projects can range from a few thousand dollars to much more. Cost depends heavily on menu upkeep and whether you link to existing ordering and reservation tools or commission more complicated connections.

  • Restaurant: get more calls from Google

    Restaurants can encourage more useful calls from Google by keeping hours, menus, phone numbers, and booking instructions accurate. Show the food and dining experience you actually offer, request honest feedback, and make pickup, reservations, and group inquiries easy to distinguish. Track the requests that become visits or orders, while reducing calls caused by missing information.

  • Restaurant: Google Business Profile guide

    A restaurant's Google Business Profile should help a guest decide what to eat, when to visit, and how to order or reserve. Keep your location, hours, menu, photos, and action links accurate. Check the public listing after changes so a lunch guest does not find an old menu or follow a link to the wrong location.

  • Local SEO checklist for restaurants

    Start restaurant local SEO with accurate business details, current menus, clear dining hours, and working links for orders or reservations. Keep your website and local listings consistent, use real food and room photos, and invite honest reviews. Then check the site's technical basics and whether visitors can complete the dining task they came for.

  • Restaurant website mistakes

    Restaurant websites frustrate guests when menus are hard to read, hours conflict, ordering buttons lead to the wrong place, or practical visit details are missing. Put a current menu, location, hours, and the main guest actions within easy reach. Test each path on a phone using the same information your staff gives customers.

  • What to write on a restaurant homepage

    Your restaurant homepage should tell diners what you serve, where you are, when they can eat, and how to book or order. Put the menu and practical next steps ahead of a long origin story. Describe the real dining experience, explain group arrangements, and keep hours, prices, and booking instructions consistent with daily service.

  • Restaurant: get more Google reviews

    Invite restaurant guests to leave an honest Google review after they have experienced the meal and service. Use a small receipt message, a review link, or a brief invitation that does not pressure diners. Include dine-in and takeout guests fairly, avoid rewards or rating requests, and respond to feedback without exposing private booking or order details.

  • Restaurant: Facebook page vs. a website

    Facebook can work well for restaurant updates, specials, and conversations with regulars. A website is useful when diners need a current menu, accurate kitchen hours, directions, and a clear order or reservation route in one place. Use Facebook for timely news and the website for the details people rely on when planning a meal.

  • Restaurant: blog post ideas for customers

    Restaurant blog posts work best when they help guests plan a meal or occasion: choosing lunch, arranging pickup, visiting with a group, or requesting catering. Use your actual menu, policies, and local knowledge. Connect each article to current practical information rather than publishing generic food lists or promising more reservations.
