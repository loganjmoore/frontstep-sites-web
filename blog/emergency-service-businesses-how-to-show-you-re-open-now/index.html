Emergency businesses: show you're open

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Show emergency availability by stating your actual service hours, the urgent jobs you handle, and the areas you cover beside an easy-to-use phone number. Keep your website and business listings consistent. Only claim round-the-clock service if you provide it, and explain whether an after-hours call reaches dispatch, an on-call technician, or voicemail.

When someone searches for an emergency plumber or a garage door company open now, they need to know whether you can help tonight. A large phone number is useful, but it does not explain who answers or whether a technician can attend. Put those answers near the top of the page and make sure they match what happens when someone calls.

Define what open means for your business

Separate office hours, phone coverage, and field service. These may be the same, but an answering service taking messages overnight is different from a crew available to leave for a job. Customers should not have to call to discover that distinction.

Choose wording that matches your operation. These are examples to adapt only if they are true:

  • "Emergency plumbing calls answered day and night. Call for current technician availability."
  • "Office open weekdays. Existing customers can reach the on-call HVAC technician after hours."
  • "Garage door emergency appointments available evenings. Call to confirm service in your town."
  • "Messages received overnight are returned when the office opens."

Avoid a permanent "available now" label if nobody checks whether you are available. A busy crew, weather closure, or unanswered phone can make that label misleading. A clear schedule and a call to confirm availability are easier to keep accurate than a status badge that never changes.

Name the urgent problems you actually handle

A customer with water spreading across the floor does not need a long list of every plumbing service. State the urgent work you accept, such as active supply leaks, blocked drains, or failed water heaters, along with any important limits. Do not imply every problem can be fully repaired on the first visit.

An HVAC company should distinguish no-heat calls from routine tune-ups. If you handle gas furnaces but not oil systems, say so. If emergency visits are limited to existing maintenance customers, put that condition beside the emergency number.

A garage door company might help when a broken spring leaves a vehicle trapped or a door will not close. Explain whether you offer urgent repair appointments or only make the opening secure until parts arrive. Use language customers recognize, including "garage door stuck open," where it fits naturally.

Keep your emergency service page focused on the work. Our guide to writing a useful service page covers how to explain the scope without burying the call button.

Make the phone call easy and explain what comes next

Place a working call button near the main availability statement. Display the number as readable text too, so someone using a laptop can dial it from another phone. Repeat it where a customer finishes reading about the urgent service.

Test the button from an actual phone. Check that it opens the expected number and reaches the correct business line. A button labeled "Emergency help" that opens a general contact form adds uncertainty when someone needs an answer quickly.

Beside the number, tell callers what information will help you decide whether you can attend. For example, ask for their town, the problem, and whether access to the property is available. Keep sensitive access codes out of public forms.

If you use a form for nonurgent requests, label it clearly. State when those messages are reviewed using a schedule you can meet. The click-to-call guide explains how to make the phone route clear across the rest of the site.

Keep business listings and website hours consistent

Google Business Profile lets you maintain regular hours and exceptions for dates such as holidays. Review the public result after updating it, and use Google's guidance on editing business hours if you need help. Set the hours that accurately represent your business and follow Google's applicable guidelines.

Do not mark the business open around the clock just because a form accepts messages at any time. If the business has different office and emergency arrangements, explain those distinctions on the website and avoid contradictory statements across listings.

Check the homepage, contact page, emergency service page, and footer. Then check the business profiles customers use to find you. A footer copied from last year's site can undermine the careful wording at the top of the new page.

Keep one short reference document with your regular hours, holiday arrangements, phone numbers, and approved availability wording. Use it whenever a staff member or website provider makes a change. This also helps with keeping business information consistent.

Explain coverage, fees, and the limits of arrival estimates

List the towns or area where you accept urgent calls. If overnight coverage is narrower than daytime coverage, describe that difference explicitly. Someone outside your emergency area should be able to recognize it before waiting for a callback.

Explain whether an after-hours visit has a separate charge and when the customer will learn the amount. You do not have to publish a fixed repair price when the cause is unknown. You can explain that the visit charge and approval process are discussed before dispatch, if that is your actual practice.

Avoid promising a fixed arrival time across a large territory unless you can consistently honor it. Traffic, ongoing jobs, and parts availability can affect the visit. Give the customer a current estimate during the call and explain how you will notify them if it changes.

For immediate danger, make clear that your repair line does not replace emergency services. Keep any such notice short and relevant, without turning the page into repair instructions for a hazardous situation.

Test the whole after-hours experience

Arrange a test with the person responsible for the phone rather than surprising a busy on-call technician. Open the website outside normal office hours and follow the same steps a customer would. Check the listing, read the page, and place the call.

Listen to the greeting and voicemail message. If the website says someone answers overnight but the recording promises a callback next business day, decide which statement is accurate and fix the mismatch. Make sure staff know what the website promises.

Repeat the check when staffing, holiday hours, or your service area changes. Put an end date on temporary notices and assign someone to remove them. Use the website update schedule to keep these checks part of normal operations.

Frequently asked questions

Can I say 24/7 if calls go to an answering service?

You can describe round-the-clock call answering if that is true, but explain what happens after the message is taken. Do not let that wording imply round-the-clock technician availability when you do not provide it.

Should I use a separate emergency phone number?

Use one only if it has a clear purpose and dependable coverage. Test both numbers and make the difference obvious so callers do not have to guess which line is monitored.

Do I need a live open-now indicator?

No, accurate hours and a clear call instruction can answer the customer's question. A live indicator needs reliable information about the relevant schedule and any exceptions to be useful.

What if every technician is already on a job?

Tell callers the current situation and give only an arrival estimate you can support. If the shortage continues, update any website notice that suggests immediate attendance.

The short version

State when you answer, when you can send help, and which urgent jobs you cover. Keep those facts consistent across the website, listings, and phone greeting. Test the call path before a customer needs it in a hurry.
