What to write on a tree service homepage

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A tree service homepage should state your main services, coverage area, and real availability, then explain how you assess and quote work. Separate pruning, removals, stump grinding, and storm requests. Show relevant projects and accurate credentials, describe cleanup clearly, and give customers a direct way to call or arrange an assessment without promising results you have not evaluated.

What should your tree service homepage say to help the right customer contact you? It should explain what your crew does and how you decide what a job involves. A homeowner planning routine pruning should not have to navigate a page written entirely for storm emergencies, and an urgent caller should not have to search through a long company story.

Lead with services, location, and honest availability

Start with a heading such as "Tree pruning, removals, and stump grinding in Oak Valley." This is example wording to adapt, not a list every company should claim. Use your actual area and remove any service you do not provide.

Follow with a plain explanation: "Tell us about the tree work you need, and we'll arrange the right next step for an assessment." If your first step is always a phone conversation, say that. If you charge for an assessment, make the charge clear before booking it.

Put a visible phone number near the opening. Add a separate "Request an assessment" option for planned work. Neither button should imply that submitting a form reserves a crew or confirms a same-day visit.

Describe urgent contact accurately. "Call to discuss storm damage during our operating hours" is more useful than an unsupported promise of immediate response. The tree service website guide explains how these opening details fit into the full site.

Explain each service without making a diagnosis online

Use short service sections that help customers recognize their request. Avoid describing every job as "tree care" and leaving the visitor to work out what that includes.

Example lines to adapt include:

  • Pruning: "An assessment of your goals and the tree before we agree on the pruning scope."
  • Tree removal: "Removal planning based on the tree, access, nearby structures, and agreed cleanup."
  • Stump grinding: "A separate quote for stump work when it is not included in your removal proposal."
  • Storm-related work: "Call to discuss the situation and our current availability."

Keep the descriptions true to your own operation. If another company handles stump grinding, explain who quotes, schedules, and completes that part. Customers should not discover the arrangement only after the tree is gone.

Do not tell visitors that a tree is safe, diseased, or ready for removal based on a generic symptom list. Your homepage can explain your assessment service without pretending to evaluate an individual tree remotely.

Link to detailed service pages when you have them. Keep the homepage focused on choosing the right next conversation.

Describe what affects the assessment and quote

Tree work can be difficult for a homeowner to describe. Make it easier by explaining what you will discuss instead of requiring them to know the method or equipment needed.

A useful paragraph might read: "At the assessment, we review the requested work, access to the tree, nearby structures, and the cleanup you want included. We explain the proposed scope before you decide whether to proceed." Use this only if it matches your process.

Ask about practical details such as gates, parking, neighboring property access, and whether the owner can attend the visit. Explain that these details help plan the job. Avoid presenting a simple height estimate as enough information for every quote.

If permissions or local requirements may affect the project, keep the website guidance general. Tell customers to check with the relevant local authority and explain who is responsible for confirming requirements in your process. Do not publish blanket legal claims that apply to every tree or property.

Make cleanup and property care concrete

"We leave everything spotless" sounds reassuring but can mean different things to different people. Describe the cleanup options in your proposal and on the page. Branch removal, log removal, leaving firewood, stump grinding, and handling grindings are separate scope questions.

An example line is: "Your quote states what happens to brush, logs, and any stump grindings, so you know what will remain on the property." That is a practical promise about communication when it matches your actual quote process.

Explain how you discuss access and site protection before work. Do not guarantee that heavy equipment can cross every yard without any effect. Tell customers that access and ground conditions are part of planning rather than hiding the subject behind a broad reassurance.

Include the customer's preparation responsibilities. If vehicles, pets, or outdoor furniture need to be kept away from the work area, explain when you provide those instructions. Keep the homepage focused on arrangements, not instructions for customers to perform hazardous work themselves.

Use evidence that matches the claims you make

Choose real project photos with permission. A caption can identify the service and a meaningful constraint, such as a removal with limited backyard access. Do not turn one unusual job into a claim that every project needs the same equipment.

Show the finished area as well as the tree work when it helps demonstrate the agreed cleanup. A potential customer may be as interested in the yard afterward as in a photograph of the crew at work.

State credentials precisely. Identify who holds a certification when that distinction matters, and check that it is current. Describe insurance only in wording you can support. Avoid using stock badges or logos to imply approvals you have not received.

Use genuine reviews with faithful wording and appropriate permission or attribution. Do not invent a customer quote about safety or professionalism. The tree service review guide can help you build a real feedback routine instead.

End with a request customers can actually complete

Finish with a short invitation: "Tell us your town, the work you are considering, and the best way to reach you." A form can collect those details without demanding an exact tree height, species, or equipment plan.

If you accept pictures, explain that they are optional and should be taken only from a safe position. An assessment may still be needed. For immediate danger or suspected utility involvement, direct visitors to emergency services or the utility as appropriate rather than relying on your form.

Test the page on a phone. Can a visitor find the contact route before the photo gallery loads? Does the form clearly say that it is a request? Does the message reach the person responsible for follow-up?

The contact form guide helps you keep the questions useful. Read the page aloud after editing and remove any sentence that sounds more impressive than informative.

Frequently asked questions

Should I say that every crew member is an arborist?

Only use titles and credentials that accurately describe the people doing the work. If a credential belongs to a particular person, name that person and their role appropriately. Do not broaden one person's qualification into a company-wide claim.

Should the homepage include a tree removal price list?

Only if you can define the scope behind the prices accurately. Otherwise, explain the factors assessed and how customers receive a quote. Avoid a low headline figure that does not include the cleanup people expect.

How much emergency wording belongs on the page?

Enough to make your actual contact arrangements clear. Keep planned services visible too, and do not promise constant availability unless you provide it. Update any temporary storm-response message when capacity changes.
