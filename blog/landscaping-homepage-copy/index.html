What to write on a landscaper homepage

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A landscaper homepage should name the work you do, the places you serve, and how customers request an estimate. Separate recurring lawn care from one-time projects, explain what each service includes, and show real examples. Use plain sample lines only as starting points, replacing every service, location, and promise with details that match your business.

What should you write on your landscaping homepage so visitors understand what they can hire you for? Begin with the services and service area, then explain the estimate process and show examples of your work. A homeowner should not have to decode "outdoor living excellence" to find out whether you mow lawns or install planting beds.

Write an opening that identifies the job and place

Your first heading should make the business easy to recognize. Use your main service and actual location rather than a slogan that could belong to any outdoor company. Keep the opening short enough to read on a phone.

For a lawn care business, a sample heading to adapt is: "Recurring lawn care in your town." A supporting line could say: "Mowing, edging, and seasonal cleanups for local homes. Contact us to check route availability and request an estimate."

For an installation business, try a different direction: "Planting beds and landscape updates for homes in your area." Follow with the actual work you offer, such as bed preparation, planting, and mulching. Do not use either example unchanged if it describes services you do not provide.

Choose one clear main action, such as "Request a lawn care estimate" or "Discuss a landscape project." Keep the phone number visible for people who prefer to call. The landscaper website essentials guide explains how these details fit into the wider site.

Give recurring care and project work separate explanations

If you offer both, introduce them as different customer needs. A weekly mowing customer wants to know about routine visits and route coverage. A landscape project customer wants to discuss goals, scope, and a proposal.

Sample lawn care copy could read: "Ask about regular mowing and edging for your property. We will discuss the lawn, access, visit schedule, and any additional work before confirming the service." Change the tasks and process to match your own.

For projects, a sample is: "Tell us which part of the yard you want to improve. We will review the site, discuss the planting or bed work involved, and prepare a scope for you to consider."

List only meaningful services, with brief explanations where needed:

  • Mowing and edging, with the normal visit scope stated.
  • Seasonal cleanups, including what is collected or removed.
  • Mulching and bed care, with preparation explained.
  • Shrub trimming, where it is part of your work.
  • New planting or sod installation, if offered.

Do not add irrigation, retaining walls, or tree work just to make the list look complete. A specific, truthful service list helps people decide whether you are the right call.

Explain the estimate without inventing a starting price

Visitors want to know what happens after they contact you. A short process section can remove uncertainty even when every property needs its own quote. Describe the actual steps your business follows.

For example: "Send your town, contact details, and a short description of the work. We will confirm whether we cover your area and discuss whether a site visit is needed. You will receive the proposed scope and price before agreeing to the work."

Use that wording only if it matches your process. If estimates have a fee, say so. If submitting a form does not book a visit, make that clear near the form and in the confirmation.

Explain relevant cost factors in everyday language. For recurring mowing, property size, access, terrain, and the agreed tasks may affect the quote. For a planting project, the preparation, materials, and completed scope need discussion.

Do not publish a low starting price unless it represents a real service you can describe and keep current. The guide to showing prices on your website helps you decide what useful pricing information you can offer.

Show finished work with captions that explain it

Choose a few examples that represent the jobs you want to book. A maintained lawn, refreshed front bed, and larger planting installation answer different questions. Avoid filling the page with impressive gardens your team did not create.

Pair each photo with a plain caption. A sample structure is: "Front bed cleanup and fresh mulch in the general service area. The project included the agreed removal, bed preparation, and finished mulch." Replace the details with the actual work.

For before-and-after images, describe what changed without implying that every result will look identical. Plant appearance can change with time and conditions. Do not invent claims about water savings, property value, or guaranteed plant survival.

Get permission to publish customer property and keep private details out of the frame. Use an honest account of the job instead of an invented testimonial. When you have a real review to display, preserve its meaning and identify the source.

Keep image files small enough for comfortable phone browsing. The pictures should support the service description while leaving contact options easy to reach.

Make service boundaries and expectations easy to find

Name the towns or neighborhoods you serve. If recurring routes cover a smaller area than one-time projects, explain the difference. This can save a homeowner from requesting a service you cannot schedule where they live.

Sample wording could be: "Our regular lawn care routes cover the listed neighborhoods. For planting and cleanup projects elsewhere in the county, contact us to discuss the location and scope." Use actual coverage in place of that general example.

Answer practical questions about access and communication. Customers may need to discuss gates, pets, parking, or how you notify them about weather-related schedule changes. State your actual process without promising a fixed weather outcome.

Keep office hours and response expectations honest. If you reply between jobs or during set office hours, say so. Avoid presenting a request as an immediate booking when you still need to review the property and schedule.

Your landscaping Google Business Profile should give the same basic business information. Contradictory hours or service areas undermine otherwise clear copy.

Finish with a small, specific request

End the homepage by inviting the next useful action. A customer planning planting work needs a different prompt from someone joining a mowing route. You can offer both choices without making the form complicated.

A sample closing prompt is: "Tell us your town and whether you need regular lawn care or a one-time project. Add a short description, and we will get back to you about the next step." Set a response expectation your team can meet.

Ask only for the information needed to reply. Name, contact details, location, and job type are a practical starting point. Make photos optional rather than requiring a detailed yard survey from every visitor.

Read the finished homepage aloud and remove sentences that add no information. Then test the phone link and send a form request. Clear copy needs a working contact process behind it.

Frequently asked questions

Should I call the business landscaping or lawn care?

Use the term that best matches your main work, then name the actual services. If you offer both recurring maintenance and installation projects, explain both. Do not rely on a broad label to do all the work.

Can I copy the sample lines exactly?

Use them as drafts and replace the general details with your own facts. Check every promise about services, scheduling, and estimates. Your copy should describe what a customer can actually expect.

How much company history belongs on the homepage?

Include a short introduction if it helps customers understand who they are hiring. Put services, coverage, and contact information first. A longer story can live on an about page.

Should I advertise year-round service?

Only if that accurately describes your business. Explain which services change with the season and how customers check availability. Remove old seasonal messages when they no longer apply.

The short version

Write your landscaper homepage around the job, location, estimate process, and next step. Give lawn care and project work distinct explanations, then support them with genuine photos. Replace vague promises with details your office and crew can deliver.
