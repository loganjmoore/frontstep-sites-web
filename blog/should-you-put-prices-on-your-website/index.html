Should you put prices on your website?

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Yes, put prices on your website when you can explain what the customer gets for that amount. Use fixed prices for clearly defined work, realistic ranges for variable jobs, and an estimate process for work that needs inspection. State the limits beside the price so customers do not mistake a starting rate for a complete quote.

Should you put prices on your website if every job is a little different? Usually, you can share something useful without promising a final bill before you have seen the work. The goal is to help a customer understand the likely commitment and decide whether to call.

Choose a price format that matches the work

Start with how you actually price jobs. A posted figure works best when the service has a clear beginning and end. If you already quote the same scope repeatedly, hiding all pricing may just create another phone call to answer a basic question.

Choose the format service by service:

  • Fixed price: Use this for a defined service with clear inclusions, such as a standard haircut without color or a detailing package for a specified vehicle size.
  • Starting price: Use this when a real, available version of the service has a lower entry price, with identifiable extras.
  • Typical range: Use your own comparable completed jobs, then explain what moves a customer toward either end.
  • Inspection or estimate first: Use this when access, condition, measurements, or diagnosis substantially change the work.

You do not need one pricing rule for the whole business. An HVAC company might publish its diagnostic visit charge while quoting equipment replacement after assessing the home. Explain whether that visit charge applies toward approved repairs, if that is your policy.

Make the scope as visible as the number

A price without a scope invites customers to fill in the blanks. A homeowner may assume a move-out cleaning includes the inside of the oven, cabinets, and refrigerator. Your cleaner may have priced empty rooms and standard surfaces only.

Put the important conditions directly beside the price. Do not bury them on another page or make people discover them after submitting a form. Include what the service covers, the unit being priced, and the circumstances that require a different quote.

For a pressure washing service, explain whether the quoted work covers the driveway alone or also the sidewalk and patio. Mention whether heavy staining needs separate assessment. For lawn care, describe whether mowing includes trimming and blowing clippings off paved areas, and how oversized or overgrown lots are handled.

List any charges the customer needs to understand, such as travel outside your normal area or optional haul-away work. Describe how applicable taxes and other charges are handled without trying to give tax advice. Clear scope belongs on the service page where customers make their decision.

Use starting prices and ranges honestly

A starting price should describe a job you actually accept at that amount. If nearly every customer needs an unavoidable extra, the displayed starting figure is a poor guide. Change the offer or explain the usual total more clearly.

Build ranges from your own records, not a number borrowed from another business. Separate unlike jobs before calculating anything. Interior painting of one empty room is different from painting occupied rooms with damaged walls, dark existing colors, and detailed trim.

You can explain those differences without publishing a number for every possibility. A useful note might say: "We quote after measuring the rooms and checking wall condition. Repairs, ceilings, and trim are listed separately in your estimate." Adapt that example only if it matches your process.

A range should also have a next step. Tell the customer whether photos can narrow it down or whether you need a visit. See how to write website text in everyday language if your explanation starts sounding like a contract.

If you cannot publish a price, publish the process

Some work cannot be priced responsibly from a short description. A roofer assessing a leak needs to understand more than the size of the stain on the ceiling. An electrician planning a new circuit needs details about the existing system and the requested installation.

Instead of leaving a blank, answer the questions that determine the customer's next step:

  1. Is the estimate or diagnostic visit charged?
  2. What information should the customer provide before the visit?
  3. Will you give a written scope before work starts?
  4. How do you handle additional work discovered later?
  5. When should the customer expect the next response?

Use your actual policies. Do not describe estimates as free if there are conditions you have not explained. Do not promise a response time your working schedule cannot support.

Keep the inquiry simple. Ask for the service, location, and enough detail to direct the conversation. A long questionnaire can wait until it is needed; the guide to contact forms customers can finish covers that balance.

Treat pricing as a useful filter

Publishing prices can help someone decide that your service fits their budget. It can also help someone decide it does not. Fewer inquiries are not automatically a bad result if you spend less time quoting work you would never take.

Do not judge the change by call volume alone. Track whether inquiries concern services you offer, whether customers understand the scope, and whether estimates become booked work. Also record the questions people still ask about the price.

For example, a mobile detailer might find that customers keep asking whether pet hair removal is included. That is a reason to improve the package description. It is not necessarily a reason to hide the whole price list.

Your competitors can see public prices, but they cannot see your whole operation through a rate card. Decide how much detail helps your customers, then review the result against your own workload and margins.

Keep every published price current

Make a short list of the places where your rates appear: service pages, a pricing page, downloadable menus, and any listings you maintain. When a rate changes, check that list together. A correct main page does not fix an old price still shown elsewhere.

Give one person responsibility for updates. Review the scope at the same time as the amount. A package that no longer includes haul-away needs a wording change even if the price stays the same.

Read the finished page on your phone. Can you see the price, conditions, and next step without opening several separate sections? Ask someone unfamiliar with the business to explain what they think they would receive. Their answer is a useful check on whether your website feels trustworthy.

Frequently asked questions

Will showing prices scare customers away?

It may put off people whose budgets do not fit the service. That can save both sides time, but vague or misleading prices can also put off suitable customers. Explain the scope and review the quality of inquiries after publishing.

Should I put every service price online?

No, you can publish defined packages and explain how you quote variable work. A customer should still understand any charge involved in getting the estimate. Use different formats when different services need them.

What if a job costs more than the starting price?

Describe the factors that can increase the price beside the starting figure. Give the customer the actual scope and quote before they agree to the work. If your starting offer is rarely available, reconsider how you present it.

The short version

Publish the price information you can explain honestly. Pair every number with its scope, and describe the estimate process when you need to inspect the job first. Keep the information current and judge it by the quality of the conversations it produces.
