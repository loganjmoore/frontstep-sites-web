Landscaping: get more calls from Google

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Landscapers can make Google inquiries more useful by keeping their Business Profile accurate, explaining services and coverage on their website, and requesting honest customer reviews. Separate recurring lawn care from one-time landscape projects so people know what to request. Then test the call and form process, and track whether inquiries match the work and routes you can serve.

How do you get more calls from Google for landscaping work you actually want? Make it easy to identify your services, your route area, and how estimates work. A homeowner seeking weekly mowing is asking a different question from someone planning new planting beds, and your business information should make that difference clear.

Understand what you can influence

Google describes local results in terms of relevance, distance, and prominence. Complete business information helps it understand your business, but you cannot choose a searcher's location or buy a better local ranking. See Google's explanation of local ranking.

For a landscaper, the practical task is to describe the work and area accurately. A mowing company should not look like a landscape design studio if it does not offer design. A crew focused on installations should not invite recurring lawn inquiries it cannot take.

Treat your profile, website, and reviews as connected customer information. The profile introduces the business, the website explains the work, and genuine feedback gives prospective customers another perspective. None is a switch that guarantees a set number of calls.

Start with the inquiries you want. Write down the services, neighborhoods, property types, and job sizes that fit your operation. Those choices guide your wording and keep marketing aligned with crew capacity.

Make the Business Profile match your actual operation

Check the business name, phone, website link, hours, and service area. Review the categories available in your account and choose those that accurately describe your work. Do not add unrelated services just to appear in more searches.

Make your service list specific. Depending on your business, it could include recurring mowing, edging, seasonal cleanups, mulching, shrub trimming, planting, sod installation, or landscape design. Only include irrigation or hardscape work when you actually provide it.

Represent your location honestly, including whether customers visit you or you travel to them. Do not invent a branch in each neighborhood on your route. Google's business representation guidance explains the distinction.

Use current photos of your work with permission. A maintained lawn, defined bed edge, or completed planting project should include enough context to show what your crew did. Avoid presenting a stock garden as a local customer project.

Separate lawn routes from landscape projects

A website headed only "Beautiful outdoor spaces" does not tell a homeowner whether you offer weekly mowing. Name that service directly if it is part of your business. Explain what is normally included and what is quoted separately.

For recurring lawn care, answer questions such as:

  • Which neighborhoods are on your current route?
  • What tasks are included in the usual visit?
  • How do you discuss visit frequency and seasonal changes?
  • Do you need to assess the property before quoting?
  • How can a customer ask about access or gate arrangements?

For landscape installations, describe a different path: discuss the goals, assess the site, agree on scope, and review a proposal. Explain whether you handle planting, bed preparation, drainage-related work, or other project types within your actual qualifications.

Do not bury both conversations in the same generic paragraph. Give each meaningful service enough space to explain the next step. The landscaper homepage copy guide shows how to introduce both without overloading the opening screen.

Build local pages around genuine differences

Your service area should reflect where you can work reliably. Recurring mowing depends on practical routes; a one-time planting project may justify a different travel area. Explain that distinction where customers can find it.

You do not need a separate page for every nearby subdivision. Start with a clear area list. Add a local page when you have useful information such as actual project examples, route availability, or a meaningful service difference.

A real project story might describe refreshing overgrown beds at a local home, including the requested work and completed scope. Get permission for photos and omit the address. Do not invent neighborhood soil conditions or claim that every yard there needs the same treatment.

Keep seasonal advice appropriate to your location and verify plant or treatment claims before publishing. An article copied from another climate can give readers the wrong expectations. Your own job notes are a better starting point for showing how you work.

Ask for reviews after real service milestones

Choose a sensible point in the customer relationship to invite feedback. For a planting project, that might be the completed walkthrough. For recurring maintenance, it could be a normal service follow-up after the customer has experienced your work.

Use a consistent rule rather than selecting only customers you expect to praise you. Ask for an honest review, make it optional, and do not offer a free cut, mulch discount, or other reward. Do not write the review for the customer.

Keep the request brief and relevant. Sample wording could be: "Thanks for choosing us for your yard cleanup. If you would like to share your experience, we welcome an honest Google review." Include the correct review link through a channel the customer has agreed to use.

Respond to concerns about missed areas, cleanup, or scheduling through your normal service process. Do not publish private property details to argue your side. The landscaping review guide covers a routine your team can maintain.

Make every suitable inquiry easy to handle

Visibility is of little use if your phone number is wrong or the form reaches an abandoned inbox. Open the website on your phone, tap the number, and submit a test request. Confirm that the message arrives with enough information to respond.

Ask for contact details, town, and the type of work. For a landscape project, optional photos can help start the conversation. Do not require a homeowner to measure the entire yard before learning whether you serve the area.

Track inquiries simply. Note the source when the customer knows it, the requested service, the town, and whether you could quote the job. Several calls outside your route may point to unclear coverage rather than a need for more marketing.

Also record repeated misunderstandings. If people keep asking for tree removal you do not offer, inspect the wording that suggested it. Improve the information, then judge the change by actual conversations over time.

Frequently asked questions

Should I target every town around me?

Focus on places you can serve reliably and profitably. Recurring mowing routes may need tighter boundaries than installation projects. Tell customers where each service is available.

Do I need a separate page for mowing?

It is useful when recurring lawn care is a substantial part of your business. Explain the visit, coverage, and quoting process. Keep it distinct from one-time landscape design or installation work.

Will more reviews guarantee more calls?

No, reviews are one part of how people assess your business. Your service fit, location, availability, and contact process also matter. Request honest feedback without promising a particular result.

What can I improve today?

Check your phone, hours, coverage, and main service descriptions. Send a test website inquiry and confirm delivery. Then correct the most common misunderstanding you hear from callers.

The short version

Make your profile and website describe the landscaping work you actually accept. Keep lawn routes distinct from larger projects, show genuine work, and ask fairly for reviews. Measure progress through suitable inquiries and completed follow-up.
