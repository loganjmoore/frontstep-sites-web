Pressure washing: more calls from Google

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Give Google and potential customers a clear picture of your pressure washing business: accurate profile details, a useful website, and honest customer reviews. Describe the surfaces you clean and the towns you serve. Then make it easy to call or request a quote, and check which inquiries become jobs that fit your business.

Someone searches for "driveway cleaning near me," while another person types "green siding cleaning" without knowing the term soft washing. How can your pressure washing business turn those searches into useful calls? Start by describing the actual jobs you handle, then remove the friction between finding you and requesting a quote.

Set up your profile around the real business

Check your Google Business Profile for the correct business name, phone number, website, and hours. Choose the available category that best describes your main work, and describe additional services accurately. Do not make your name a string of towns and cleaning keywords.

If you travel to customers and do not receive them at your base, represent that arrangement accurately. Use a realistic service area. Listing towns far beyond a sensible route may invite work you cannot profitably schedule, even if the job itself sounds appealing.

A complete profile, a website, and reviews are useful parts of the work, but they are not Google's three official ranking factors. Google describes local ranking in terms of relevance, distance, and prominence in its local ranking guidance. You cannot guarantee a position by completing a checklist.

Review your public details after changes to your phone coverage or work schedule. If nobody answers during a particular period, set an honest expectation for callbacks. Visibility is of little use when the first experience is an unanswered call with no clear next step.

Describe the surface instead of selling pressure alone

Homeowners usually care about a dirty surface, not the equipment used to clean it. Explain which jobs you take: concrete driveways, house siding, patios, fences, decks, or commercial walkways. Leave out services you do not provide, especially roof cleaning if it is outside your training and equipment.

Separate services when the customer's concerns differ. A driveway page can explain quoting, access, and the limits of stain removal. A house washing page can describe your inspection process and how you choose an appropriate cleaning method for the surface.

Avoid suggesting that higher pressure is always better. Describe your process at a customer level without giving risky instructions about equipment settings or chemical mixtures. Say that you assess the material and condition before agreeing on the work.

Use familiar search language naturally. "Pressure washing in your town" can introduce the business, while "concrete driveway cleaning" and "house washing" help visitors find the right service. The pressure washing homepage copy guide offers examples you can adapt without stuffing search phrases into every sentence.

Show results with honest limits

Before-and-after photos are useful when they are comparable and clearly explained. Photograph the same area from a similar angle, and avoid presenting a wet surface beside a dry one as though the difference is entirely your work. Ask permission before showing a customer's property.

Use captions to explain the job. A shaded concrete patio with surface growth is different from a driveway with old oil marks. Describe what you cleaned and avoid claims that every stain disappears.

Do not label an unknown substance as a particular mold or promise health outcomes from cleaning it. You can describe what is visible without making a diagnosis. Similarly, do not promise that cleaning will fix failed paint, damaged concrete, or weathered wood.

Keep examples organized by surface so a visitor can judge relevant work. A restaurant manager looking for walkway cleaning should not need to scroll through a long gallery of fences. A few clear, well-described jobs can answer more questions than a large unlabeled collection.

Ask for feedback after the customer sees the work

Make review requests part of your normal job closeout. Wait until the agreed work is finished and the customer has had a chance to look at it. Ask for an honest review rather than a particular rating or a list of keywords.

A simple request might be: "If you have a moment, would you share how the cleaning job went? Here is our review link." Treat this as sample wording, not a quote from a customer. Keep the request optional and never exchange a discount for a review.

Do not send only satisfied customers toward public reviews while diverting everyone else into a private form. Give customers a fair opportunity to describe their experience. Address any service problem directly, without making the review a bargaining tool.

When feedback raises a recurring issue, update your website. If customers expect all rust or oil staining to disappear, your scope explanation may need work. Clear limits before quoting are better than repeatedly defending them afterward.

Turn the visit into a quote you can act on

Put a working call button near the main service information. Offer a short quote form for people who cannot call, asking for their town, surface, and preferred contact method. Optional photos can help, but do not force people to measure every slab before contacting you.

Explain what you need to confirm before setting a price. Access, surface condition, water arrangements, and the actual cleaning area may all matter to your process. State that submitting an inquiry does not reserve a visit unless your system really confirms bookings.

Read the guide to contact forms and test your own form from a phone. Check message delivery, not just the thank-you screen. Make sure someone is responsible for replying when the crew is out working.

Keep a simple log of calls and requests: town, service, how the customer found you, and whether the job was booked. The guide to measuring website inquiries can help you spot useful patterns. If inquiries are mostly for roofs you do not clean, revise your descriptions before chasing more visits.

Frequently asked questions

Should I advertise pressure washing and soft washing together?

Yes, if you actually provide both, but explain the surfaces and jobs each service covers. Customers should not have to choose a technical method before asking for help. Avoid claiming one approach suits every material or condition.

Will posting job photos every day guarantee more calls?

No, a posting schedule does not guarantee rankings or inquiries. Choose clear, relevant photos and keep your core information accurate. Spend time checking that visitors can reach you and understand what you offer.

Should I include prices for driveway cleaning?

You can show a starting price or explain the quoting process if the assumptions are clear. Describe what changes the scope instead of implying every driveway costs the same. Confirm the job details before treating an online request as a final quote.
