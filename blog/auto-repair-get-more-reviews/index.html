Auto repair: get more Google reviews

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Ask for an honest review after the repair or diagnostic visit has been explained and the customer has completed the pickup process. Send a direct link through an appropriate existing communication channel, keep participation optional, and avoid incentives. Apply the same approach across customers, including those whose visits involved difficult news or declined repairs.

Your shop completes brake jobs, diagnostics, and maintenance visits all week, but review requests happen only when someone remembers. How do you ask more consistently without making pickup awkward? Add a brief, optional invitation to your normal completed-visit routine and keep it separate from payment and repair decisions.

Choose a sensible point after the service conversation

Pickup can involve several important tasks. The service adviser explains the work, reviews the bill, discusses any remaining recommendations, and returns the keys. A review request should not interrupt those responsibilities or make the customer feel rushed through them.

Use the same completion point for the kinds of visits your shop handles. That might mean a message after pickup or an invitation in the normal service follow-up. Let the customer get through the handoff before asking for feedback.

A diagnosis-only visit can count as a real customer experience even if no repair was approved. Someone who chose not to proceed still experienced your explanation, estimate, and communication. Do not restrict invitations to customers who bought the largest jobs or seemed happiest at the counter.

If work remains open, concentrate on the service issue and communicate the next step. Do not pretend the visit is complete just because the vehicle is temporarily back with the customer.

Use neutral wording that does not ask for a favor rating

Keep the invitation short. Here is sample wording you can adapt: "Thanks for bringing your vehicle to our shop. If you would like to share your experience, here is a link to leave an honest Google review."

For a completed diagnostic visit, another example is: "Thank you for choosing us for your diagnostic appointment. You are welcome to share feedback about your experience using this Google review link." These are invitation examples, not invented customer testimonials.

Avoid "help us get five stars" and instructions about which service terms or employee names to include. Google's review policy prohibits incentives, selective positive solicitation, and requests for specific review content.

A customer can write a short review, a long one, or none at all. Your adviser should not hover over the person's phone, ask to see the submitted rating, or imply that the technician's treatment depends on it. Keep the invitation separate from the service relationship.

Make the link easy to use after the customer leaves

Use your own shop's review link and test it on a phone. A similarly named business or an old location can confuse customers if you copy the wrong destination. Check any printed cards or templates after changes to the business listing.

Google supports direct review links and QR codes for requesting reviews. Customers need a Google Account to leave a review. Google's review guidance explains those basics.

Use an appropriate existing communication channel rather than adding customers to a new message stream without considering their preferences. For example, a normal service follow-up email may be a practical place for the optional link. Respect requests to stop receiving follow-ups.

A card at pickup can also work if it is simply an invitation to use later. Do not turn the waiting room into a station where customers are expected to review the shop before receiving keys. The guide to asking for reviews without pressure offers more ways to keep the request comfortable.

Keep offers and feedback screening out of the process

Do not trade oil changes, tire rotations, discounts, or prize entries for reviews. An incentive can influence the decision to post even when you say the rating is up to the customer. Keep promotional offers independent of review participation.

Avoid sending a satisfaction survey that routes only happy customers to Google. You can collect private feedback about the visit, but that feedback should not decide who gets the public review invitation. Do not ask staff to judge which customers are likely to award a high score.

Do not buy reviews or recruit people who have no genuine customer experience. A supplier, employee, or family member is not a substitute for a customer's independent account of a repair visit. Your review process should reflect actual service experiences.

If you choose to send a reminder, make it a single courteous follow-up and then stop. Do not repeatedly call customers asking why they have not posted. Review requests should not become a source of irritation after an otherwise ordinary maintenance visit.

Reply to concerns without publishing the repair order

A negative review about a diagnostic charge, a delayed part, or an unresolved symptom can be frustrating. Read the service record and talk to the adviser before responding. Public replies should stay calm and protect the customer's information.

Do not publish vehicle identification numbers, license plates, payment details, declined work, or private messages to prove your point. The person reading the reply is also judging how you might handle their own information after a disagreement.

An illustrative reply might say: "We are sorry to hear that the explanation at pickup was unclear. Please contact our service desk so we can review your visit and discuss the concern." Adapt it honestly; do not admit to facts you have not checked or promise a remedy you cannot provide.

Keep any repair follow-up independent of the review. Never offer a refund, free work, or priority service in exchange for removing criticism. The guide to replying to a bad review helps you address concerns without turning the response into an argument.

Make review requests part of a reliable shop routine

Give one role responsibility for sending invitations after the chosen completion point. That may be the service adviser or office staff member who handles follow-up. Record that an invitation was sent so another employee does not send a duplicate.

Do not set staff quotas for obtaining reviews or particular ratings. Focus on whether the agreed customer process is being followed. The customer controls the public response; your team controls the clarity and quality of the service.

Review recurring feedback in your shop meetings. Confusion about diagnostic time may mean your initial authorization needs a clearer explanation. Complaints about pickup updates may point to a communication gap when parts arrive late. Fix those patterns in the actual workflow.

Then update the website where appropriate. The auto repair website guide helps explain service and approval processes before the visit, while the Google Business Profile guide helps keep hours and contact information accurate.

A steady process is more useful than a burst of requests followed by months of silence. Keep it manageable enough that the shop can maintain it during busy weeks without sacrificing service communication.

Frequently asked questions

Can I ask someone who declined the repair?

Yes, if they had a genuine service experience such as a diagnostic visit. Do not exclude them because they declined the estimate or might have mixed feedback. Use the same neutral invitation and let them decide whether to participate.

Should technicians ask customers to mention their names?

No. Let customers choose their own words and details. Staff recognition is better handled through your internal process than through instructions about public review content.

What if a customer says the repair did not solve the problem?

Address that as a service concern through your normal review and follow-up process. Do not focus the conversation on protecting the rating. Any appropriate remedy should stand independently of what the customer posts.

The short version

Ask after the service handoff, provide an easy link, and leave participation and wording to the customer. Use feedback to improve explanations and follow-up without rewarding or screening reviews.
