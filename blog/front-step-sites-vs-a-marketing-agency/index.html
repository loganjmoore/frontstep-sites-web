Front Step Sites vs a marketing agency

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Front Step Sites fits an owner who wants a business website with ongoing changes at a clear recurring price. A marketing agency may fit better when you need someone to plan and run advertising, coordinate larger campaigns, or handle a complex project. Compare the work each proposal includes before comparing its price.

Front Step Sites is a done-for-you website service for small businesses, priced at $99 a year with the domain included, while a marketing agency is a firm that plans and runs advertising and larger campaigns. Do you need a website service, or do you need a marketing agency? It is easy to compare the invoices and miss that you are buying different work. Start with what needs fixing in your business, then choose the person or service equipped to do that job.

Decide whether the problem is the website or the marketing

A plumber whose referrals cannot find a service area or working phone number has a clear website problem. The immediate job is to explain the services, show where the plumber works, and make contacting the business straightforward. A complicated campaign is not needed to correct those missing details.

An HVAC company adding an installation crew may have a broader problem. It might need a seasonal advertising plan, different messages for repairs and replacements, and someone to review which inquiries turn into suitable appointments. A website is part of that work, but it is not the whole assignment.

Write one sentence describing your need. For example: "People referred to us need to see our bathroom remodeling work and request an estimate." If you cannot yet name the problem, review whether your website is getting you customers before buying a larger package.

Know what the Front Step Sites plans include

Front Step Sites costs $99 a year with the domain included and no setup fee. On that plan, you get 2 change requests a month, made within 2 business days. Customers request changes by writing in their account.

The Grow plan costs $29 a month or $290 a year. It includes unlimited changes the same business day and Google reviews shown on the site. New pages, blog posts, and local SEO setup are included.

Choose around the updates you actually expect to request. A house cleaner might need an occasional service area correction or a new page explaining move-out cleaning. A landscaper changing available seasonal services may want more frequent updates. Write out those likely requests before choosing a plan.

Those website features should not be treated as a proposal for managing paid advertising or a large campaign. If that is the work you need, compare agency proposals that explicitly include it. The guide to a $99 website helps you think through the narrower website decision.

Choose an agency when you need broader work

An agency can be the better fit when you need several kinds of marketing managed together. Depending on the agency and the agreed scope, that could include advertising, campaign planning, original photography, branding, or detailed reporting. Ask which people will do the work rather than assuming every agency provides every service.

Consider a roofer trying to reach owners planning full replacements while still taking repair calls. The campaign needs to distinguish those jobs, match the crew's capacity, and give the office a way to recognize the inquiries. An agency with relevant experience may be useful for that coordination.

A larger remodeler might also need custom project galleries and a detailed process for screening inquiries. Bring those requirements to the proposal stage. If you mainly need a specific custom website project rather than ongoing marketing management, hiring a freelancer is another option to compare.

Compare the whole bill and the whole workload

An agency proposal and a website subscription may cover very different things. Ask for the website work, ongoing management, advertising spend, and optional extras to be listed separately. Then compare the totals over the same period, including what happens after an introductory term or initial build.

Do not assume that a large fee includes everything or that a small recurring fee makes every requested feature possible. Turn vague words into concrete deliverables:

  • Which pages will be created, and who supplies or writes the text?
  • Which services require separate software or outside providers?
  • What routine changes are included after launch?
  • Who checks inquiries and reports on the results?
  • What work will still fall to you or your office?

Your time belongs in the comparison. Collecting photos, approving service descriptions, and explaining your business still require your input. The subscription versus one-time build guide explains why the first invoice is only part of the decision.

Check ownership and the ordinary update process

Front Step Sites registers the domain in the customer's own name. Customers can leave any time and take their domain with no transfer fee. That domain policy is separate from questions about moving a complete website, its files, or any connected software.

Ask an agency the same practical ownership questions. Who controls the domain account, site content, business profiles, advertising accounts, and any reporting tools? What will you receive if the relationship ends? Have the proposal explain these points plainly, and ask a lawyer about contract terms you do not understand.

Also test the everyday working relationship before committing. Describe an actual request, such as changing a seasonal service, replacing project photos, or correcting holiday hours. Ask how you would submit it, who would handle it, and when you could expect it to appear. A clear answer is more useful than a broad promise of ongoing support.

Ask for evidence that matches your goal

When reviewing an agency's work, look beyond attractive homepages. Ask for an example involving a similar business problem and an explanation of the work performed. A campaign for a large online retailer does not automatically demonstrate an understanding of a local garage door company's service area and scheduling limits.

Ask how success will be measured. Useful discussions connect inquiries with suitable work, response times, and capacity. Be cautious when every answer is about page views or an impressive report without explaining what the business gained.

Google advises owners hiring search help to ask about relevant experience, communication, and expected results. It also warns against guaranteed first-place rankings in its guide to hiring an SEO. Neither a website subscription nor an agency contract can promise that customers will choose you. Choose clear responsibilities and credible work over certainty no provider can deliver.

Frequently asked questions

Is an agency unnecessary for a small business?

No. A small business can have a demanding marketing problem, such as launching a new service while managing paid campaigns. The useful question is whether the proposed work solves a problem you actually have and can afford to address.

Does local SEO setup mean ongoing advertising is included?

No, you should treat them as different kinds of work. Ask any provider to explain exactly what setup covers and obtain a separate description of advertising work if you need it. Paying for a website is not the same as funding or managing a campaign.

Should I choose the more expensive option if I want growth?

A higher price alone does not establish a better fit. You need useful work, enough capacity to handle customers, and a way to evaluate results. Buy the scope that supports your next step rather than the largest package available.

Can I move my domain later?

Front Step Sites customers can leave any time and take their domain with no transfer fee. Ask any agency to put its own transfer and cancellation process in writing. Also ask separately what happens to the site content and connected accounts.

Consider an agency when you need advertising, coordinated campaigns, or more complex work defined in a proposal. Compare responsibilities, total costs, and exit arrangements before deciding.
