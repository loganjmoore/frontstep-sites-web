Painting: how to get more Google reviews

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Ask painting customers for an honest Google review as part of a consistent project handoff, after you have walked through the work and explained any remaining items. Share a direct review link without requesting a particular rating. Invite feedback fairly, never offer an incentive, and handle touch-ups or complaints separately from the review request.

How do you get more Google reviews for your painting business without making the final walkthrough awkward? Build a simple request into your normal handoff. Customers should understand what is complete, what still needs attention, and that any review is entirely their choice.

Choose a handoff point that makes sense

A review request sent while the kitchen is masked off can feel premature. So can one sent before the homeowner has seen an exterior repaint in daylight. Choose a consistent stage when customers can reasonably describe their experience, such as after the final walkthrough and written handoff.

Use that stage for all eligible customers, including those who raised concerns. Do not ask a private satisfaction question and send the public review link only to people who sound pleased. Your process should invite honest experiences rather than screen for praise.

Painting projects sometimes close in stages. A cabinet job may involve returning doors, adjusting hardware, and addressing a punch list. Explain the outstanding work separately. Do not describe the project as finished merely because you want to send an automated review request.

Decide who owns the ask. It might be the owner who completes the walkthrough or the office person who sends care information. Giving one person responsibility prevents the crew lead, estimator, and office from sending three competing messages.

Ask plainly and leave the rating to the customer

At the walkthrough, you could say: "Thanks for having us work on your home. I'll send our handoff notes and a Google review link. If you'd like to share your experience, we'd appreciate an honest review."

For a follow-up message, adapt this example: "Thank you for choosing us for your interior painting project. If you'd like to leave an honest Google review, here is the link. Your feedback helps people understand what working with our crew is like."

These are sample scripts, not customer quotes. Replace the project description with the actual work and keep the language natural for your business. If the message contains a punch list, separate that information clearly so the review does not look like a condition for finishing the job.

A property manager may care about access coordination and turnover readiness. A homeowner may care about floor protection or communication during a color change. You can say customers are welcome to describe what mattered to them, but do not hand them a keyword-filled paragraph to paste.

Google allows businesses to ask for genuine reviews and provide a review link or QR code. It prohibits incentives such as discounts in exchange for reviews. See Google's review guidance before choosing a request tool.

Make the link easy to use

Get the review link through your business profile and test that it leads to the correct painting company. A customer should not have to search through several similarly named businesses. If your company operates multiple genuine locations, check that the link matches the team that performed the work.

Put the link in a short handoff message or on a clearly labeled card. A QR code can be convenient on paper, but it should not be the only option in an email someone is already reading on a phone. Give them a normal clickable link there.

Do not ask customers to review the job on a crew member's phone. Leave them free to use their own account, their own device, and their own words later. Avoid standing beside them waiting for a submission.

If you need to fix the business information first, use the painting Google Business Profile guide. A good request should send someone to an accurate listing with the same business name they recognize from the estimate.

Keep follow-up fair and separate from repairs

One brief reminder can be enough. Choose a reasonable interval after your first request and stop if the customer declines. Do not make review reminders part of every invoice or touch-up conversation indefinitely.

Your project record can track the handoff date, whether a request was sent, and whether a reminder is due. There is no need to record a private score that decides who gets invited. If several people help with administration, the record also prevents accidental repeat messages.

Never offer a free room upgrade, discounted trim work, or a gift card in exchange for a review. Do not make touch-ups contingent on posting, changing, or deleting one. Google's fake engagement policy also rejects selectively soliciting positive reviews.

Treat repair requests as service work. Confirm the concern, arrange the appropriate next step, and keep the customer informed whether or not they ever review you. Your website should explain how to contact you about a finished project, just as it explains how to request a new estimate. The painting website guide can help you make both routes clear.

Reply to the substance without exposing the job

For a positive review, a short thank-you can acknowledge the part the customer already mentioned: "Thank you for sharing your experience with the cabinet project. We appreciate you choosing our team." Avoid adding the address, amount paid, or details about when the home was empty.

For a complaint about missed spots, reply without diagnosing it publicly: "I'm sorry you have concerns about the finish. Please contact our office so we can review the areas you mentioned and arrange the next step."

Check your records before making specific promises. If the reviewer describes work you cannot identify, say you would like to confirm the project details privately. Do not accuse someone of lying because their public account name differs from the estimate.

The bad review reply guide offers a fuller approach. Report content through Google's process when you believe it violates policy, but do not assume an unfavorable review qualifies for removal.

Look for repeated themes across feedback. Confusion about who buys paint, when furniture moves back, or whether touch-ups are scheduled can reveal gaps in your handoff. Improving those explanations is more useful than polishing the wording of another request.

Frequently asked questions

Can I ask before the customer pays?

You can choose a consistent request point based on the customer's experience rather than payment status. Keep the request separate from collecting the balance so it does not feel like pressure during a financial conversation.

Should I ask customers to mention their town?

Let customers choose what they share. You do not need a scripted location or service phrase to make their account useful to another homeowner.

What if a customer wants help writing the review?

Explain how to reach the review page, but leave the actual account to them. They can describe what happened in plain words, and a short honest review does not need marketing language.
