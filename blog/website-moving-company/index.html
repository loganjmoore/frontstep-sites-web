Moving Company: what your website needs

Updated October 2, 2026

Short answer

A moving company website needs a clear service area, separate pages for local and long-distance moves, and a plain explanation of how your estimates work, since those are the questions a customer comparing movers asks first. Add real photos of your crew and trucks, your USDOT number if you cross state lines, and a short quote-request form.

A customer comparing movers is usually getting two or three quotes before choosing one. What does your website need to say so you make that short list? Your service area, whether you handle their kind of move, and how your pricing actually works.

Separate local moves from long-distance moves

A local move and an interstate move are different services with different pricing logic and different paperwork. Lumping them into one vague "moving services" page makes both harder to explain and harder for a customer to find.

Give each its own section or page:

  • Local, in-town or same-county moves.
  • Long-distance or interstate moves.
  • Commercial or office moves, if that's part of your business.

Name the difference plainly on the homepage so a customer with a cross-country move doesn't have to dig for whether you even do that work.

Explain how your estimates actually work

Customers researching a move compare pricing carefully, and a site that's vague about estimates loses that comparison. Say whether you offer binding or non-binding estimates, and whether pricing is based on weight, cubic footage, or hours. You don't need to publish a single fixed price for every move, but you should explain the process: an in-home or video walkthrough, a written estimate, then a confirmed date.

Movers who are upfront about this save themselves disputes and bad reviews later. If you're deciding what to publish, our guide on whether to put prices on your website covers ways to explain costs honestly without guessing at a job you haven't seen.

Put your USDOT number on the site if you cross state lines

If you move households across state lines, federal law requires you to register with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration and hold a USDOT number and MC operating authority. A purely local, in-state operation is exempt. If you're registered, put the number on your long-distance page or in the footer. It's a small detail a cautious customer can actually verify, and it reads better than leaving it out entirely.

Make your service area easy to check

List your core coverage area in plain text near the top of the homepage, then build out a fuller page for the cities or regions you actually move people in and out of regularly. Give each real service-area page something genuinely useful: typical building types in that area, parking or loading realities, and whether a move there is usually local or long-distance. Our guide to service-area pages covers when a dedicated page helps versus when it's better folded into a regional one.

Don't build a page for a town you served once and don't plan to return to. A thin page with no real detail doesn't help a customer or your search rankings.

Show evidence from real moving jobs

Photos of your actual trucks, your crew, and your equipment do more for trust than a stock image of an unmarked van. Get permission before showing a customer's home, and keep addresses and other identifying details out of frame.

If you show reviews, use the real wording and link back to the source rather than inventing a satisfaction number. Moving is stressful enough that a customer wants to see other people made it through in one piece.

Give every visitor a simple way to request a quote

A short quote-request form next to your phone number should ask for the move dates, origin and destination, and a rough idea of size, with photos optional. Say when you typically respond, and be clear that submitting the form doesn't confirm a booking.

Before calling the site finished, check it like a customer would:

  1. Open it on your phone over cellular data.
  2. Confirm your destination area shows up as covered.
  3. Tap the phone number and check it dials correctly.
  4. Send a test quote request and confirm it reaches you.
  5. Read the confirmation message and make sure it explains what happens next.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need separate pages for local and long-distance moves?

Yes, if you offer both. They're different services with different pricing and different customer questions, and one vague page makes it harder for either kind of customer to find what they need.

Should my USDOT number be on my website?

If you're registered for interstate moves, yes. It costs nothing to add and lets a cautious customer verify you're a legitimate, regulated mover.

Do I need an online booking calendar, or is a quote form enough?

A quote-request form is usually enough to start, especially if your estimates depend on an in-home or video walkthrough. Add real booking later if your process genuinely supports confirming a date online.

What should I fix first on an existing moving company site?

The phone number, the service area, and whether local and long-distance moves are clearly separated. Fix those before adding more photos or pages.

The short version

Build your moving company website around the questions a comparison-shopping customer actually asks: do you serve my area, do you handle my kind of move, and how does your pricing work. Separate local from long-distance, explain your estimate process honestly, and test the phone and quote form yourself before sending anyone else to the site.
