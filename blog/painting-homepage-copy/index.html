Painting: what to put on your homepage

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A painting contractor homepage should name the work you do, the area you serve, and the next step for an estimate. Follow that with clear service descriptions, real project examples, and an explanation of preparation and scheduling. Write about the customer's rooms, surfaces, and concerns instead of relying on broad claims about quality.

What should you write on your painting company's homepage so a homeowner knows whether to call? Start with the project they are considering, then explain how you would approach it. Your page should help someone compare a bedroom repaint, an exterior refresh, or cabinet painting without making all three sound like the same job.

Open with the work, place, and next step

Your first heading should tell readers what you do. An example to adapt is: "Interior and exterior house painting in Cedar Grove." Replace the location and services with your own, and do not use the line if you only handle one kind of work.

Follow with a sentence that answers a practical concern. For example: "Tell us which rooms or surfaces need painting, and we'll arrange the next step for an estimate." That is more useful than a claim about transforming dreams into reality.

Place a clear action beside it. "Request a painting estimate" fits a form. "Call about your project" fits a phone link. If you offer free estimates, say so only if that describes the work being requested.

Avoid promising immediate availability simply to make the opening more appealing. A homeowner trying to finish before moving day needs an honest scheduling conversation. The painting website guide explains how the opening fits with the rest of your site.

Describe each service through its actual scope

Give your main services short, distinct descriptions. You do not need to fit every detail on the homepage, but readers should understand where their project belongs.

Here are example lines you can adapt to your own work:

  • Interior painting: "Walls, ceilings, doors, and trim, with preparation and protection agreed before work starts."
  • Exterior painting: "Exterior repainting with a site visit to assess surfaces, access, and preparation."
  • Cabinet painting: "A consultation to discuss your cabinet surfaces, finish goals, and how the work will affect use of your kitchen."
  • Rental repaints: "Repainting between tenancies, with the rooms, repairs, and handover date agreed in the quote."

Remove anything you do not offer. If you stain decks but do not refinish cabinets, your menu should reflect that difference.

Link service summaries to fuller explanations when available. A customer planning to stay in the house during an interior repaint needs different information from a property manager arranging access to an empty unit. Let each page answer its own set of questions.

Explain preparation in words homeowners recognize

"We never cut corners" is difficult for a visitor to evaluate. Describe what happens before paint goes on instead. Explain how you assess the existing surface, agree on repairs, protect surrounding areas, and prepare the work area.

An example paragraph might read: "At the estimate, we look at the condition of the surfaces and discuss the preparation your project needs. Your written scope identifies the areas to be painted and any agreed repairs. We also explain what you need to move before the crew arrives."

Use that only if it matches your process. Avoid implying that every crack, damaged board, or moisture problem is included in a standard repaint. Tell customers how additional work is identified and discussed.

For occupied homes, explain the household arrangements you actually offer. Who moves large furniture? Where will supplies be kept? What needs to stay clear? These details show care more convincingly than repeating "professional" throughout the page.

Do not turn the homepage into a technical coating manual. Give enough information to make the next conversation easier, then discuss product selection and surface-specific questions during the estimate.

Show projects with captions that explain the work

Choose actual jobs that represent what you want to book. If your best work is interior painting, a homepage dominated by exterior photos sends the wrong message. Include a mix only when it reflects your services.

A useful caption could say: "Living room and hallway repaint, including ceilings and white trim." Another could say: "Exterior siding and porch trim, with preparation included in the agreed scope." These are example formats, not claims about completed projects.

For each real example, check that the caption matches the photograph and your records. Use the town only when appropriate and with the customer's permission. Avoid invented quotes, fake customer names, and claims that a paint job produced a particular increase in home value.

If you include reviews, keep the wording faithful to the original and identify the source appropriately. Do not write your own testimonial and present it as customer feedback. Use the business photo guide to improve the pictures you already have before buying decorative stock images.

Explain estimates, timing, and the working day

Give readers a short outline of what happens after they contact you. A sensible example, if accurate for your business, is:

  1. You discuss the property, surfaces, and desired timing.
  2. You arrange a visit or another suitable assessment.
  3. You provide a written scope and price.
  4. You agree on scheduling and preparation responsibilities.
  5. You review the finished work with the customer.

Explain what can affect timing without sounding evasive. Exterior work may need scheduling flexibility around conditions. Cabinet projects may affect kitchen access. A move-in deadline matters, but your page should invite that discussion rather than promise a date you have not checked.

If you publish prices, define what they cover. "From" pricing without exclusions can create more questions than it answers. The website pricing guide explains how to handle services that need an individual scope.

Finish this section with the information you want in an inquiry. Town, project type, rooms or surfaces, and a preferred timeframe are a practical start. Avoid requiring customers to know paint quantities before you will speak with them.

Finish with a focused invitation and a copy check

Your closing action should repeat the next step from the top. "Tell us about your painting project" can lead to a short form. Include a visible phone number for people who would rather discuss the work.

Read the whole page as someone who has never hired you. Remove claims such as "the area's number one painter" unless you have a clear, current basis for them. Check that qualifications, insurance statements, and warranties match what you can document.

Read every example aloud. If you would not say it during an estimate, rewrite it. The guide to writing website text when you're not a writer can help you turn ordinary customer conversations into useful copy.

Ask someone unfamiliar with your business to identify your services, coverage area, and estimate process from the page. Any hesitation shows you where another plain sentence would help.

Frequently asked questions

Should the homepage list paint brands?

Only when that information helps customers understand your actual offer. Do not imply that one product suits every surface or that a brand relationship exists when it does not. Detailed product decisions can belong in the project discussion.

What can I write if I do not have many project photos?

Start with the real examples you have permission to publish. Explain the scope and process clearly while you build a broader portfolio. A small honest selection is better than borrowed work presented as your own.

Should I promise a fixed completion time?

Only if you can define the work and support the promise. For most individually quoted projects, explain how you agree on timing and handle changes. Make it easy for customers to tell you about deadlines before an estimate.
