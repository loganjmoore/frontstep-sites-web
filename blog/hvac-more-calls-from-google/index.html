HVAC: get more calls from Google

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Focus on an accurate Google Business Profile, website pages that match your heating and cooling services, and honest customer reviews. Then make calling and scheduling straightforward. Distinguish urgent repairs from replacement estimates and maintenance visits so people understand what you offer before they contact your office.

You want more calls from people who need the heating and cooling work your crew actually does. How do you make Google a better source of those calls? Start by matching your online information to your equipment expertise, service area, and real scheduling capacity.

Understand what you can improve

Your Business Profile, website, and reviews are practical places to work, but they are not a complete description of Google's ranking system. Google identifies relevance, distance, and prominence as its main local ranking factors. You cannot buy a better organic local position. See Google's local ranking guidance.

For your business, that means concentrating on accurate information and a useful customer experience. Do not judge the whole effort by one search from your own desk. Someone across town looking for furnace repair has a different situation from someone near your shop comparing ductless installations.

Define the calls you want before changing pages. A company equipped for residential heat pumps may not want commercial rooftop-unit inquiries. A repair-focused crew might prefer fewer replacement estimate requests during a heat wave. Your online wording should reflect those choices.

Match your profile to the equipment you service

Check your main business information, service descriptions, phone number, hours, and website link. Use an accurate business category and describe the actual work your technicians can take on. Do not list boiler repair simply because another HVAC business offers it.

Review distinctions that matter to callers:

  • Central AC repair versus new system installation.
  • Gas furnace service versus electric heating service.
  • Heat pump troubleshooting and replacement.
  • Ductless equipment, including which work you support.
  • Preventive maintenance and seasonal inspections.
  • Residential versus commercial equipment.

Use current photos of your crew and completed work with permission. An outdoor unit photograph needs context: what kind of project was it, and what part did your team handle? Avoid implying a manufacturer endorsement you do not hold.

The HVAC Google Business Profile guide provides a fuller upkeep checklist. Treat the profile as current business information, especially when your availability changes with the season.

Write pages around the reason someone calls

"HVAC services" is too broad to answer every search. A person typing "AC not blowing cold air" wants help arranging a repair. Someone searching for a heat pump replacement wants to understand the estimate process and compare options.

Give those situations different pages or clearly distinct sections. On an AC repair page, explain the systems you service, the towns covered, your diagnostic process, and how repair approval works. Do not turn a symptom into a guaranteed diagnosis. Warm air alone does not give you enough information to promise a particular part replacement.

On a replacement page, explain what you review during an assessment: the existing system, the home's comfort complaints, and the installation scope. If you evaluate ductwork or electrical requirements as part of that process, describe your actual approach. Avoid promising utility-bill savings you cannot support for that home.

The HVAC website guide helps organize these pages. Give each one a relevant next step, such as calling about repair availability or requesting a replacement assessment.

Turn seasonal attention into clear scheduling

Before the first hot stretch, read your cooling pages as if you were a homeowner with a warm upstairs bedroom. Are you offering an inspection, a repair appointment, or a system estimate? Those are different services, and the visitor should not need to guess which form to complete.

Before heating season, check furnace and heat pump information. Replace expired promotions and explain current scheduling honestly. If you advertise after-hours service, make sure the phone route reaches the people responsible for it.

Keep urgent calls separate from routine maintenance requests. A form that is checked the next business day should say so. A confirmation email should acknowledge receipt without claiming a technician is already scheduled.

You can still collect useful details without a long questionnaire. Ask for the town, contact number, broad equipment type if known, and a short description of the problem. Do not require a model number before a homeowner can ask for help. The HVAC homepage copy guide offers wording you can adapt.

Ask for honest reviews after real service

Build the review invitation into the completion process for repairs, maintenance, and installations. Avoid treating equipment replacements as the only jobs worth mentioning. Customers also care about how you communicate during an ordinary diagnostic visit.

Keep the request neutral and brief. For example: "Thanks for choosing us for your service visit. If you would like to share your experience, here is our Google review link." That is sample wording, not a customer quotation.

Let the customer choose what to say. Do not ask them to repeat a service keyword, name a technician, or award a specific rating. Do not connect a review to a discount or priority scheduling. Google's review policies prohibit incentives and attempts to manipulate review content.

Use private customer feedback to improve your work as well. If callers consistently misunderstand a diagnostic fee, clearer website wording and better phone explanations are more useful than arguing about it after the appointment.

Follow the call through to a booked job

More visibility is only useful if your office can handle the resulting inquiries. Test the website number, your voicemail, and the contact form. Check what happens when the dispatcher is already on another call or the owner is in an attic.

Keep a simple inquiry log using broad categories: repair, maintenance, replacement, outside service area, and unsupported equipment. Note how the caller found you when they know. Record whether the inquiry became a booked visit, rather than treating every call as a sale.

Look for patterns before making changes. Repeated commercial refrigeration calls may mean your service wording is too broad. Replacement requests from distant towns may mean your coverage description is unclear. Missed calls during business hours point to an operations problem that another blog post cannot fix.

Review one issue at a time. Update the relevant page, tell the office what changed, and see whether later conversations are clearer. Avoid claiming success or failure from a tiny sample or a weather-driven rush.

Frequently asked questions

Should I focus on AC repair or installation searches?

Focus on the work you want and can support. Give repairs and installations separate explanations because the caller's questions differ. You can adjust prominence seasonally without pretending one service no longer exists.

Will more reviews guarantee a top position?

No. Reviews are one part of a broader local search picture, and no honest review process guarantees a ranking. Ask consistently because real experiences help prospective customers judge your business.

Do I need a separate website for each town?

Usually, a clear service-area section is a better starting point. Add useful local pages only when you have distinct information and genuinely serve those places. Several nearly identical websites create more details to maintain.
