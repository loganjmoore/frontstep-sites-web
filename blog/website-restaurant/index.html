Restaurant: what your website needs

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A restaurant website needs a readable current menu, accurate kitchen hours, directions, and clear actions for ordering, reserving, or calling. Make the main choices easy on a phone. Explain pickup, large-party, and dietary inquiry arrangements so diners know whether your restaurant fits their plans before they arrive or contact you.

Someone opening your restaurant website may be choosing dinner from a parked car or planning a family meal for next weekend. They need the menu, hours, location, and the right way to order or reserve. Put those tasks first so the website helps customers make a decision instead of slowing them down.

Put the dining decision on the first screen

Start with the restaurant name, type of food, and location. "Neighborhood Italian restaurant in downtown Lancaster" tells a visitor more than a slogan about unforgettable moments. Use your real identity and describe the experience accurately, whether that is counter service, table service, or takeaway.

Make the main actions easy to recognize: "View menu," "Order pickup," and "Reserve a table" if you actually accept reservations. Do not give every action equal emphasis if one is clearly the normal path. A lunch counter may prioritize ordering, while a dining room may prioritize the menu and reservations.

Keep a phone number visible for questions that need a person. A visitor asking about a larger party should not have to search the footer or open a social account. Our click-to-call guide explains how to keep that route usable on every page.

Skip the full-screen introduction or automatic music. A good food photo can establish the mood while leaving room for the details that let a hungry visitor act.

Make the menu readable without extra work

Publish the menu as readable page text with current item names, descriptions, and prices. A downloadable version can be an extra option, but diners should not need to pinch and zoom a photograph of a printed menu just to compare dishes.

Separate menus when the service differs. Lunch, dinner, weekend brunch, and takeaway may have different items or hours. Label them clearly so someone does not choose a dish that is unavailable during their planned visit.

For each menu, check:

  • Prices match what customers will be charged for the listed item.
  • Optional additions and relevant choices are understandable.
  • Availability matches the service period named on the page.
  • Dietary labels describe the actual dishes accurately.
  • A contact route is available for questions about ingredients or preparation.

Avoid promising that a dish is suitable for every allergy based only on a short label. Invite diners to discuss their requirements with staff who can answer about current ingredients and preparation. Update the menu when an item changes rather than leaving a correction buried in a social post.

Distinguish opening hours from kitchen service

A bar may stay open after food service ends. A restaurant may close between lunch and dinner, or stop accepting pickup orders before the dining room closes. Spell out those differences wherever customers make a plan.

Show regular hours and keep holiday or temporary changes current. If you close for a private event, the website should not continue inviting walk-ins for that period. Give someone on the team clear responsibility for updating hours across the places customers use.

Directions should include the full customer address and a map or directions link. Add a short note if the entrance faces a side street, the dining room is upstairs, or pickup happens at a separate counter. Describe access accurately rather than relying on a vague "easy access" claim.

Parking information can help when it is specific and current. Say where customers may park and avoid implying that neighboring spaces belong to your restaurant. The restaurant local SEO checklist covers keeping these practical details aligned with your listings.

Send orders and reservations to the correct place

Test every ordering button from a phone. It should reach your current ordering destination and the correct location if you operate more than one restaurant. A link that opens a general provider page and requires another search creates unnecessary work.

Explain whether an action confirms an order or only sends an inquiry. The same applies to reservations. If a group request needs a staff reply, label it as a request and say when the customer should expect confirmation, using a timeframe your team can meet.

For pickup, tell customers where to collect food and how they will know it is ready. For delivery, direct them to the actual ordering route without promising coverage or timing that you cannot control. Keep the page's language consistent with the process customers encounter after clicking.

Large parties, private dining, and catering need separate questions. Ask for the date, approximate group size, type of event, and contact details. Do not make an event organizer use the same small table form if your team needs to discuss the arrangements before accepting them.

Show the restaurant customers will actually visit

Use current food and room photographs that match your service. A diner planning a quiet meal needs to understand the seating and setting, while a parent may want to know whether the space suits a family visit. Pictures should support those decisions rather than show only tightly cropped plates.

Include the entrance or pickup area if finding it is not obvious. A clear daytime photo can be more useful to a first-time visitor than another decorative close-up. Keep image files light enough that the menu and buttons remain easy to reach on a mobile connection.

Add a short introduction explaining the restaurant's food and approach without making visitors read a long origin story first. Share specific, supportable details instead of claims such as "the city's most loved restaurant" that you cannot substantiate.

Check the finished site as if you were deciding where to eat tonight. Find the relevant menu, confirm food service hours, and follow the order or reservation route. Our guide to websites built for phones first can help you spot obstacles that are easy to miss on a desktop.

Frequently asked questions

Is a PDF menu enough?

A PDF can be useful as a downloadable copy, but readable menu text is easier to browse directly on a phone. Keep any PDF consistent with the current menu so customers do not compare conflicting prices or items.

Should a restaurant website try to generate more calls?

It should make calling easy when a customer needs help. Clear menus, hours, and ordering instructions can also answer routine questions so staff spend less time repeating those details during service.

Do I need online reservations?

Only if reservations fit how you run the dining room. If you use walk-ins or phone bookings, say so clearly and explain how you handle larger groups.
