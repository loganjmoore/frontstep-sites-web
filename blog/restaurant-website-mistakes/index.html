Restaurant website mistakes

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Restaurant websites frustrate guests when menus are hard to read, hours conflict, ordering buttons lead to the wrong place, or practical visit details are missing. Put a current menu, location, hours, and the main guest actions within easy reach. Test each path on a phone using the same information your staff gives customers.

A guest wants to see your dinner menu before deciding where to eat tonight. Another is trying to collect a pickup order on the way home. Your restaurant website should help both without making them hunt through a photo slideshow or call for information that could be clear on the page.

The menu is a blurry image or an old download

A photographed menu can become hard to read on a small screen. Guests should not have to pinch and drag around a page to compare dishes and prices. Put the main menu into readable website text, with clear sections and enough spacing.

A download can still be useful as an extra option. Label it clearly and make sure it matches the current menu. Remove outdated copies from the pages you control so guests are not choosing from last season's dishes.

Separate menus when serving times differ. Breakfast, lunch, dinner, and weekend brunch should not look like one all-day offer if they are not. State when each is served and how guests can check current availability.

Describe dishes in practical terms. A creative name may need a short explanation of what arrives on the plate. For dietary or allergy questions, give guests a way to speak with staff and keep published information accurate; do not make promises your kitchen cannot support.

Hours tell different stories in different places

The homepage says you close late, but the ordering page has stopped accepting pickup. The footer still shows an old Sunday schedule. These conflicts make a guest unsure whether a trip or order is worthwhile.

Use clear wording for the hours that matter. If the kitchen stops taking orders before the bar closes, explain that distinction. If lunch service pauses before dinner, show the break instead of implying food is available continuously.

Choose one person to update hours across the website and your business listings whenever they change. Holiday opening needs a deliberate check. Do not rely on a social post to correct hours that remain wrong on the main site.

Ordering and reservation buttons are misleading

Label each action by what it does. "Order pickup," "Order delivery," "Reserve a table," and "Ask about a large party" describe different paths. A generic "Book now" button can create uncertainty if guests do not know whether it means a table or an event.

Check the destination for every button. It should lead to the correct restaurant and location, not a general service homepage or an old venue listing. If a destination is unavailable, provide a useful next step that your team can actually support.

Make inquiry forms honest about their purpose. A catering request does not confirm the event, and a large-party request does not automatically reserve seats. The confirmation should explain what happens next without promising a response time you cannot meet.

Avoid placing several competing order buttons without context. If different routes serve different needs, explain those differences plainly. Test the complete journey on a phone before promoting it.

Photos hide the information people came for

A full-screen video of the dining room may look attractive while the menu and address sit far below it. Put the useful actions first and let photos support the decision. Guests should be able to learn what you serve without waiting for a gallery to load.

Use photos of your actual food and premises. Show dishes in the form guests can expect to receive, and remove images of items that are no longer available unless their context is clear. Stock meals can create expectations your kitchen never intended.

Prepare images for the web rather than uploading every original at full size. Choose a focused set: recognizable dishes, the dining room, and the entrance if it helps people find you. The website photo guide offers practical ways to gather those images.

Give the menu and contact information real text, not text baked into graphics. That makes them easier to read and maintain when prices or hours change.

Visit and pickup details are missing

An address alone may not explain which door to use or where pickup orders are collected. Add practical directions for the last part of the visit. Describe the entrance, parking arrangements, or pickup point accurately without implying space is guaranteed.

Guests may also need to know whether you accept walk-ins, how large parties are handled, and whether patio seating can be reserved. Put the answers near the relevant action. Do not bury every useful detail in a long FAQ far from the booking link.

For pickup, explain the collection process you actually use. A guest should know whether to come to the counter or another marked area. Keep instructions consistent with what staff are ready to do during service.

If you offer delivery or off-site catering, explain your genuine coverage and inquiry process. Do not advertise nearby towns as served unless you can fulfill those requests. The restaurant website guide helps you organize these details around guest needs.

Run a guest check before changing the design

Ask someone unfamiliar with the site to find a dinner dish, check tonight's hours, and start a pickup order. Then ask them to find the restaurant and enquire about a larger group. Watch where they hesitate.

Check your own form inbox and all external destinations. Use an authorized test method if testing would otherwise place a real order or reserve a table. A working button alone does not prove the rest of the path is clear.

Fix incorrect information and broken actions first. Then improve readability, photo size, and page layout. This keeps the work tied to actual guest problems instead of a redesign that leaves old menu details untouched.

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep a PDF menu?

Yes, as an additional option if it is current and clearly labeled. Give guests a readable menu on the page too. The download should not be the only way to discover your dishes and prices.

Should every page have an order button?

Make the main action easy to find where it is relevant, especially on menu and location pages. Keep its label clear about pickup or delivery. Do not let it obscure other important information on a small screen.

Do I need separate pages for multiple locations?

Separate pages can be useful when locations have different hours, menus, directions, or ordering links. Make those differences explicit and check every destination. A shared page can work only if guests can reliably choose the right location.
