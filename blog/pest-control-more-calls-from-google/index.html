Pest control: get more calls from Google

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

To get more useful pest control calls from Google, keep your business details accurate and explain the pests, properties, and areas you actually serve. Build clear pages around inspection and next steps, support them with genuine reviews, and make contacting you simple. Avoid promising an identification, treatment result, or arrival time before you can confirm it.

A person searching for pest control may be looking at ants near a kitchen sink or hearing movement above a bedroom ceiling. They want to know whether you handle the problem, how soon they can speak to someone, and what happens next. Your online information should answer those questions calmly and clearly.

Make the business profile accurate before expanding it

Check your Google Business Profile for the right name, phone number, website, service area, and hours. Choose categories that fit the services you provide. Do not advertise termite work, wildlife removal, or bed bug treatment simply because those searches might bring calls if you do not offer the work.

Google says local results depend on relevance, distance, and prominence in its local ranking guidance. A complete profile is useful, but it does not guarantee a top position throughout your entire coverage area.

Be precise about availability. A form that accepts messages overnight is not the same as an overnight response service. If your technicians work by appointment, explain how customers request an inspection and when someone handles inquiries.

Check existing listings elsewhere for an old number or former address. A worried caller should reach your current team, not a disconnected line. Keep the same core facts on your website so a customer does not have to decide which version to trust.

Give each pest problem the explanation it needs

Pest-specific pages are useful because the customer questions differ. Someone asking about recurring ants needs a different explanation from a property manager dealing with possible bed bugs. A rodent inquiry may involve inspection and entry-point work, while termite concerns need their own assessment process.

For each service you genuinely offer, explain:

  • The types of concerns that should prompt a call.
  • What information you collect before arranging a visit.
  • What the inspection is intended to establish.
  • How you discuss the recommended service and its price.
  • Whether follow-up is part of the agreed plan.

Use familiar phrases such as "ant control in Plano" or "rodent inspection near me" where they fit naturally. These are example searches, not claims that a particular phrase has a certain search volume.

Avoid turning a symptom into a diagnosis. Bites, noises, or marks alone may not identify the pest. Your page can explain that the team assesses the evidence before recommending a service. The pest control homepage copy guide shows how to carry that message onto the first page.

Explain the visit before asking for the booking

Customers may hesitate because they do not know what an inspection involves. Describe your actual arrival process, the areas you may need to access, and how you review findings with the customer. If there is an inspection charge, state it or explain when it is confirmed.

Keep preparation guidance specific to the agreed service. Do not publish a universal instruction to empty every cupboard or discard belongings when that may not apply. Say when the customer receives preparation instructions and who they should contact with questions.

Questions about pets, children, aquariums, and re-entry deserve a direct conversation about the planned service. Explain that your team provides the relevant instructions. Avoid blanket claims that all treatments are harmless or that no preparation is ever needed.

Clarify commercial work too. A restaurant manager may need access arrangements and service records, while an apartment manager may need to coordinate multiple units. Create separate explanations when your process differs, rather than promising that one residential package covers every property.

Build trust without embarrassing the customer

Use real business photos that help someone recognize the team and understand the service. A technician preparing for an inspection or your marked service vehicle can be useful. Graphic infestation images are not necessary on every page and may distract from the next step.

Ask permission before showing work at a customer's property. Keep addresses, unit numbers, paperwork, and identifying features out of public photos unless you have an appropriate reason and permission to include them. Pest problems can be sensitive for households and businesses alike.

Invite honest reviews through a consistent follow-up routine. Do not trade free visits or discounts for reviews, and do not limit the request to customers who first give positive private feedback. Google's review content policy prohibits incentivized reviews.

Keep public replies discreet. Thank the reviewer without naming the pest, treatment details, or property situation unless there is a clear reason to discuss what they themselves shared. Our guide to replying to a bad review can help you handle complaints without exposing private service details.

Make the call route work under real conditions

Put a visible phone number near the top of each service page. Test that tapping it opens the correct number on a phone. If you offer a form as well, ask for the town, property type, brief concern, and a reply method.

Allow customers to say they do not know which pest is involved. A required pest selection can make someone guess when what they really need is an inspection. An optional photo can help start the discussion, but do not encourage handling pests or entering unsafe areas to get one.

Send requests to a place your team checks, and make the confirmation explain the next step. Distinguish "We received your request" from "Your appointment is booked." The click-to-call guide explains how to keep the phone route accessible across the site.

Track useful outcomes: the concern reported, town, inspection arranged, and service accepted. If inquiries repeatedly involve work you do not do, review the wording attracting them. More calls only help when the team can respond appropriately and provide the service being requested.

Frequently asked questions

Should I make a separate page for every insect?

No, focus on services you actually offer and can explain usefully. Closely related concerns can share a page when the assessment and service process are similar.

Can I promise same-day pest control on the website?

Only make a promise your operation can consistently honor. If openings vary, ask customers to call about availability and confirm the appointment before they rely on it.

Should I quote treatment prices without an inspection?

You can explain a defined service price when the scope is clear. If the pest, extent, property, or treatment plan is uncertain, explain how you assess the work and confirm the price instead.
