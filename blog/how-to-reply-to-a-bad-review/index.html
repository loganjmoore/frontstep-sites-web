How to reply to a bad review

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Reply to a bad review by acknowledging the concern, checking what happened, and offering a clear way to talk privately. Keep the response short, factual, and free of customer details. If the review breaks the platform's rules, report it, but do not assume a complaint will be removed because you disagree.

Someone has left a bad review of your business, and you want to answer without making things worse. Start by checking the job record and writing a calm reply with one useful next step. The reply should help the customer and show future customers how you handle a problem.

Check the facts before you start typing

Read the review twice. Separate the complaint from the language around it. A customer saying your plumber was "useless" may actually be reporting a missed arrival window or a leak that returned after the visit.

Look up the appointment, estimate, invoice, and messages. Ask the person who handled the work what happened. Do this privately, without collecting the whole crew around a phone to argue about the reviewer.

Check what your business promised. If your website says "same-day repairs" but you only meant same-day appointments, the customer may have understood the offer differently. That is something you can fix in your website service page, even while you investigate the job.

If you are angry, draft the response somewhere outside the review screen. Read it again once you can remove insults, sarcasm, and guesses about the customer's motives.

Give the reply a simple job

A useful response acknowledges the specific concern and tells the customer what to do next. It does not need to settle every disputed point in public.

Use this order:

  1. Name the concern in ordinary language.
  2. Apologize for a mistake you have confirmed, or acknowledge the frustration while you investigate.
  3. Offer a direct business contact and explain what you will review.

For example, "I'm sorry you were left waiting without an update. Please call our office and ask for the service manager so we can review the appointment and follow up."

Do not promise a refund, replacement, or return visit unless someone has authority to arrange it. Saying "we will look into it" is reasonable only when you have a person assigned to do that work.

Google's guidance on review replies recommends concise, professional responses, protecting customer privacy, and moving complicated discussions to phone or email. Keep the public response focused on those basics.

Adapt the wording to what actually happened

These are example replies, not quotes from customers. Replace the details with facts you have checked and your real business contact information.

For a missed appointment: "I'm sorry we missed the agreed arrival window and did not keep you informed. Our office would like to arrange a time to speak and review what went wrong. Please call the number on our website and ask for the owner."

For an unexpected charge: "Thank you for raising your concern about the bill. We would like to review the estimate and completed work with you. Please contact our office so we can discuss the details privately."

For a repair that did not resolve the problem: "I'm sorry to hear the problem has returned. Please contact our service desk so we can review the work and discuss the next step. We need to look at the job details before suggesting a solution."

For a customer you cannot identify: "We have not been able to match this review to a booking from the information shown. Please contact our office with the appointment details so we can investigate."

The last example matters. A public profile name may differ from the name on an invoice, so an unfamiliar name is not proof of a fake review.

Leave private details out of the argument

Do not publish an address, invoice, phone number, payment history, security code, or screenshot of a private message. A customer mentioning part of a job does not make the rest of your records suitable for public posting.

An auto repair shop can say it wants to review an estimate without posting a customer's vehicle registration. A cleaner can acknowledge a concern without describing the condition of a private home. Avoid naming employees to shift blame, too.

Resist the urge to quote your paperwork line by line. Even if your account is accurate, a long argument can leave future customers wondering whether their own dispute would become public.

Check the contact route you give. A broken form or unanswered inbox makes an otherwise helpful reply a dead end. If needed, improve how you handle website inquiries so complaints reach someone who can act.

Report rule-breaking reviews for the right reason

A low rating is not automatically a policy violation. Google's review reporting guidance says removal depends on a policy violation, not whether the business dislikes or disputes the review.

If you believe the review contains spam, threats, or private information, read the platform's current rules and use its reporting process. Save a private copy and note the reason you reported it. Do not promise your team that removal is certain.

Do not offer money, discounts, or free work in exchange for deleting or changing a Google review. Google's rules on fake engagement and rating manipulation prohibit those incentives.

You can still consider a fair remedy for a service problem. Keep that decision separate from the rating. If a complaint includes a serious legal allegation, get advice from a lawyer before discussing its details publicly.

Finish the follow-up and fix the pattern

Put the complaint on someone's task list with an agreed next action. Record whether you reached the customer and what you agreed to do. A polished public reply is not the same as solving the problem.

Look for a practical change you can make. Confusion about weekend availability may mean your hours need attention. Repeated questions about callout fees may mean the booking conversation and website explain them poorly.

Add those corrections to your website update routine. Continue asking customers for honest Google reviews through your normal process, without launching a campaign to bury a complaint.

Frequently asked questions

Should I reply to a review with no written comment?

You can leave a brief invitation to contact the business. Do not invent a complaint or assume you know which job the rating concerns.

Should I apologize if I think the customer is wrong?

You can acknowledge frustration without agreeing to an unverified account. State only what you know and invite a private conversation about the details.

What if the customer keeps arguing publicly?

Avoid adding another long reply to every point. Keep a clear contact route available and continue any useful investigation privately.

Can I use the same response for every bad review?

Keep a basic structure, but acknowledge the actual concern in each response. A reply about scheduling makes little sense under a complaint about workmanship.

The short version

Check the facts, acknowledge the concern, and offer a practical next step. Protect private information and follow through on what you promise. Use the complaint to correct any misleading information on your website.
