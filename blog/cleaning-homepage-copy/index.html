Cleaning: what to put on your homepage

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Your cleaning homepage should say what you clean, where you work, and how customers request a quote. Separate routine visits from deep cleans, move-outs, and office work. Use specific task descriptions, explain access and supplies, and show real evidence so customers understand what they are buying before they contact you.

Writing a cleaning homepage gets easier when you think about the questions you answer before a first visit. Customers want to know whether you take their type of property, what the clean includes, and whether they can trust the arrangement. Use the example lines below as starting points, and keep only statements that match your business.

Open with the cleaning work and the place

Your headline should identify the service without making someone interpret a slogan. "Recurring house cleaning in Bloomington and nearby neighborhoods" is clearer than "A brighter life begins here." If offices are your main work, lead with that instead: "Office cleaning for local workplaces after the working day."

Follow the headline with a short explanation. For example: "Choose regular cleaning, a first deep clean, or help preparing an empty home for move-out." This tells a homeowner what they can ask about without forcing them to read every page.

Use one main action, such as "Request a cleaning quote," with a phone number nearby. If your team needs to review the property before accepting it, avoid a button that implies the customer has booked a visit immediately.

Keep the opening focused on the visitor's decision. You can explain your company history farther down the page. A customer with a move-out deadline needs to find the service and contact route before reading why you started the business.

Describe each service with a useful boundary

Write a short paragraph for each service you actually offer. Name the work, explain who it suits, and state one important limit. That is more helpful than giving every service the same promise of a spotless space.

For recurring cleaning, an adaptable example is: "Regular visits to keep your kitchen, bathrooms, floors, and living spaces on a consistent cleaning schedule. We agree on the checklist before the first visit." Add your actual frequency options rather than assuming every company offers the same schedule.

For a deep clean, explain how it differs from maintenance work. You might include detailed attention to reachable trim or buildup in agreed areas, but only if that is your scope. "Deep clean" alone does not tell a customer whether you will clean inside appliances or move furniture.

For move-outs, try: "Cleaning for empty homes before handover, with appliance and cabinet interiors quoted according to the agreed checklist." For offices, explain workspaces, restrooms, breakrooms, and hours. If these need longer descriptions, link to the relevant service pages rather than crowding the homepage.

Explain the quote before asking for details

Customers should understand why you cannot always quote from a bedroom count alone. Condition, property size, visit frequency, additional tasks, and access can change the work. Say that plainly without making the process feel like a negotiation with hidden charges.

A useful example is: "Tell us about your property, the cleaning you need, and your preferred timing. We will review the scope with you before confirming the price and visit." Replace that process if you use a walkthrough or another method.

Near the form, list the details that help you begin:

  • The town or ZIP code where the property is located.
  • Whether it is a home, office, or another property you accept.
  • The approximate size and requested service.
  • Any deadline or recurring schedule preference.
  • A phone number or email address for the reply.

Do not ask for door codes in a public inquiry form. Arrange sensitive access details later. Our contact form guide explains how to keep the first request useful without turning it into a lengthy questionnaire.

Replace vague trust claims with working details

"Reliable and professional" does not tell someone how a visit works. Explain who arrives, how customers communicate changes, and what happens if they notice something that needs attention. Use your actual process, including any limits, instead of promising a level of service you cannot consistently provide.

If you bring supplies, say so and explain how you handle requests about products or surfaces. If customers provide particular items, make that clear. Avoid broad claims such as "safe for every surface" or "chemical-free" that do not explain what you use.

Customers may also need to know whether they must be home and how pets should be managed during the visit. A short line such as "We agree on entry arrangements and pet instructions before your appointment" is useful if it reflects what you do.

Add real reviews with permission where needed, and present their wording accurately. Photos should show work you can identify without revealing private belongings. For more ways to replace empty promises, see what makes a local website trustworthy.

Finish the page with coverage and the next step

List your normal service towns in readable text. If you group visits by route or accept some areas only for certain job sizes, explain the arrangement before a customer expects an appointment. A map can support the explanation, but it should not replace it.

Repeat the quote action near the end of the page. An example closing line is: "Tell us what needs cleaning and where you are. We will discuss the right service and available dates." Keep this promise tied to a process someone on your team owns.

Read the finished page out loud. Replace terms you would not use in a customer conversation, and remove repeated claims that add no detail. Check every sample sentence against the actual checklist your crew follows.

Then test the page from a phone. Can you find the service area, understand the scope, and send a request? Once that works, the guide to getting cleaning calls from Google can help you connect the page with your broader business information.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use the word maid on my homepage?

Use language your customers understand and that accurately describes your business. You can mention a familiar search term naturally, but you do not need to repeat it when house cleaning is the clearer description.

Should I promise a spotless result?

Describe the agreed work and your process for handling concerns instead. Broad promises can create expectations about permanent stains, damage, or tasks that were never included in the visit.

Can I use the same copy for homes and offices?

Some company information can be shared, but the service explanations should differ. Homes raise questions about pets and belongings, while offices often need walkthroughs, scheduled access, and a clear division of cleaning responsibilities.
