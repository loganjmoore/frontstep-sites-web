What a website redesign should cost

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A website redesign should cost what it takes to solve a clear business problem, with the work and ongoing costs spelled out before you agree. Typical custom freelancer projects often cost a few thousand dollars, but a basic replacement can cost much less. Fix broken contact details or weak service descriptions before assuming you need a full rebuild.

Your website looks old, and someone has told you it needs replacing. Does that mean a new logo, a different layout, or starting again from scratch? Before you spend anything, work out whether the website is failing customers or simply failing your own taste test.

Start with the customer task that is failing

Open your site on your phone and act like someone who has never heard of your business. Find a service, check whether you cover their neighborhood, and try to contact yourself. Actually send the form and confirm that the message arrives.

A heating contractor's customer might need furnace repair before the temperature drops overnight. A remodeler's customer may want photographs of finished bathrooms and an explanation of how estimates work. Those are different tasks, and a redesign should address the one your customer actually has.

Write down the failure in plain language. "People cannot find our commercial service" is useful. "The site needs more impact" is too vague to price or test. Our guide to checking whether your website brings customers helps you look beyond whether you like the colors.

Separate small repairs from reasons to rebuild

An outdated photograph or a missing service description rarely justifies replacing everything. Make a repair list first:

  • Correct the phone number, hours, and service area.
  • Put a working contact option on the pages customers visit.
  • Replace inaccurate prices and expired promotions.
  • Explain the services people keep calling to ask about.
  • Remove forms or links that lead nowhere.

A rebuild becomes more reasonable when the same problem affects the whole site. Perhaps every page is difficult to read on a phone, the navigation cannot accommodate your services, or routine changes require workarounds nobody understands.

Business changes can justify one too. A lawn mowing company that now concentrates on drainage and retaining walls needs more than a new header photograph. Its pages should help customers understand estimates, site access, and the kinds of projects it accepts. Sometimes you can achieve that within the existing site. Ask before paying to replace it.

Compare costs by scope and ongoing responsibility

There is no useful universal price for a redesign. Replacing a few informational pages is different from building a customer portal, moving a large project gallery, or connecting a booking system.

As a broad planning guide, typical custom freelancer projects often cost a few thousand dollars, with complex work costing more. Treat that as a general range, not a quote or a minimum you must spend. Request a written price for your actual requirements.

Give each provider the same short brief

You cannot compare quotes if one person is pricing a homepage refresh and another is pricing all your copy and photographs. Send each provider the same list of needs.

Include your current site address, main services, service area, and the specific failures you found. List the pages you want to keep, revise, add, or remove. Explain whether you can supply accurate text and usable photographs, or need help preparing them.

State what the customer must be able to do when the work is finished. For example, a mobile mechanic might need customers to describe the vehicle, explain whether it starts, and provide its location before arranging a visit.

Ask the provider to identify what is included, what is excluded, and what would change the price. Agree on how you will review the work and who gives final approval. Make sure the quote identifies any new accounts or recurring services you would have to manage.

Protect useful pages when you replace the site

A redesign should account for existing page addresses, customer bookmarks, and links from other websites. Keep useful page addresses where practical. If an address must change, ask for a plan that sends visitors from the old page to its relevant replacement.

Google recommends mapping old URLs to new ones, setting up redirects, and testing the new site. It also warns that rankings can fluctuate during a significant move. A careful migration reduces avoidable problems, but nobody should promise that search positions cannot change. See Google's site move guidance.

Save a copy of your current text and photographs before work begins. Identify who manages your domain and business email so the website change does not accidentally disturb another service. The checklist for switching website providers explains what to discuss before launch.

Approve the working website, not just its appearance

A handsome homepage is only one part of the job. Before approving the redesign, use the preview to complete the same customer tasks that failed earlier.

Read the service pages on your phone. Check that buttons dial the right number. Submit a real test inquiry and make sure it reaches the person responsible for replying. Follow links from service pages to the contact page and back.

Check business facts separately from the design. A polished page can still list a town you no longer serve or imply that emergency calls are answered overnight when they are not.

After launch, repeat those checks on the public site. Keep a short record of inquiries and the jobs they concern so you can see whether the new pages attract the work you want. Use the same measures you used before the redesign.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a business website be redesigned?

Use its condition and usefulness rather than its age. If customers can understand your services and contact you easily, regular updates may be enough. Rebuild when the existing structure keeps getting in the way.

Will a redesign automatically improve Google rankings?

No. A clearer, more useful site can address real weaknesses, but changing its appearance does not guarantee better rankings. Treat promises of a particular search position as a reason to ask harder questions.

Should I change my domain during the redesign?

Usually there needs to be a separate business reason, such as a name change. Keeping a familiar domain avoids asking existing customers to learn a new address. Discuss any necessary domain change as its own part of the project.

What if I cannot afford a full redesign?

Prioritize inaccurate information and broken contact methods first. Then improve the pages for your most important services. A focused repair can be a sensible stopping point if it solves the customer problem.

The short version

Price a redesign around specific problems, required pages, and ongoing responsibilities. Test small repairs before replacing a useful website. If you rebuild, protect existing page addresses and verify the complete customer journey before and after launch.
