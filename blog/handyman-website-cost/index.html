Handyman: what a website costs in 2026

Updated September 30, 2026

Short answer

A handyman website can start at $99 a year with a done-for-you service. Typical custom freelancer or agency work can range from a few thousand dollars to much more, depending on the scope. Prioritize a clear list of accepted jobs, your working area, real project photos, and a practical estimate request before paying for complicated features.

You want a handyman website that brings the right inquiries without becoming another job to maintain. The cost depends on whether you need a simple way to request repairs or a larger system for scheduling and quoting them. Define that job first so you can compare proposals fairly.

Start with the jobs you actually accept

A useful handyman site helps someone decide whether to call about a sticking door, damaged drywall, furniture assembly, or a list of small repairs. It also explains which work you do not take. "We do everything" creates more confusion than confidence.

Write a short scope for the website before asking for prices. Include your main services, the towns you cover, examples of completed work, and the information you need to estimate a job. If you have a minimum visit charge, decide how to explain it.

A straightforward site can group related tasks into repair, installation, and assembly pages or sections. You do not automatically need a separate page for every hinge, handle, and shelf. The handyman website guide can help you decide what a first version needs.

Be accurate about qualifications and service limits. If particular work requires licensing or permits, check with your state or local authority rather than publishing a broad promise you cannot support.

Typical custom freelancer and agency projects range from a few thousand dollars to considerably more, depending on the work. A freelancer may be a good fit for a specific design and content project. Pay attention to the estimate request

A visitor should be able to describe a job without knowing the trade term. "The bathroom door rubs at the top" is more useful than forcing them to choose an uncertain repair category. Ask for the symptom, location, and enough context to decide the next step.

Useful request details can include:

  • The customer's town and preferred contact method.
  • A short description of each job.
  • Whether parts or fixtures have already been purchased.
  • Relevant photos, if your form supports them.
  • Any timing constraint, such as preparing a rental for a new tenant.

Ask your provider whether photo handling is part of the quoted work and how you receive the images. Test it with an ordinary phone photo. A form that technically exists but fails when someone attaches pictures is not ready for customers.

Automatic booking needs more care. Furniture assembly, door adjustments, and drywall repairs may require different time, materials, and follow-up visits. A request that you review personally may fit better than a calendar that confirms work before you understand the scope.

Spend on evidence before decoration

Good project photos show the kind of work you want more of. A neatly fitted door latch, repaired wall, or assembled storage unit can be more relevant than a polished stock photo of a construction crew. Get permission and keep customer addresses out of the images.

Ask who writes the project captions and service descriptions. "Small drywall repair, ready for the next agreed finishing step" tells the customer more than "quality workmanship." Describe the work accurately and make clear whether painting was included.

A visitor may also want to know whether you work in occupied homes, handle several small jobs in one visit, or accept landlord inquiries. Answer those questions with your real operating process. They help customers decide whether you are a fit before calling.

Review the handyman website mistakes before approving a design. Clear services and working contact details should receive budget before decorative animation or an oversized gallery.

Compare ownership and upkeep costs

The initial build price is only part of the decision. Ask about the domain, hosting, ongoing changes, renewals, and what happens if the relationship ends. Request a normal following-year cost as well as the launch cost.

Small service changes matter. You may stop taking ladder work, reduce your travel area, or change how you quote materials. Find out how those updates are handled and whether the provider charges separately for them.

Keep control of the address printed on your truck and business cards. The domain ownership guide explains why registration details deserve attention. Ask what access and content you retain if you move elsewhere.

Before accepting the site, submit a realistic inquiry for a loose cabinet door and several other small tasks. Confirm the message reaches the right place and the acknowledgement explains what happens next. Test the phone button too. You are paying for a usable path from question to conversation.

Frequently asked questions

Should I pay for online quoting?

Only if you can define what the system can safely and accurately quote. Hidden damage, uncertain materials, and unclear scope can make an automatic price misleading. A clear estimate request is often a sensible first step for varied repair work.

Do I need a website page for every town?

Not automatically. Separate pages are useful when you have distinct information about the service in each place. A clear coverage section is better than paying for repeated pages with only the town name changed.

Is photography a separate cost?

It may be, so ask whether the proposal includes taking photos or only preparing images you supply. You can start with clear phone photos of real completed jobs. The important part is that they represent your work accurately and can be published with permission.
