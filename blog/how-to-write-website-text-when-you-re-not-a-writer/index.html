Website text tips if you're not a writer

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Write your website text the way you explain your work to a new customer. Say what you do, where you work, what the customer can expect, and how to contact you. Start with notes from real conversations, organize them by page, and replace broad claims with details you can honestly support.

How do you write website text when writing is not your job? Start with the answers you give customers every week, then put them in an order a new visitor can follow. You do not need a clever slogan before you can explain what your business does.

Collect answers before you write paragraphs

Open a blank document and answer a few questions in rough notes. Use the words you would use if a neighbor asked about hiring you. You can speak the answers into your phone and tidy them later if that feels easier.

Start here:

  • What work do you want people to hire you for?
  • Where do you provide it?
  • What does a customer usually ask before booking?
  • What is included, and what needs a separate quote?
  • What happens after someone calls or sends a request?
  • What should someone know before the appointment?

Use your own knowledge, not private customer messages pasted onto a public page. You can learn from repeated questions without publishing names, addresses, or the details of someone's home.

Keep facts separate from ideas you still need to check. If you are unsure about a warranty, service area, or response time, leave yourself a note to verify it. A polished sentence does not make an uncertain promise safe to publish.

Give each page one clear job

Your homepage should help a visitor understand the business and choose where to go next. A service page should explain one kind of work in enough detail to support a decision. Your contact page should make the next step straightforward.

For the homepage, draft a plain opening sentence using your main service and area. An example for a fictional cleaning business might be: "We clean homes and small offices in Cedar Falls, with recurring visits and one-time move-out appointments." Use that structure only with services you actually offer.

Follow it with a short explanation of who the service suits, a few service links, and contact instructions. Avoid trying to fit every job you have ever done into the first paragraph. The visitor should not need to sort through commercial work to find out whether you clean apartments.

Save your business story for a focused section or an about page. The guide to writing an about page for a local business helps you choose details that matter to customers.

Replace praise with details people can use

Words like "quality," "reliable," and "professional" are easy to write because they sound positive. They do not explain much on their own. Look for a concrete fact that shows what you mean.

For example, instead of "We offer quality painting services," a painter could explain: "Your estimate lists the rooms, surfaces, preparation, and coats included in the job." That tells the homeowner something they can check. Only use it if your estimates actually include those details.

Instead of "We make auto detailing convenient," a mobile detailer could explain whether they visit homes or workplaces and what access they need. State whether the customer must provide water or power. Those facts help someone decide whether the service will work at their apartment or office.

A tree service can explain how it quotes debris removal and whether stump grinding is separate. A salon can say how new color clients arrange a consultation. Specific text sounds more believable because it answers the question behind the customer's hesitation.

Build service pages from the booking conversation

Think about the conversation between "Do you do this?" and "Let's book it." Your service page should cover the useful parts in roughly that order.

  1. Name the service in everyday words.
  2. Describe the customer situation it addresses.
  3. Explain the main inclusions and limits.
  4. State where you provide it.
  5. Explain pricing or how the estimate works.
  6. Tell the customer what to do next.

For garage door opener repair, customers may want to know whether you assess an existing opener or only install replacements. They may also need to know what information to have ready when they call. Explain your process without pretending you can diagnose their problem from a short description.

If pricing varies, say why. "We quote after checking the wall condition and measuring the rooms" is more helpful than "Contact us for competitive rates." Use the pricing guide to choose between fixed amounts, starting prices, and an estimate process.

Write the next step as an instruction

A visitor should know what clicking a button or sending a message will do. "Request a cleaning estimate" is clearer than "Start your journey." "Call to check appointment availability" tells someone what kind of conversation to expect.

Explain what information you need without asking for everything at once. A landscaper might need the town, the type of work, and a short description to begin. Measurements, access details, and photos can follow if the job needs them.

Distinguish an inquiry from a confirmed booking. If submitting a form only requests a callback, say so beside the form. Do not write "You're booked" unless your process actually reserves an appointment.

Include a response expectation you can keep. If you return messages after the day's jobs, write that plainly instead of implying someone monitors requests continuously. The guide to writing a useful service page shows how the explanation and contact step work together.

Edit by reading aloud and checking facts

Read the page as if you were explaining it across a kitchen table. Shorten anything you would struggle to say. Replace "residential exterior surface cleaning solutions" with the specific work, such as house washing, driveway cleaning, or patio cleaning.

Check one thing at a time. First, confirm the services and locations. Then check prices, hours, and promises. Finally, fix awkward sentences and repeated words. Trying to perfect everything at once makes a simple page feel like a large project.

Ask someone outside the business to read it and tell you what you do, where you work, and how to hire you. If they miss one of those points, make that information easier to find. You do not need to defend the wording; you need it to work.

Use service names customers recognize, but do not force the same phrase into every heading. Local keyword guidance can help you match familiar wording to the right page. Keep the finished text in a document you can update when the business changes.

Frequently asked questions

Should I say "I" or "we" on my website?

Use whichever accurately describes the business. A solo owner can use "I" without sounding less capable. Do not imply you have a large team if customers will work directly with you.

How much text does my homepage need?

Write enough to explain your services, area, useful proof, and next step. There is no single word count that suits every homepage. Move detailed service explanations onto their own pages when they interrupt that simple introduction.

Can I copy another company's wording and change it?

Use your own explanation of your own work. Another company's wording may include promises, services, or policies that are wrong for you. You can notice which customer questions a page answers without borrowing its sentences.

The short version

Start with the answers you already give customers. Organize them around the service, the scope, and the next step, then read everything aloud and verify the facts. Useful details will do more work than polished claims.
