Handyman: what your website needs

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A handyman website needs a specific job list, your service area, real project photos, and a straightforward estimate request. Explain minimum charges, materials, and how you assess the work. Help customers describe the repair and send useful pictures, while clearly stating which jobs require a different contractor or specialist.

A homeowner visiting your handyman website wants to know whether their small repair is a job you will take. They may have a sticking interior door, a damaged patch of drywall, or several shelves still sitting in boxes. Show those jobs clearly, then explain how to get them onto your schedule.

Lead with the jobs you actually want

"No job too small" can sound welcoming, but it leaves important questions unanswered. Will you visit for one curtain rod? Can someone combine loose cabinet handles, a door adjustment, and furniture assembly in one appointment? Your opening should help the customer recognize a suitable job.

Use a headline such as "Small home repairs and assembly in Fort Collins," replacing the location and services with your own. Add a short list of common work underneath. Drywall patches, interior door adjustments, shelving, and flat-pack furniture assembly tell customers more than a long paragraph about quality.

Put a phone number and an estimate request beside that explanation. If you cannot answer while using tools, explain the reply process honestly. Do not promise immediate callbacks unless you have someone who can deliver them.

State your service towns early. A homeowner should not complete a detailed repair form before discovering that their address is outside your route. Mention any visit minimum near the first estimate action so a small-job customer understands the basic arrangement.

Organize services around a homeowner's list

Group related tasks rather than presenting a wall of loosely connected keywords. Useful groups might include doors and hardware, wall repairs, mounting and shelving, assembly, and small maintenance visits. Only include categories that match your skills, equipment, and permitted work.

Under each group, name examples and boundaries. For drywall, explain whether you patch small holes, match texture, prime, and paint. A customer may assume a finished repair includes painting the entire wall unless you explain the scope.

For mounting, ask about the object, wall material, and location. For assembly, say whether customers should provide the product information before you quote. A heavy wardrobe and a small bedside table need different planning even when both arrive flat-packed.

Be explicit about work you do not perform. Avoid saying you handle all electrical, plumbing, or structural repairs unless that is accurate and appropriate for your qualifications. Check with your state and local authorities about the work you may offer. The service list should help someone choose the right provider, not create expectations you must reverse later.

Explain estimates, materials, and repeat visits

Small repair work becomes frustrating when the price basis is unclear. Tell customers whether you use fixed quotes, hourly work, a minimum visit charge, or a combination. You do not have to publish one price for every possible job, but you should explain how the estimate is formed.

For example, you might say that clear photos can support an initial estimate, while concealed damage or uncertain wall construction requires a closer look. State how you discuss additional work before proceeding. Avoid presenting a photo estimate as a guarantee when important conditions are unknown.

Explain who buys materials. Customers need to know whether you supply common fixings, collect agreed items, or install products they purchase. If shopping time or collection affects the charge, describe your actual policy.

Some repairs also need more than one visit because of drying time, missing parts, or preparation. Set that expectation when it applies rather than promising everything will be completed in one trip. Prove the finish, not just the toolbox

Use photos of completed work that resemble the jobs you want. A neat drywall patch, an aligned cabinet door, or properly finished trim can be more persuasive than a stock picture of someone holding a drill. Show enough context for visitors to understand the task.

Add a brief caption explaining the scope. "Small wall repair, texture touch-up, and paint to the agreed area" sets a different expectation from "Wall repaired." If the customer handled painting afterward, make that clear rather than implying you completed the entire finish.

Include an honest introduction to the person or team who arrives. Homeowners are inviting someone into their space, so a recognizable photo and clear contact details matter. State credentials and insurance only when they are current and apply to the work described.

Reviews can support those details when they reflect real jobs. Do not invent testimonials for a new service. Our guide to trustworthy local business websites offers practical ways to show evidence even when your gallery is still small.

Ask for a job description you can use

A useful estimate form helps the customer explain the repair without needing trade knowledge. Ask what they want fixed, where the property is, and how you should reply. Let them add several tasks when combining jobs is part of your service.

Photo instructions should be specific:

  1. Take one wider picture showing the work area.
  2. Take one close picture showing the problem.
  3. Add product information for anything you will assemble or install.
  4. Mention access limits, such as stairs or occupied work areas.

Make uploads optional or provide another contact route. Someone with a broken cabinet hinge should still be able to ask for help if the upload fails. Do not ask customers to climb, open equipment, or disturb a damaged area just to produce a better photograph.

Frequently asked questions

Should I list every repair I have ever done?

No, concentrate on work you currently accept and can explain clearly. A long list can attract unsuitable requests and bury the services that make up your normal schedule.

Is it better to ask for calls or photos?

Offer both when you can manage them. Photos help define visible repairs, while a conversation is useful when a customer cannot describe the problem or needs to discuss several tasks.

Do I need a page for each small task?

Usually you can group related tasks into useful service pages. Create a separate page when the work needs a distinct explanation, such as assembly preparation or a multi-step wall repair.
