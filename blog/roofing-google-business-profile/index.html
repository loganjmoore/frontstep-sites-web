Roofing: Google Business Profile guide

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A roofer's Google Business Profile should tell homeowners which roof types and jobs you handle, where you work, and how to request an inspection. Keep business details and hours accurate, use genuine project photos, and explain repairs separately from replacements. Review the profile after storms or scheduling changes so public information matches what your office can offer.

What should your roofing Google Business Profile say to someone deciding whether to call? Make the roof types, service area, and inspection process clear enough for a homeowner to recognize a fit. A ceiling stain after rain and a planned roof replacement are different situations, and your profile should not make either customer guess what you do.

Get the business identity and ownership right

Search for your business before creating a new profile. If a listing already exists, work on gaining access to that record. An old phone number or a former marketing provider is a reason to clean up access, not automatically start a duplicate.

Use an account arrangement the business controls. Keep track of who manages the profile, who receives verification requests, and who can make urgent corrections. An outside helper should not be the only person able to reach your company's information.

Represent your real business name, location, and service model accurately. Do not create pretend offices in towns where you want roofing jobs, or present a home base as a walk-in showroom. Follow Google's business representation guidelines.

Check the phone and website destination from the public profile. A homeowner should arrive at the right company, with the same basic identity and a clear inspection request option. Fix old branding or redirects that make that connection confusing.

Describe roof types and job scope clearly

Choose the closest available category for the roofing business you actually operate. Review additional categories only where they accurately reflect your work. Use the service information and description to explain the details homeowners need.

Start by separating repair work from replacement work. If you take both, say so. If you only take full replacements, make that clear before someone calls about a few damaged shingles.

Depending on your scope, useful service descriptions could cover:

  • Asphalt shingle roof repairs and replacements.
  • Metal roofing installation or repair.
  • Flat or low-slope roof work.
  • Leak investigation and flashing repairs.
  • Roof inspections and replacement estimates.
  • Storm damage assessments.

Do not list every roof material simply because it appears in a competitor's profile. A homeowner with a tile roof needs an accurate answer about your experience. The same applies to commercial roofing, gutters, skylights, and other related work.

Give the fuller explanation on your website. The roofer website essentials guide helps connect a brief profile description to pages that answer inspection and project questions.

Make coverage and availability believable

Name the towns or counties where you regularly accept work. Explain meaningful boundaries on the linked website, particularly if your repair coverage is narrower than your replacement area. Do not leave callers to interpret a vague regional description.

Use accurate customer-facing hours and keep holiday information current. If someone can leave a message at any time but your office responds during the working day, explain that arrangement. Taking a message is different from promising an immediate roof inspection.

After a storm, review the words you are using. "Call to discuss inspection availability" gives an honest next step. A permanent promise of instant storm response can create expectations your crews cannot meet when demand changes.

Avoid promising an insurance outcome. You can describe your inspection and documentation process without saying a homeowner's insurer will approve or pay for the job. Keep the profile focused on the roofing service you control.

Choose project photos with a clear purpose

A finished roof photo should help someone understand your work, not just fill space. Select real projects that show the materials and jobs you want to book. Where you add descriptions, identify whether the work was a repair, a replacement, or another service.

For example, a shingle replacement overview and a detail of completed flashing tell different parts of a project story. Use images your team already obtained appropriately during the job. Do not ask homeowners to climb onto roofs to create marketing material.

Get permission before publishing property photos. Avoid visible addresses, license plates, and other identifying details. A general town reference can provide context without identifying the house.

Keep claims modest and factual. A picture can show the appearance of finished work, but it cannot establish that a roof will never leak or prove a precise remaining lifespan. Avoid captions promising an outcome you cannot support.

Add current team or vehicle images where useful. A homeowner arranging an inspection may appreciate recognizing the company arriving at the property. Remove outdated images you control when they no longer represent the business.

Use updates and questions to explain the next step

Profile posts can share a timely business update or direct readers to useful service information. Google's profile posts guide explains the available publishing feature. Keep the message tied to something a customer needs to know.

A roofer might post about scheduling replacement estimates, share a permitted project example, or clarify office availability after severe weather. Date-sensitive messages need a review point. An old storm announcement should not keep implying that a temporary schedule is still active.

Collect recurring questions from the office. Homeowners may ask whether you repair metal roofs, inspect detached garages, or need access inside the property. Publish accurate answers on your website and respond to public questions if that feature is available on your profile.

Do not depend on a particular question-and-answer layout remaining unchanged. Your own service pages should hold the durable answers. The roofer homepage copy guide helps you introduce the inspection process in plain language.

Keep reviews and information under regular review

Assign someone to check the profile after changes to staff, phones, hours, or service scope. Include a public check of the website link and call option. The editing screen is not the whole customer experience.

Invite honest reviews consistently after real work, without rewards or pressure. Reply to concerns about cleanup, scheduling, or communication through a professional service process. Do not publish a customer's private project or claim details in a public response.

Keep a simple list of corrections made and recurring questions received. If several callers ask whether you do repairs, your service description may be too focused on replacements. Update the wording to reflect your actual scope instead of assuming the callers failed to read.

Use the roofing review guide to build a routine the office can maintain. The profile should stay useful between busy seasons, not just receive attention when new leads are needed.

Frequently asked questions

Should I create a profile for every town I serve?

Do not invent locations to appear closer to customers. Represent your actual business and its genuine service area. If you open a real additional location, check Google's eligibility requirements before setting it up.

Should storm damage be the main service description?

Only if that accurately represents your business. Keep planned replacements and ordinary repairs visible when you offer them. A homeowner without storm damage should still know whether to call.

Can I use manufacturer roof photos?

Only use images you have permission to publish and make their purpose clear. Do not present them as your own completed projects. Your genuine work is more useful when showing what your team has done.

How often should I update the profile?

Check it routinely and whenever business information changes. Review temporary availability messages after storms and busy periods. Accuracy matters more than publishing something on a fixed daily schedule.

The short version

Use your roofing profile to explain the jobs you take, where you work, and how inspections begin. Support that information with real projects and honest availability. Keep the public details aligned with what your office tells callers.
