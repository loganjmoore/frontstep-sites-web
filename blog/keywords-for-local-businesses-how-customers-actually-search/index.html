Keywords for local businesses

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

Local business keywords are the words customers use to find a service, describe a problem, or check whether a business serves their area. Start with your actual services, the towns you cover, and questions from real inquiries. Use those words in helpful pages instead of repeating a phrase or building a page for every variation.

What should you put on your website when customers search differently from the way you describe your work? Start with the language you hear on calls and estimates. Good local keywords connect a customer's need with a service you actually provide, in a place you actually work.

Sort searches by what the customer needs

A keyword can be a whole question, not just a single word. Someone who knows the service may search for "water heater replacement in Cedar Rapids." Someone with a problem may search for "water heater leaking from bottom." Those searches can lead to different conversations.

Use these groups to organize your ideas:

  • Service and location: "interior painter in Madison" or "dog grooming in Tempe."
  • Nearby service: "garage door repair near me" or "barber near me."
  • Problem: "AC running but house not cooling" or "garage door won't close."
  • Project detail: "move-out cleaning with oven cleaning" or "electrician for EV charger installation."
  • Decision question: "does stump grinding include root removal" or "how long does exterior painting take."

These are example phrases, not measured search rankings or a claim about how often people use them. Keep the ones that fit your work. A tree service that does not offer stump grinding should not build a page suggesting that it does.

Find useful words without buying a tool

Review recent inquiries, estimate notes, and customer questions. Record the language without personal details. Pay attention to the difference between your invoice wording and the words someone uses before they know what service they need.

An auto repair customer might say, "The car squeals when I brake," rather than naming a component. That phrase can inform a brake inspection page. It should not become a diagnosis based on a noise alone. Explain that an inspection identifies the cause and the work required.

If your website is connected to Google Search Console, review the queries and pages in its search performance reports. Google explains what that information shows in its Performance report guide. Use it to spot relevant searches your site already appears for, not as a complete list of everything customers search.

Combine those sources into a short working list. The guide to setting up Search Console can help if you do not have access yet. You can still start with customer conversations today.

Match each group to the right page

Give each important service a page when there is enough useful detail to explain it. Then place related questions on that page instead of creating separate pages for every wording change.

For a garage door company, broken spring replacement and opener repair involve different customer needs. Each may justify a service page. "Garage door spring repair," "broken garage spring," and "replace garage door spring" usually do not need three nearly identical pages.

Write down the main purpose of each page before drafting. A useful note might be: "This page helps homeowners understand our opener repair service, the area we cover, and how to arrange an assessment." Anything that does not help that purpose can go elsewhere.

A well-structured service page answers the practical questions behind a keyword: what you do, what you do not do, how the visit works, and what happens next. A page full of phrases without those answers leaves the customer doing the work.

Add location details that mean something

Name the towns and neighborhoods you actually serve. Explain meaningful boundaries, such as whether you travel outside your usual area for larger projects or restrict recurring lawn routes to certain neighborhoods. A clear boundary can prevent inquiries you cannot use.

You do not need to write "near me" into every paragraph. Tell people where the business operates and provide clear location information. A heading such as "Drain cleaning in Cedar Rapids" is easier to understand than a sentence that repeatedly calls your business "the drain cleaner near me."

Separate town pages can make sense when the content is genuinely different. You might describe a real service route, completed projects used with permission, or specific booking arrangements. Replacing one town name in otherwise identical text does not give readers much help.

Before expanding, read when service area pages help. Start with one accurate coverage page if you have little distinct information for individual towns. Never imply that you have an office where you only travel for jobs.

Put the words where they clarify the page

Use the main service and location in the page title and opening when they fit naturally. Then explain the work in ordinary language. Your headings should help someone skim, such as "What our move-out cleaning includes" and "What we need before quoting."

Google's Search Essentials recommends using words people would search for in prominent, descriptive places. That does not call for a fixed number of repetitions. Once the subject is clear, spend your effort answering the customer's questions.

Keep related terms only when they describe real differences. A painter can explain walls, ceilings, trim, preparation, and cleanup because those are parts of the job. Adding a string of unrelated towns or services at the bottom of the page does not improve that explanation.

Google identifies unnatural repetition and blocks of location names used to manipulate rankings as examples of keyword stuffing in its spam policies. Read your draft aloud. If a sentence sounds like something nobody would say to a customer, rewrite it.

Review results against the jobs you want

After publishing, watch the questions that come through calls and forms. Ask customers how they found you, and note which service they wanted. Search visibility is useful only if it connects with work you can actually take.

If a cleaner receives repeated inquiries about services outside the package, improve the scope wording. If an electrician gets calls for appliance repair they do not offer, check whether the page creates that impression. The next fix may be greater clarity, not more keywords.

Review your working list when your services or coverage change. Remove unsupported claims and add questions that keep recurring. For a simple way to connect website activity with actual inquiries, see how to tell if your website is getting customers.

Frequently asked questions

How many keywords should each page target?

Start with one clear service or customer need, then cover the related questions naturally. There is no useful universal number of phrases to squeeze into a page. The page should feel complete to a customer who needs that service.

Should I use the exact words "near me"?

You do not need to force that wording into your sentences. Describe your services and actual coverage clearly instead. Use town and neighborhood names when they help someone understand whether you can serve them.

Do I need paid keyword software?

You can start without it by reviewing inquiries and your own search data. A tool may help with deeper research, but it cannot tell you which jobs suit your crew or whether your page describes them honestly. Get those decisions right first.

The short version

Choose keywords from real services, real locations, and real customer questions. Give each important need a useful page, then write naturally and review the quality of the inquiries you receive.
