Handyman website mistakes that cost calls

Updated September 26, 2026

Short answer

A handyman website should show the repairs you accept, where you work, how estimates happen, and how to contact you. Vague service claims, missing visit terms, misleading project photos, and long forms make that harder. Start by helping a homeowner describe an ordinary small job without needing to understand construction terminology.

A homeowner has a loose towel rail, a sticking door, and a flat-pack cabinet still in its box. They search for a local handyman and land on your site. If they cannot tell whether you take that kind of work, they have to guess or move on.

Saying you do everything hides your useful services

"No job too big or small" sounds welcoming, but it does not help someone choose the right person. You probably have practical limits on scope, travel, height, materials, or specialist work. Explain them.

List real jobs in ordinary language. Door adjustments, cabinet hardware, furniture assembly, and small drywall repairs tell a homeowner more than "complete property solutions." Group related tasks so the page stays readable.

State important exclusions where they help someone decide. If you do not undertake roof work, major renovations, or regulated electrical work, do not imply otherwise with broad wording or stock photos. Check with your state or local authority if you are unsure about licensing requirements for a service.

Your handyman homepage should let someone recognize a familiar problem quickly. Save the full business history for a later section instead of making it the only information above the service list.

Customers cannot work out how estimates happen

A bare "call for pricing" leaves basic questions unanswered. Is there a minimum visit? Can several jobs be combined? Do you charge for an initial visit, and are materials handled separately? Describe your own process without promising a fixed price for unknown work.

You can explain what helps you assess a job before traveling. Ask for a short description, the town, and relevant photos. Tell people when you may still need to inspect the work in person.

A good explanation might say: "Send a photo of the damaged area and describe what has changed. We will review the job and explain the next step before arranging work." Adapt that example to your actual process.

Make any published price conditions easy to find. A starting figure without its scope can create an argument at the door. The website pricing guide covers ways to explain prices when every job is not identical.

The service area sounds larger than your route

If you regularly work on small repairs, travel can decide whether a visit makes sense. A site claiming an entire region may bring inquiries you cannot serve efficiently. Name the places you actually cover and explain how you assess work outside them.

A customer should not have to submit their full street address just to find out whether you visit their town. Ask for an approximate location first when that is enough. Save detailed access arrangements for the appropriate private conversation.

Explain any difference between normal repair visits and larger planned jobs. You might consider a longer trip for a full day of agreed work, but that should be your real policy rather than a promise made by the website writer.

Keep your coverage consistent across the homepage, service pages, and contact form. If you have moved or reduced your area, remove old place names so customers do not receive conflicting answers.

Project photos imply work you did not do

A stock image of a complete kitchen renovation can misrepresent a business focused on cabinet adjustments and small installations. Show jobs at the scale you accept. Customers looking for smaller repairs need evidence that you welcome them.

Use a short caption to explain each project. Say what you repaired, assembled, or installed and what was outside the scope. Do not take credit for an entire room because you fitted the shelves.

Before-and-after images can help if they show a comparable view and honest result. Avoid photos of unfinished work unless you explain the stage. Ask permission before publishing a customer's home, and check for identifying details in mirrors, documents, and windows.

You do not need a huge portfolio. A few clear examples across your main services can help someone understand the work. Keep adding relevant photos as you complete jobs, rather than filling empty space with unrelated building images.

Contact forms require a construction vocabulary

A homeowner may know only that a cupboard door hangs crooked or a latch no longer catches. Let them describe that. A form requiring a precise repair type, wall construction, and material list can ask too much before you have spoken.

Start with name, contact details, town, and a job description. If photos are useful, explain what to capture and allow another way to contact you. Customers should not get stuck because they cannot upload a picture.

The contact form guide can help you remove unnecessary questions. Do not ask for sensitive access details such as lockbox codes in a general inquiry form. Handle those later through your agreed process.

Make the phone number easy to use from every service page. If you cannot answer while working, state how people can leave a message and what they should include. Avoid advertising instant replies unless that is how your business operates.

Test the site with an ordinary repair request

Open your site on a phone and act like someone who has never hired you. Look for a small repair, check the service area, and try to request an estimate. A friend unfamiliar with the site can help identify confusing wording.

Submit a test request and confirm where it arrives. Read the confirmation message and check whether it promises a quote, a call, or a booking. Those are different steps, so the wording needs to match what you do.

Check the images on a slower connection and make sure text is readable without zooming. Remove anything that gets between the customer and the service information. Fixing that path is more useful than changing colors while the form remains broken.

Frequently asked questions

Should I list work I might offer later?

List services customers can request now. You can add new work when you are equipped and ready to accept it. Advertising future services as available creates avoidable disappointment.

Will showing a minimum visit put people off?

It can help people decide whether the job suits your service before they call. Explain what the minimum means and whether combining tasks is possible. Clear expectations are more useful than surprising someone after you arrive.

Should I let customers book a full day online?

Only if your process confirms that the work fits the time and scope before making a promise. A list of repairs may contain something that needs different equipment or a separate specialist. An inquiry-first approach can give you room to assess that.
